## The short version: a big new bet from a cheap insurer

Fairfax Financial (TSX: FFH), Prem Watsa's Toronto-based insurance and investment holding company, agreed on October 7, 2026 to buy **half of Boots**, Britain's best-known pharmacy and beauty chain, alongside the Weston family. The stock barely reacted: it closed at **C$2,199.92**, down about **0.55%** on a day the S&P/TSX Composite fell **1.7%**. It capped a soft year: on the TSX screener (October 7 close), the shares are down about **10.7%** over twelve months, while Canada's big life insurers rose about 27% to 52%.

At about **7.8x trailing earnings**, Fairfax is the cheapest of Canada's large insurers on the TSX screener, and also the cheapest on price to sales and price to book. The question for a new buyer is whether Boots is a smart use of that capital or a distraction from the core insurance business. Below we go through the deal terms and who runs Boots, the balance sheet behind the cheque, and how Fairfax compares with its closest TSX peers — Intact, Definity, Power Corp, Great-West Lifeco, Manulife and Sun Life — on size, returns, valuation, dividends and analyst targets.

## What happened: the Boots deal with Wittington

On **October 7, 2026**, Fairfax said it had agreed to partner with **Wittington Investments**, the private holding company of the Weston family, to acquire **The Boots Group** from Sycamore Partners. The deal brings:

- A total purchase price of about **US$8.9 billion, including assumed debt** (about C$12.7 billion, as reported by The Canadian Press).
- An equity commitment from Fairfax of **up to about US$2.3 billion**, for **50% of Boots' equity** after closing.
- **Operational control for Wittington**, with **Galen Weston** becoming Boots' chair. Wittington controls George Weston Limited and, through it, Loblaw, which owns **Shoppers Drug Mart**, so the operating partner already runs Canada's largest pharmacy chain.
- Boots' retail business in the **UK and Ireland**, plus (per The Canadian Press) its Thailand operations, franchised businesses, optical division and the **No7 Beauty Company**. Boots' businesses in Germany and Mexico stay with the current owners.
- Closing is expected in the **first quarter of 2027**, subject to customary conditions and regulatory approval.

For Fairfax, this is a classic Watsa-style move: a large, long-term stake in a well-known business, with a trusted partner running it. It is also a **cheque, not a takeover**: Fairfax does not take on the job of fixing Boots' stores.

### The balance sheet behind the cheque

The size matters relative to Fairfax's resources. At **June 30, 2026**, the holding company held **US$2.3 billion of cash and marketable securities**, plus another US$2.3 billion (at fair value) in investments in associates and non-insurance companies, and its **US$2.0 billion revolving credit facility was undrawn**. Fairfax reports in US dollars.

The second-quarter print, reported **July 30, 2026**, was steady rather than spectacular:

- **Net earnings of US$1.39 billion** (US$63.38 per diluted share), versus US$1.44 billion a year earlier.
- **Book value per basic share of US$1,304.39**, up **4.8%** in the first half after adding back the US$15 dividend.
- An undiscounted **combined ratio of 93.1%** and **US$458.6 million of underwriting profit**, with gross premiums written up 4.1%.
- **1,055,190 shares bought back** in the first half for about **US$1.72 billion**, cutting the share count by 4.2% since December 31.

The weak spots are the ones every property and casualty insurer faces right now: pricing is softening in some North American and specialty lines, and quarterly earnings swing with investment gains and losses. Total debt to capital (excluding non-insurance companies) also rose to 28.0% from 26.2% at year-end.

## Business overview: Fairfax vs Canadian insurance peers

Fairfax is a holding company whose main business is **property and casualty insurance and reinsurance**, through companies such as Northbridge, Crum & Forster, Zenith, Allied World, Odyssey Group and Brit, plus a growing international arm. Insurance premiums are invested before claims are paid, and Fairfax runs that investment portfolio itself, including large stakes in businesses like Eurobank and, soon, Boots.

On the TSX screener, Fairfax sits in the **Finance** sector. Banks were left out as not comparable. **Intact (IFC)** is the closest peer, as Canada's largest property and casualty insurer, and **Definity (DFY)** is the smaller domestic P&C name. **Power Corp (POW)**, **Great-West Lifeco (GWO)**, **Manulife (MFC)** and **Sun Life (SLF)** are the large life insurers and insurance holding companies that complete the group of Canada's big listed insurers.

Put simply, the market values Fairfax well below the size of its earnings.

Fairfax is the cheapest name here on **trailing P/E at 7.79**, against a group average of about **16.1**, and on **forward P/E at 8.31**. The TSX screener rates Fairfax a **Buy**. The MarketBeat consensus target of **C$2,751.57** from seven analysts sits about **25%** above the October 7 close of **C$2,199.92**. **National Bank** has the street-high target of **C$3,450** with an Outperform rating (raised from C$3,300 on July 27), while **CIBC** started coverage at **Neutral with C$2,350** on June 26. ## Why it matters: from cheap insurer to insurer plus Boots

The old Fairfax story was simple: **a cheap insurer that compounds book value with smart investments**, and returns capital mainly through buybacks. Boots adds a large, cash-generating retail business, run by an owner that knows pharmacy retail, without Fairfax taking on the operating risk.

For investors, the question is **whether Watsa's next big bet earns more than buying back Fairfax's own discounted shares**. If Boots compounds at a higher rate than the roughly 13% earnings yield implied by a 7.8x P/E, it adds value; if not, the buyback would have been the better use of the money.

## Bull case

1. **Low expectations.** At a single-digit earnings multiple, Fairfax does not need heroics for the shares to work, and it has a long record of growing book value per share.
2. **A modest cheque.** The up-to-US$2.3B commitment is under 7% of Fairfax's market value, and the Westons, who already run Shoppers Drug Mart, will run the stores.
3. **A shrinking share count.** Fairfax cut its share count by about 4% in the first half alone, so every dollar of earnings goes further.

## Bear case and risks

1. **The conglomerate discount.** Fairfax is a complex holding company, and complex holding companies can trade at a discount for a long time. Adding a UK retailer could widen it.
2. **A UK retail turnaround.** Boots faces lower foot traffic, a history of store closures and strong beauty rivals such as Sephora. It needs investment to modernize, so returns are not guaranteed.
3. **Softening insurance pricing.** Property and casualty pricing is easing in parts of North America, and Fairfax's earnings swing with investment gains and losses.

## What to watch

1. **Analyst target updates.** All seven targets predate the Boots deal; watch whether the C$2,751.57 consensus moves.
2. **Fairfax's third-quarter results.** Book value per share, the combined ratio and the pace of buybacks will show how much capital is left after Boots.
3. **Regulatory approvals and closing.** The deal is expected to close in the first quarter of 2027.
4. **Underwriting results.** Watch whether the combined ratio holds in the low 90s as insurance pricing softens.

## Bottom line

Fairfax's Boots deal is a characteristic Prem Watsa move: a large, long-term stake in a famous business, alongside a partner with deep pharmacy experience. The market shrugged, and the stock still screens as the cheapest of Canada's big insurers, at about 7.8x trailing earnings and 1.2x book, after a weak year that followed a huge five-year run. The real test is capital allocation: whether Boots earns more than Fairfax's own buybacks. Watch analyst revisions, third-quarter book value and the 2027 closing.
