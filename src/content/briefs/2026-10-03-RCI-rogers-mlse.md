## The short version: all of MLSE, in a soft telecom tape

Rogers Communications (TSX: RCI.A), Canada's largest wireless carrier and a major cable and media company, closed at **CA$44.99** (Class A) on October 2, 2026, down about **13.5%** over the last month and about **9%** over twelve months. On **October 1**, it completed a **C$4.35B** purchase of the remaining **25%** of Maple Leaf Sports & Entertainment, giving it **100% of MLSE**: the Leafs, the Raptors, Toronto FC, the Argonauts and Scotiabank Arena. The new story is **Rogers Sports**, one unit that combines MLSE with the Blue Jays and Sportsnet.

On the screener, Rogers trades at only about **4x trailing earnings**, but that figure is distorted; on forward earnings it is about **9.5x**. The question for a new buyer is whether a C$4.35B sports cheque earns its keep for a telecom with a leverage question. Below we go through the close, how Rogers got here, and how it compares with Bell, TELUS and Quebecor on size, returns, valuation, dividends and analyst targets.

## What happened: the C$4.35B MLSE close

On **October 1, 2026**, Rogers completed the purchase of the remaining **25%** of MLSE from **Kilmer Sports Inc.** for **C$4.35B**, bringing its ownership to **100%**. What comes with it, and what changes:

- **MLSE** owns the Toronto Maple Leafs (NHL), Toronto Raptors (NBA), Toronto FC (MLS), Toronto Argonauts (CFL), development teams, Scotiabank Arena and a Live Nation partnership.
- Rogers will create **Rogers Sports**, combining MLSE with the **Toronto Blue Jays**, **Rogers Centre**, **Sportsnet**, more than **30 television networks** and more than **40 radio stations**.
- **Keith Pelley** stays President and CEO of MLSE and takes on additional Rogers Media accountability; **Mark Shapiro** stays President and CEO of the Blue Jays.
- The operating model for Rogers Sports is still being built; in the interim it is business as usual.

The close is **a strategic step, not an earnings event**. Sports franchises produce media rights, ticket and sponsorship revenue, but their value lies mostly in content for Sportsnet and in long-term asset appreciation. The cost is real: C$4.35B in cash adds to a balance sheet that was already stretched.

### The path from the Bell stake to 100%

The October 1 close is the last step in a two-stage buyout. In **2025**, Rogers bought **Bell's former 37.5% MLSE stake** for about **C$4.7B**, which made it the majority owner. It then followed the path to exercise its option on **Kilmer's 25%** in July, and league approvals cleared for the October close.

Put together, Rogers has spent roughly **C$9B** over about a year to move from part-owner to sole owner of Toronto's main sports franchises. That is a large bet on content for a company still digesting its 2023 purchase of Shaw. It also explains why investors focus on leverage and free cash flow rather than on trailing earnings.

## Business overview: Rogers vs communications peers

Rogers earns most of its money from **wireless** and **cable** (internet, TV and home phone), with a smaller **media** division that includes Sportsnet, the Blue Jays and now all of MLSE. Wireless and internet subscriptions are the steady, recurring core; media and sports are more volatile and capital-hungry, but they feed the content side of the bundle.

On the TSX screener, Rogers sits in the **Communications** sector. The peers are the other liquid national and regional carriers: **Bell (BCE)**, the largest telecom by revenue and Rogers' main rival in wireless, internet and media; **TELUS (T)**, the third national carrier, with health and agriculture technology units; and **Quebecor (QBR.A)**, the owner of Vidéotron and Freedom Mobile. Smaller names in the sector were left out as microcaps. The table uses the Class A shares (RCI.A) because the more liquid Class B shares were not in this screener export.

## Why it matters: from leveraged consolidator to sports-and-content owner

The old narrative was Rogers as **a leveraged wireless and cable consolidator** still digesting Shaw and its first MLSE stake. The new narrative is **full ownership of Toronto's sports complex** under one Rogers Sports banner: content, tickets and wireless cross-selling in one house.

Income investors still get a mid-4% yield. Growth investors and sports fans care whether the C$4.35B cheque earns its keep. The open question is whether Rogers eventually brings in outside capital for the sports assets, or keeps them on its own balance sheet.

## Bull case

1. **One roof for Toronto sports.** 100% of MLSE plus the Blue Jays and Sportsnet sit under Rogers Sports after a clean October 1 close.
2. **Street support.** Consensus near C$59 with a Buy bias holds even after National Bank's trim.
3. **Room to wait.** A forward multiple near 9.5x, a 4.45% yield and a low payout leave room if free cash flow holds.

## Bear case and risks

1. **More leverage.** C$4.35B on top of the earlier C$4.7B Bell stake raises leverage and execution risk in sports and media.
2. **No re-rating yet.** The stock is down about 13.5% over a month alongside Bell and TELUS; closing MLSE did not lift it.
3. **Distorted earnings.** Trailing ~4x earnings is not a free lunch; trailing net income is inflated, so real earnings power sits closer to the forward figure.

## What to watch

1. **Rogers Sports structure.** How the unit is organized and whether Pelley's media remit shows up in next-quarter commentary.
2. **Q3 results.** Wireless and cable trends, plus any disclosure of the sports contribution.
3. **Leverage and free cash flow** after the C$4.35B payment.
4. **Analyst targets.** Whether the cluster near C$59 holds.

## Bottom line

Rogers now owns all of MLSE, and with the Blue Jays and Sportsnet it controls one of the largest sports and content packages in Canada. On forward earnings it is the cheapest of the big Canadian carriers, with a lower payout than Bell or TELUS and about 32% upside to consensus. The issue is cost: roughly C$9B spent on MLSE in about a year, on top of the Shaw debt, while the telecom sector is out of favour. The next test is whether free cash flow and leverage improve enough in the coming quarters to make the sports bet look like a strength rather than a burden.
