## The short version: a war-driven windfall, priced to fade

Methanex (TSX: MX), the world's largest producer and supplier of methanol, closed at **CA$95.94** on November 2, 2026, up about **95%** over twelve months and **17%** in the last month alone. The reason is not a new plant or a new product. The conflict in the Middle East has kept roughly **a third of internationally traded methanol** off the market, and Methanex is selling into that shortage from plants in Louisiana, Texas, Alberta, Chile and Egypt.

The third quarter showed how much that is worth: earnings well above forecasts, a cash surge, the 2027 bonds on their way out and a new **5% buyback**. Yet the stock trades at only about **7x next year's expected earnings**, the cheapest multiple in its peer group, which tells you the market expects this windfall to fade. Below we go through the quarter, the buyback and debt plan, and how Methanex compares with Nutrien, CCL Industries, Chemtrade and CES Energy on size, returns, valuation, dividends and analyst targets.

## What happened: a Q3 beat and a buyback on October 29

On **October 29, 2026**, after the market close, Methanex reported third-quarter results and announced a share buyback. All figures are in US dollars:

- **Adjusted earnings of US$3.69 a share**, against analyst estimates of about **US$2.86**
- **Revenue of US$1.39B**, up about **50%** from US$927M a year earlier
- **Adjusted EBITDA of US$543M**, versus US$191M in the third quarter of 2025
- An **average realized price of US$510 a tonne**, up from US$345 a year earlier
- **Cash flow from operations of US$646M**, about **3.5x** the US$184M of a year ago, and adjusted free cash flow of US$520M
- A **normal course issuer bid for up to 5% of its shares** (3,868,213 shares) from **November 4, 2026** to November 3, 2027

The key detail is that **price did the work**. Production slipped about 3% to 2.14 million tonnes, even with record North American output, because Chile ran on one plant through most of the quarter and Trinidad's Titan plant was idled. Management said the conflict's "dramatic impact" on the industry persisted through the quarter, with the region that normally supplies 30% to 35% of traded methanol badly curtailed.

### Cash, debt and the outlook

The cash is going to the balance sheet first. Methanex ended September with **US$912M** of cash, redeemed **US$300M** of its **US$700M** notes due October 2027 after the quarter, and expects to repay the final **US$400M** in the fourth quarter. For October and November it expects a realized price of about **US$525 to US$550 a tonne** and **higher adjusted EBITDA** than in the third quarter.

There was also a footprint change. Methanex agreed to sell its remaining New Zealand natural gas entitlements from early 2027 and expects to idle its Motunui site indefinitely in the first quarter. The shares jumped **14%** on October 30 to about C$100, then slipped about **4%** on November 2 as oil prices fell.

## Business overview: Methanex vs TSX chemical and materials peers

Methanex makes and sells methanol, a basic chemical used in plastics, adhesives, paints and fuels, plus some ammonia from its Beaumont, Texas plant. Its biggest assets are in the US Gulf Coast, where it added Beaumont and a 50% stake in Natgasoline through its June 2025 acquisition, along with plants in Medicine Hat, Chile, Egypt, New Zealand and Trinidad. Earnings swing hard with methanol prices, which is why the stock behaves like a commodity producer.

The screener puts Methanex in **Process industries**. From that sector we kept the chemicals and materials names with real scale: **Nutrien** (fertilizer, like methanol a natural-gas-based product), **CCL Industries** (specialty labels and packaging), **Chemtrade** (industrial chemicals) and **CES Energy Solutions** (oilfield specialty chemicals). The sector's cannabis producers were left out as not comparable, along with smaller packaging and paper names, to keep the table chemicals-focused.

## Why it matters: from leveraged cyclical to cash machine

A year ago, Methanex looked like a **cyclical stock carrying extra debt** from its big US acquisition, with the shares near a 52-week low. Today it is a **cash machine for as long as the supply shock lasts**, and that cash is going to debt first and buybacks second.

For investors, the question is not this quarter. It is how long methanol stays this tight, and how much of the windfall Methanex can lock in, through lower debt and fewer shares, before prices normalize.

## Bull case

1. **The windfall is not over yet.** Methanex guided to a higher realized price of about US$525 to US$550 a tonne and higher adjusted EBITDA in the fourth quarter.
2. **Debt down, share count next.** The US$700M of 2027 notes should be gone by year-end, and the 5% buyback starts November 4, so more of each dollar of cash flow accrues per share.
3. **Low-cost assets stay low-cost.** Its US Gulf Coast plants run on cheap natural gas and North American output hit a record, which supports earnings even after prices fall.

## Bear case and risks

1. **The windfall depends on lost supply.** If shipping through the Strait of Hormuz returns to normal, methanol prices could fall fast.
2. **High prices are hurting demand.** Raymond James noted Chinese methanol-to-olefins plants cutting operating rates sharply in September.
3. **Earnings are expected to shrink.** Consensus sees earnings more than halving by 2028, and Methanex is shrinking its footprint in New Zealand and Trinidad.

## What to watch

1. **December and January methanol prices**, the first read on whether the fourth-quarter guide holds into 2027.
2. **Any deal that reopens Middle East methanol exports**, the single biggest swing factor for prices.
3. **The pace of the buyback** under the new issuer bid that runs to November 3, 2027.
4. **Fourth-quarter results**, expected early in 2027, including the final US$400M bond repayment.

## Bottom line

Methanex has turned a supply shock into real cash: a big earnings beat, the 2027 bonds nearly repaid and a 5% buyback about to start. The stock has nearly doubled in a year, and at about 7x next year's expected earnings the market is clearly treating this as a peak-cycle windfall rather than a new normal. The next tests are whether methanol prices hold through the winter and how much of the cash Methanex returns before supply comes back.
