## The short version: a simpler CAE, paid for in cash

CAE (TSX: CAE), the Montreal company best known for its flight simulators and pilot training, closed at **CA$35.00** on Friday, October 23. On **October 26, 2026**, it agreed to sell **Flightscape**, its airline operations software business, to SITA for an enterprise value of **US$500M** (about **C$701M**). The stock rose **4.6% to C$36.60** that day, but it is still down about **11% over the last year** and up only about **2% over the last month**, the weakest one-year record in its peer group.

The deal turns CAE into a simpler simulation and training company with more room on its balance sheet. On trailing earnings the stock looks pricey at about **39x**, but on free cash flow it trades at about **14x**, well below the peer average. The question for a new buyer is whether a cleaner story and lower debt can close the gap with peers that have rallied hard. Below we go through the deal, the quarter behind it, and how CAE compares with Bombardier, MDA Space, Exchange Income, Magellan Aerospace and Calian on size, returns, valuation and analyst targets.

## What happened: Flightscape goes to SITA

On **October 26, 2026**, CAE announced a definitive agreement to sell Flightscape to **SITA**, a global aviation technology company owned by the air transport community.

- **Price:** enterprise value of **US$500M** (about C$701M), subject to customary adjustments.
- **What Flightscape does:** software that helps airlines plan flights, manage crews, control operations, coordinate ground activities and run in-flight services. SITA says it is used daily by **more than 90 airlines**.
- **Timing:** closing is expected in the **first half of 2027**, after regulatory approvals and other closing conditions. Flightscape keeps operating as usual until then.
- **Use of proceeds:** CAE says it will invest in its **core simulation and training businesses** and **return capital to shareholders**, while keeping financial flexibility.
- **Advisers:** BMO Capital Markets and Citigroup for CAE; Rothschild & Co. for SITA.

The sale ends a strategic review CAE started about **five months earlier**. Under chief executive **Matthew Bromberg**, the company has been cutting costs and focusing on cash flow, including job cuts in April. For SITA, Flightscape adds the operational core to a string of airline-operations purchases, including Safety Line in 2021 and Big Blue Analytics earlier this year.

For scale, C$701M is about **a quarter of CAE's net debt** of roughly C$2.6B at the end of June. The sale is a balance-sheet and focus story more than an earnings event: CAE gives up a recurring software stream in exchange for cash that can cut debt or fund buybacks.

### The Q1 fiscal 2027 print behind the deal

CAE reported its first quarter of fiscal 2027 (to June 30) on **August 12, 2026**:

- **Revenue of C$1.17B, up 6.8%** from C$1.10B a year earlier.
- **Adjusted EPS of C$0.26**, flat; reported EPS fell to C$0.10 after C$48M of restructuring costs.
- **Defense:** revenue up **8.3%** to C$532M, with adjusted segment operating income up **9.1%**.
- **Civil Aviation:** revenue up 5.6% to C$642M, but adjusted segment operating income down **13.7%** to C$106M, hit by higher costs, lower simulator-sales contribution and the Middle East conflict.
- **Free cash flow of C$104M**, net debt-to-adjusted EBITDA of **2.27x**, and an adjusted backlog of about **C$19.2B**. The fiscal 2027 outlook was unchanged.

The weak spot is Civil margins. Defense is improving, but the bigger Civil business earned less on more revenue, and that is the part of the story the Flightscape cash cannot fix on its own.

## Business overview: CAE vs aerospace and defence peers

CAE builds full-flight simulators and runs a global network of training centres for airline and business-jet pilots, and it trains military crews and supplies simulation systems to defence forces. Civil training is recurring and tied to how much airlines and business jets fly; simulator sales and defence programs are lumpier. Flightscape was the digital airline-operations piece that sat beside the training business.

On the TSX screener, CAE sits in **Commercial services**, where every other name is below C$1B and mostly unrelated, so we used a documented closest-sector exception. Bombardier (BBD.B) builds business jets, the market behind much of CAE's business-aviation training. MDA Space (MDA) builds satellites and space robotics. Exchange Income (EIF) owns aviation, aerospace and manufacturing businesses. Magellan Aerospace (MAL) makes aircraft structures and engine parts. Calian (CGY) is CAE's one same-sector peer of any size and runs military training programs. We left out Air Canada, a CAE customer with a different business model, and smaller names.

## Why it matters: from a sprawling company to a focused one

The **old narrative** was a company with too many moving parts: training centres, simulator sales, defence programs and software, with Civil margins slipping and restructuring charges clouding earnings. That is part of why CAE has lagged peers that offer a cleaner aerospace or defence story.

The **new narrative** is a simpler simulation and training company with a stronger balance sheet. Selling Flightscape removes a non-core business and brings in cash that can cut debt or fund buybacks. The open question is whether investors will pay a higher multiple for that focus, or wait for proof that Civil margins are recovering.

## Bull case

1. **Cash without dilution.** About C$701M can cut roughly a quarter of net debt or fund buybacks without issuing new shares.
2. **Defense is growing.** Defense revenue rose 8.3% last quarter with higher profit, as governments spend more on military training.
3. **Cost cuts are on track.** CAE is targeting **C$125–150M** a year in transformation savings by fiscal 2030, which should lift margins as restructuring costs fade.

## Bear case and risks

1. **Civil profit is shrinking.** Civil adjusted segment operating income fell 13.7% last quarter on higher costs and weaker simulator sales.
2. **Giving up recurring software revenue.** Flightscape was a subscription-style software stream; selling it makes CAE more dependent on training and hardware cycles.
3. **A long road to closing.** The deal is not expected to close until the first half of 2027 and still needs regulatory approval.

## What to watch

1. **Second-quarter fiscal 2027 results**, expected around **November 10, 2026**, with the conference call the next morning.
2. **Civil Aviation margins**, and whether higher training-centre utilization offsets cost pressure.
3. **How CAE uses the cash**: debt reduction, buybacks, or both, and any update to its capital-allocation priorities.
4. **Regulatory approval and closing** of the Flightscape sale in the first half of 2027.

## Bottom line

CAE has a strong position in flight simulation and pilot training, a growing defence business and a backlog of about C$19.2B, and the Flightscape sale gives it a cleaner story and real cash. The issue is earnings quality: Civil margins are falling, restructuring charges are still flowing through, and the stock has lagged every peer over the last year. At about 14x free cash flow and the lowest price to book in the group, the valuation is not demanding, but the next test is the second-quarter report and whether Civil margins stop shrinking. Until then, the sale is a promise of a simpler company, not yet proof of a better one.
