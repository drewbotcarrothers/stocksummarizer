## The short version: a profit miss at a below-peer price

Nutrien (TSX/NYSE: NTR), the Saskatoon-based crop-input giant and the world's largest potash producer, **missed on third-quarter profit**. Adjusted earnings fell **37%** to **US$0.61** a share, as surging sulfur costs wiped out almost all of the profit in its phosphate business. On the same night it finished a strategic review of that business and said it will **shut its White Springs, Florida phosphate operations** in the second quarter of 2027.

The stock arrives at this news in decent shape. It closed at **CA$98.20** on November 2, up about **29%** over twelve months, though flat over the past month. On the TSX screener it trades at about **14 times trailing earnings**, below the roughly **17x** average of the TSX chemical and process names we compare it with, and it yields about **3.2%**. So the question is whether this miss is a buying chance in a cheap-looking stock, or a warning that the earnings base is shrinking. Below we go through the quarter, the closures, and how Nutrien compares with Methanex, CCL Industries, Chemtrade and CES Energy Solutions on size, returns, valuation, dividends and analyst targets.

## What happened: a Q3 miss and a phosphate reset

Nutrien reported after the close on **November 4, 2026**. It reports in U.S. dollars. The headline numbers:

- **Adjusted EPS of US$0.61**, down from US$0.97 a year earlier and short of the roughly US$0.74–0.80 analysts expected (net earnings US$288 million, or US$0.60 a share),
- **sales of US$6.06 billion**, up 1%, a little ahead of forecasts,
- **adjusted EBITDA of US$1.31 billion**, down 8%,
- a second straight quarterly miss, after a smaller one in the second quarter.

The damage came from two segments. **Phosphate** adjusted EBITDA collapsed **93%** to just **US$8 million**, because the cost of sulfur, a key input for making phosphate fertilizer, rose far faster than selling prices. **Nitrogen** fell **11%** to US$497 million as volumes dropped about 17%, reflecting the shutdown of its Trinidad plant and heavier maintenance. **Potash**, the biggest earnings engine, held steady at **US$730 million** on 4.07 million tonnes sold, and Retail slipped 12% to US$203 million on thinner crop-nutrient margins.

### Guidance, closures and cash returns

The outlook moved in both directions. Nutrien **raised the low end of its potash volume guidance** to **14.3–14.8 million tonnes** and lifted nitrogen volumes to 9.3–9.7 million tonnes. But it **cut its Retail EBITDA outlook** to **US$1.72–1.85 billion** (from US$1.75–1.95 billion) and trimmed 2026 capital spending to US$1.95–2.00 billion.

The bigger strategic news is the phosphate review. Nutrien received bids but said none reflected the long-term value of the assets, so it will **keep its Aurora, North Carolina complex** and the plants that upgrade its output, and **shut White Springs** in the second quarter of 2027 as the mine nears the end of its reserves. White Springs is expected to make up about 20% of 2026 phosphate sales but about 28% of the segment's costs. Closing it should cut sustaining capital spending and lease payments by about **US$50 million a year** and, management says, lift EBITDA and free cash flow. The bill comes first: about US$430 million of White Springs assets and US$760 million of Trinidad assets will be tested for **impairment in the fourth quarter**, along with severance and shutdown costs.

Shareholders are still being paid. Nutrien returned **US$1.3 billion** in the first nine months through dividends and buybacks, with **buybacks up 36%**, and bought back another US$83 million of stock in the fourth quarter through November 3. The NYSE shares had closed up 4% at **US$72.07** before the release and slipped about **1.5%** in after-hours trading.

## Business overview: Nutrien vs TSX chemical peers

Nutrien has four businesses: Retail (Nutrien Ag Solutions, which sells seed, crop protection, fertilizer and services to farmers in North America, Australia and South America), Potash (mines in Saskatchewan), Nitrogen (ammonia and urea plants in Canada and the U.S.) and Phosphate (Aurora and White Springs). Retail brings most of the revenue, while potash and nitrogen bring most of the profit.

On the TSX screener, Nutrien sits in the **Process industries** sector. There is no other large TSX fertilizer company, so we use the sector's chemicals and materials names: Methanex (MX), the methanol producer that, like Nutrien's nitrogen arm, turns natural gas into a commodity chemical; CCL Industries (CCL.A), the label and packaging maker; Chemtrade Logistics (CHE.UN), an industrial chemicals business that, among other things, handles sulphuric acid; and CES Energy Solutions (CEU), which supplies oilfield chemicals. Cannabis producers, which the screener also files under this sector, were left out.

Nutrien rose about **29%** over the last year, a solid gain but behind the group's two stars: CES Energy more than doubled and Methanex nearly doubled. CCL rose about 25% and Chemtrade about 10%. Nutrien trades at about **14.4 times** trailing earnings, below the group average of about **17x**, and it is the **cheapest stock here on book value** at about **1.3 times**. Its very low PEG is flattered by a big jump in trailing earnings and is not a reliable signal on its own. On next year's expected earnings, Nutrien is about **13 times**, close to the group average, because Methanex looks very cheap on that measure after its methanol windfall. Chemtrade is the cheapest on trailing earnings and sales.

The catch is cash flow. Nutrien sits near the group average on free cash flow, at about **15 times**, well above Methanex's roughly 4.5 times and a little above Chemtrade. That is not a red flag on its own, but it means the "cheap on earnings" story depends on those earnings turning into cash once sulfur costs and one-time shutdown spending ease. The fair read is that Nutrien looks **reasonably priced, not deeply discounted**: investors are not paying much for growth, but the multiple already assumes earnings won't fall sharply from here.

## Why it matters: from commodity call to cleanup story

The old narrative was Nutrien as **a pure fertilizer-price call**, priced off potash and nitrogen benchmarks. The new narrative is also **a cleanup story**: management has shut Trinidad, is closing White Springs, has sold non-core investments, is trimming capital spending and keeps buying back shares. Potash and nitrogen prices still matter most, but capital discipline and asset pruning now sit beside them.

The question for investors is whether that self-help can offset a weak phosphate business and softer retail margins. If it does, the closures leave a smaller but more profitable company with higher free cash flow per share. If it doesn't, the low multiple may simply reflect lower earnings ahead.

## Bull case

1. **Potash is strong.** The biggest earnings engine is selling at record volumes this year, and Nutrien expects global potash shipments to grow again in 2027, to 75–79 million tonnes.
2. **High-cost assets are going.** Closing White Springs and Trinidad removes two of the weakest operations, cuts capital needs and should lift free cash flow once the one-time costs are booked.
3. **The valuation leaves room.** The stock trades below its peers on trailing earnings and book value, with a yield above 3% and steady buybacks shrinking the share count.

## Bear case and risks

1. **A second straight miss.** Earnings fell short again, after a smaller miss in the second quarter, and adjusted EPS is down 37% from a year ago.
2. **Sulfur may stay expensive.** If sulfur costs stay high, what is left of phosphate (Aurora) could keep earning little.
3. **More bad news is scheduled.** Retail guidance was cut, and impairment charges and shutdown costs land in the fourth quarter.
4. **Fertilizer prices swing fast.** Crop prices, trade policy, Indian tenders and Chinese export quotas can move potash, nitrogen and phosphate prices quickly.

## What to watch

1. **Potash prices and 2027 demand.** Whether Nutrien's 75–79 million tonne global shipment forecast holds, and whether prices support the potash engine that carried this quarter.
2. **Sulfur costs and Aurora phosphate margins.** Whether the remaining phosphate business can earn a decent return once White Springs is gone.
3. **Q4 impairment charges.** The size of the White Springs (about US$430 million of assets) and Trinidad (about US$760 million) write-downs and related shutdown costs.
4. **The fall application season and Q4 results in February.** North American fertilizer demand and whether Retail margins stabilize after the guidance cut.

## Bottom line

Nutrien's third quarter was a clear miss, driven by a phosphate business that sulfur costs have almost wiped out, and the White Springs closure confirms that part of the portfolio is shrinking. But potash, the core of the company, held firm, volume guidance rose, and the stock trades below its TSX chemical peers on earnings and book value with an above-average yield. Whether that makes the miss a chance or a warning depends on potash prices and on management turning closures into higher free cash flow.
