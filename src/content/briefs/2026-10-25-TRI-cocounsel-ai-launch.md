## The short version: an AI launch moved the stock, not an earnings report

Thomson Reuters (TSX: TRI), the Toronto company behind Westlaw, the Checkpoint tax research service and Reuters News, was **CA$141.76** in our October 8 table, down about **33%** over twelve months. On **Thursday, October 22**, it launched the next generation of **CoCounsel Tax, Audit & Accounting**, an agentic AI platform for accountants, and the TSX shares rose about **6.5%** that day. They closed Friday, October 23, at **C$172.41**. Even after that move, the stock sits about **25% below** its 52-week high.

The launch matters because of what happened earlier this year. In February, new AI legal tools from Anthropic set off a selloff in data and software stocks, and Thomson Reuters fell nearly 18% in one day as investors asked whether chatbots could replace paid research. CoCounsel is the company's answer: **AI built on content rivals don't have**. At about **27.5x trailing earnings**, a little below its peer average, the question for a new buyer is whether the AI threat is turning into a growth driver. Below we go through the launch, the business behind it, and how Thomson Reuters compares with Constellation Software, CGI, Descartes, OpenText and Kinaxis on size, returns, valuation, dividends and analyst targets.

## What happened: CoCounsel moves from answering questions to doing the work

On **October 22, 2026**, Thomson Reuters released the next generation of CoCounsel Tax, Audit & Accounting, available immediately to all of its U.S. customers. The company describes it as a platform that goes from source documents and client data to a finished, reviewable result in one workflow:

- **Verified calculations:** it writes and runs code for depreciation, amortization, required minimum distributions, qualified business income and multi-scenario comparisons.
- **Finished deliverables:** multi-tab Excel workbooks with live formulas and an assumptions tab, plus memos in Word and PDF that follow a firm's own templates.
- **Grounded in Checkpoint**, the company's accounting research engine with more than **12 million** primary-source and analytical documents maintained by CPAs and tax lawyers.
- **Connected systems:** integrations with UltraTax, Engagement Manager and Guided Assurance, plus native MCP support so it can work inside other applications.

Management was candid that code execution alone is now "table stakes" in this market. Its pitch is judgment: applying the right tax authority for the right period before running a calculation, so a professional can check the work and sign it. Investors liked the pitch. The Nasdaq shares closed up almost 7% at US$123.26, and the TSX shares gained about 6.5%, before holding flat on Friday.

### The business behind the launch

The launch lands on a business that is still growing. In its second-quarter results, reported **August 5, 2026** (all figures in US dollars):

- **Revenue of US$1.95B, up 9%**, with **organic growth of 8%**.
- **Organic growth of 10%** in the "Big 3" segments (Legal Professionals, Corporates, and Tax, Audit & Accounting), which make up more than 80% of revenue.
- **Adjusted EPS of US$0.99, up 14%**, and **free cash flow of US$727M, up 29%**.
- **Recurring revenue of 82%** of the total, mostly subscriptions.
- **Full-year outlook raised** to about 8% total and organic growth, and 9.5% to 10% for the Big 3.

The company also agreed to sell 51% of its Global Print business to KKR for about US$500 million, completed a US$600 million buyback in July, and returned another US$605 million through a special distribution in May. The weak spot is the Tax, Audit & Accounting segment's margin, which slipped 1.6 points in the first half. That is the segment this launch has to lift.

## Business overview: Thomson Reuters vs Canadian software peers

Thomson Reuters sells content-powered software and research to professionals: Westlaw and CoCounsel for lawyers, Checkpoint and tax software for accountants, compliance and trade tools for corporations, and the Reuters news agency. Most of it is sold by subscription, which makes revenue steady and predictable.

On the TSX screener, Thomson Reuters sits in the **Technology services** sector, which also holds Canada's largest software companies. Constellation Software (CSU) buys and runs hundreds of niche vertical-software businesses. CGI (GIB.A) is an IT services and consulting firm. Descartes (DSG) sells logistics and customs software, OpenText (OTEX) sells enterprise information-management software, and Kinaxis (KXS) sells supply-chain planning software. We left out RB Global, which runs auctions rather than software, and Constellation's spin-offs Topicus and Lumine, which would double up on the same model.

## Why it matters: from AI loser to AI seller

For most of this year, the market treated Thomson Reuters as a **possible AI loser**: a content business whose research and drafting tools a general-purpose chatbot could copy at a fraction of the price. The February selloff priced in exactly that fear.

The **new narrative** the company is selling is the opposite: that trusted, constantly updated content plus AI is the product, and that law and accounting firms will pay more for tools that do the work and show their sources. CoCounsel reaching a million users earlier this year, and now a version that produces finished tax and audit work, supports that case. The open question is pricing: whether customers pay more for AI features, or whether AI simply becomes something they expect for free.

## Bull case

1. **Recurring revenue.** More than 80% of revenue comes from subscriptions sold to lawyers, accountants and corporations, and organic growth has held around 8%.
2. **New things to sell.** AI tools like CoCounsel give Thomson Reuters new products to sell to the same customers at higher prices, built on content rivals would need decades to match.
3. **Strong balance sheet and returns.** Net debt was under 1x EBITDA at mid-year, it bought back US$600 million of stock this year, and it has raised its dividend for 33 straight years.

## Bear case and risks

1. **AI rivals keep improving.** General AI models are getting better at legal and tax work, and the company itself calls code execution table stakes.
2. **Not cheap against peers.** Even after the selloff, the stock costs more than most peers on sales and free cash flow, and about 26x forward earnings at Friday's price.
3. **Little target upside.** The average target is barely above today's price, which leaves little room if growth slows or AI pricing disappoints.

## What to watch

1. **Third-quarter results on November 3**, where management has guided to about 8% organic growth and an adjusted EBITDA margin of about 36%.
2. **CoCounsel adoption**: any user or revenue numbers for the new Tax, Audit & Accounting platform, and whether that segment's margin recovers.
3. **The updated outlook**, as Global Print moves into the KKR joint venture and is reported as a discontinued operation.
4. **The AI fear trade**: whether new releases from AI labs knock information-services stocks down again.

## Bottom line

Thomson Reuters still runs a steady, subscription-heavy business that grew about 8% organically last quarter, pays a growing dividend and carries little debt. This year the market questioned whether AI would erode that business, and the October 22 CoCounsel launch is the company's clearest answer so far: AI that does finished tax and audit work, built on its own content. The stock has already responded, and at Friday's price it trades at roughly 26x expected earnings and close to analysts' average target. The next test is the November 3 quarter, and whether AI products start showing up in growth and margins rather than just headlines.
