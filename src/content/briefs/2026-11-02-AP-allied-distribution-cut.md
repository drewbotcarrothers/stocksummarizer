## The short version: the income is gone, the debt is the story

Allied Properties REIT (TSX: AP.UN), the owner of brick-and-beam urban offices in Toronto, Montreal and other Canadian cities, closed at **CA$5.61** on October 23, 2026, already down about **70%** over twelve months. Five days later it **cut its monthly distribution by 90%**, its second cut in less than a year, and the units fell **19%** on October 29 and another **5%** on October 30, to **C$4.03**.

For years, many investors owned Allied for one thing: a steady monthly cheque. That cheque is now 0.6 cents a unit. At about **one-fifth of its reported net asset value**, the question is whether Allied is a deep-value repair story or a value trap. Below we go through the cut, the third-quarter results behind it, the debt plan, and how Allied compares with its closest TSX REIT peers — RioCan, Choice Properties, First Capital, Dream Industrial and Dream Office — on size, returns, valuation, distributions and analyst targets.

## What happened: a 90% distribution cut on October 28

On **October 28, 2026**, after the market close, Allied reported its third-quarter results and announced that its Board of Trustees would:

- **cut the monthly distribution by 90%**, from **C$0.06** to **C$0.006** per unit (C$0.072 a year), starting with the November 2026 distribution,
- keep about **C$126M a year** of cash that would have gone to unitholders and put it toward **debt reduction**,
- still pay the previously declared C$0.06 October distribution on November 16.

It is the **second cut in a year**. In December 2025, Allied cut its monthly distribution by 60%, from C$0.15 to C$0.06. Together, the two cuts take the annual payout from C$1.80 to about seven cents, a drop of roughly 96%. CEO Cecilia Williams said strengthening the balance sheet is the priority, and that reducing the distribution "will retain cash to accelerate debt reduction."

### The Q3 print behind the cut

The quarter showed why the payout could not hold:

- **FFO per unit of C$0.194**, down **59%** from C$0.470 a year ago (excluding condo items, prepayment costs and unit-based compensation). Part of the drop comes from more units: Allied sold 56 million new units at C$10.00 in February 2026.
- **AFFO per unit of C$0.110**, down 75%, for an **AFFO payout ratio of 163%**. Allied was paying out far more than its recurring cash flow.
- **Rental revenue of C$138.1M, down 6.6%**, and same-asset NOI in the rental portfolio down 7.7%.
- **No interest income on the Westbank-related loans.** Allied stopped recognizing interest on its loans tied to KING Toronto and 150 West Georgia in Vancouver, which cut interest income to C$0.5M from C$10.0M.
- **Net debt of 12.5x adjusted EBITDA** for the first nine months, up from 12.0x in the second quarter. On the latest quarter alone, annualized, it was 13.5x.

There was some good news. Occupied and leased area reached **84.9%** and **86.8%**, ahead of plan, and Allied raised its year-end occupancy outlook to **86.0–86.5%**. Its leasing pipeline is up **49%** since the start of the year. The 2026 FFO outlook was cut to C$170–175M from C$185–200M, mostly because of larger and earlier asset sales.

### The debt plan

Allied has completed **C$321M** of property sales this year and has firm deals for about **C$340M** more, including **Toronto House** and **19 Duncan**, expected to close in the fourth quarter subject to Competition Act approval. That makes roughly **C$660M** of gross proceeds, all going to debt. Allied also expanded its sales program by another **C$750M–1.5B** over several years. It expects to repay at least C$350M of its **C$700M of April 2027 bonds** with sale proceeds and refinance the rest. The C$125M partial repayment of its 150 West Georgia loan, once expected in December 2026, is now expected as part of a resolution in 2027.

## Business overview: Allied vs TSX REIT peers

Allied owns and operates distinctive urban workspace — many of them converted brick-and-beam industrial buildings — in Toronto, Montreal, Vancouver, Calgary and other Canadian cities. Its office focus is what separates it from most of the REIT peers below.

On the TSX screener, REITs sit in the **Finance** sector alongside banks and insurers, so the peer set is the large TSX REITs: **RioCan (REI.UN)** and **First Capital (FCR.UN)**, both urban retail and mixed-use landlords; **Choice Properties (CHP.UN)**, the grocery-anchored landlord tied to Loblaw; **Dream Industrial (DIR.UN)**, a warehouse owner; and **Dream Office (D.UN)**, a downtown Toronto office REIT. Dream Office is under C$1B, but it is the closest pure-office peer, so it stays in.

A discount that large says the market does not believe Allied's IFRS property values, or expects more buildings to be sold below them before the debt is under control.

## Why it matters: from monthly income to balance sheet repair

The old Allied was a **steady monthly-income REIT** with a well-known portfolio of character office buildings, and that is why many retail investors held it. The new Allied is a **balance sheet repair story**. The income is mostly gone, and the value of the units now depends on selling buildings at reasonable prices, getting paid on the Westbank loans and bringing debt down before the 2027 bond maturities.

That shifts who the units suit. Income investors have little reason to stay for a 1.8% yield. The units now appeal mainly to value investors who are willing to wait for leasing and asset sales to close the gap to book value — and who can accept the risk that it never fully closes.

## Bull case

1. **Leasing is turning.** Occupancy beat plan, the year-end occupancy outlook went up to 86.0–86.5%, and the leasing pipeline is up 49% this year. Most of the cash flow benefit lands in 2027 as new rents start.
2. **Deep discount to book.** At about one-fifth of reported NAV, the units price in a lot of bad news. If the Toronto House and 19 Duncan sales close near their carrying values, that supports the book value and could narrow the gap.
3. **Every dollar now goes to debt.** About C$126M a year of retained cash and roughly C$660M of asset sales are directed to debt repayment, which repairs the balance sheet without another unit sale at depressed prices.

## Bear case and risks

1. **Debt is still very high.** Net debt is above 12x EBITDA, and C$700M of bonds come due in April 2027. Refinancing at higher rates would cut FFO further.
2. **The Westbank loans.** Allied no longer books interest on the KING Toronto and 150 West Georgia loans, already took a C$44M expected credit loss this year, and the C$125M 150 West Georgia repayment has slipped into 2027. More write-downs are possible.
3. **Selling into a weak market.** Asset sales shrink future cash flow, and buyers know Allied needs to sell. Allied also sold 56 million new units at C$10 in February 2026; they now trade near C$4.

## What to watch

1. **Toronto House and 19 Duncan.** Whether these sales close in the fourth quarter, and at what price versus book value.
2. **Leverage by year-end.** Whether net debt falls to the mid-11x EBITDA range Allied is targeting.
3. **The April 2027 bonds.** The plan to repay C$350M with sale proceeds and refinance the other C$350M, and at what rate.
4. **The Westbank loans.** Any recovery on 150 West Georgia and progress on KING Toronto, Allied's last committed development.

## Bottom line

Allied Properties has turned from an income REIT into a deleveraging story. The **90% distribution cut** keeps about C$126M a year for debt, on top of roughly C$660M of asset sales, and leasing is finally improving. But net debt is still above **12x EBITDA**, the Westbank loans are paying nothing, and C$700M of bonds come due in April 2027. At **C$4.03**, about a fifth of reported book value, the units price in a lot of risk; the next few quarters of asset sales and refinancing will show whether that discount is an opportunity or a fair warning.
