## The short version: a record plan at a full price

Fortis (TSX: FTS), the St. John's-based owner of regulated electric and gas utilities across Canada, the U.S. and the Caribbean, has just unveiled the **biggest spending plan in its history**: **C$32.2 billion** of capital over 2027 to 2031. It paired the plan with a **4% dividend increase**, its **53rd straight year** of raises. The stock closed at **CA$82.95** on November 2, up about **17%** over twelve months and about **10%** in the past month, then hit a new 52-week high of C$84.24 during the session on November 3 before closing at C$83.58.

The catch is price. At about **24 times trailing earnings**, Fortis already trades right on the average of its TSX utility peers, and above the **C$79.23** average analyst target. So the question for a new buyer is simple: is the good news already priced in? Below we go through the quarter, the new plan and how Fortis compares with its closest TSX peers — Hydro One, Emera, AltaGas, Canadian Utilities and Capital Power — on size, returns, valuation, dividends and analyst targets.

## What happened: a record five-year plan and a 4% raise

On **November 3, 2026**, Fortis released its third-quarter results together with its new 2027–2031 outlook. The plan brings:

- a **C$32.2 billion** five-year capital program, **C$3.4 billion** more than the previous plan and the largest in the company's history,
- about **75%** aimed at transmission and distribution, the lower-risk "wires and pipes" that regulators approve and pay a return on,
- midyear rate base growth from **C$45.3 billion** in 2026 to **C$63.9 billion** by 2031, about **7.5% a year** (up from 7% in the prior plan),
- a **4% increase** in the quarterly dividend to **C$0.665** a share, payable December 1 to holders of record on November 17,
- annual dividend growth guidance of **4–6%** extended through **2031**.

Most of the increase comes from two places. At ITC, Fortis's Midwest transmission business, it is driven by the regional grid operator's long-range transmission projects, including a US$1.0 billion competitive project in Iowa awarded in October (half of it is in the new plan). At FortisBC, it reflects continued work at the Tilbury LNG site in British Columbia, including the Phase 1B expansion and a new LNG storage tank, both of which now have environmental approval.

The plan is funded mainly with cash from operations and debt issued at the regulated utilities. New common equity is expected to come mostly through the dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP), and Fortis plans to re-establish its at-the-market share program in early 2027. That makes this a **steady compounding story, not an earnings jolt**: the payoff comes from a bigger rate base over years, not from one quarter.

### The Q3 print behind the plan

The quarter itself was solid rather than spectacular:

- **Net earnings of C$474 million, or C$0.92 a share**, up from C$0.81 a year ago (C$0.87 on an adjusted basis, which excludes taxes and costs on the 2025 sale of FortisTCI).
- **Adjusted EPS of C$0.92**, a little ahead of the roughly C$0.90 analysts expected.
- **Capital spending of C$4.2 billion** through September, with about **C$6.0 billion** now expected for 2026, up from C$5.6 billion.
- Growth came from a bigger **rate base** — the assets regulators let Fortis earn a return on — and from higher retail electricity sales at UNS Energy in Arizona.

The weak spot is cash. Heavy spending means free cash flow is negative, so the plan depends on steady access to debt markets and on new shares, which matters for the valuation below.

## Business overview: Fortis vs utility peers

Fortis owns a group of regulated utility businesses, led by ITC (U.S. Midwest transmission), UNS Energy (Tucson Electric Power in Arizona), Central Hudson in New York, FortisBC in British Columbia and FortisAlberta. Almost all of its earnings come from regulated operations, where returns are set by regulators, which is why the stock is usually treated as a low-volatility dividend holding.

On the TSX screener, Fortis sits in the **Utilities** sector. Hydro One (H) is Ontario's dominant transmission and distribution utility. Emera (EMA) owns regulated electric and gas utilities in Florida and Atlantic Canada, and announced a C$72 billion merger with Canadian Utilities in October. AltaGas (ALA) mixes U.S. gas utilities with a midstream export business. Canadian Utilities (CU) is the ATCO group's utility arm, and Capital Power (CPX) is a power producer rather than a regulated wires company. Brookfield's partnership-style units and ATCO (the parent of CU) were left out to keep the table comparable.

## Why it matters: from sleepy dividend stock to growth utility

The old narrative was Fortis as a **sleepy dividend stock**: low volatility, slow growth, a reliable raise every year. The new narrative is **a growth utility**, with a record plan driven by data centers, mining, industrial load and new transmission lines, especially in Arizona and the U.S. Midwest.

That growth has to be paid for. The plan relies on debt, the DRIP and some share sales, and the stock already reflects a good part of the good news. For investors, the question is whether faster rate-base growth is worth paying a full peer multiple, with the share price above the Street's average target.

## Bull case

1. **A record, low-risk plan.** C$32.2 billion over five years, with about three quarters in regulated transmission and distribution that earns approved returns, supports about 7.5% annual rate-base growth.
2. **Upside not yet in the plan.** ITC sees US$2.9–3.4 billion of further Midwest transmission spending beyond the plan, the Tilbury Phase 2 LNG expansion could add C$2–4 billion, and Tucson Electric Power's resource plans show up to US$1.6 billion more, with a high-load scenario adding up to US$1.5 billion.
3. **A 53-year dividend record.** Fifty-three straight yearly raises, with 4–6% a year now guided through 2031, at a payout ratio of about 74%.

## Bear case and risks

1. **Negative free cash flow.** Fortis spends more on projects than its operations bring in (free cash flow of about −C$1.8 billion over the last twelve months), so it leans on debt and new shares. Higher interest rates would raise that cost.
2. **Dilution.** Shares issued through the DRIP and the at-the-market program dilute existing holders; Q3 alone included about C$260 million of at-the-market sales.
3. **Regulatory risk.** Arizona regulators still have to approve Tucson Electric Power's new rates, with a judge recommending a 9.65% allowed return on equity. A weaker final order would trim the growth outlook.
4. **Valuation.** The stock already sits about 5% above the average analyst target, after a 10% run in a month.

## What to watch

1. **The Arizona rate decision, expected later in November.** Whether the Arizona Corporation Commission adopts the recommended 9.65% return and new rates by December 1.
2. **More Midwest transmission.** Whether additional ITC projects move into the plan, and how much of the Iowa project is added.
3. **Interest rates.** Utilities like Fortis often benefit when rates fall, because their dividends compete with bond yields and their growth is debt-funded.
4. **Fourth-quarter results in February** and analyst targets: whether more brokers follow Scotiabank and CIBC higher, closing the gap to the share price.

## Bottom line

Fortis has earned its reputation: 53 straight years of dividend raises, a mostly regulated business and now the largest growth plan in its history, with about 7.5% rate-base growth guided through 2031. The issue is price. At about 24 times trailing earnings and 22.5 times next year's, Fortis trades right around its utility peers, and the share price is already above the C$79 average target after a 10% run in a month. The next tests are the Arizona rate decision and whether analysts keep raising targets to match the bigger plan.
