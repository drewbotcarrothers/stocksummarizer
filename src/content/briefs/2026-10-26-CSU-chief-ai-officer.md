## The short version: from AI loser to AI buyer?

Constellation Software (TSX: CSU), the Toronto company that buys and runs hundreds of small vertical-market software businesses, closed at **CA$3,395.00** on Friday, October 23. On **October 21, 2026**, it named its **first Chief AI Officer**, and the stock rose about **5%** the next day. The shares are up about **22% over the last month**, but still down about **9% over the last year**, after fears that AI could replace older software hit the whole sector.

The appointment is a signal more than an earnings event: Constellation wants to buy and build AI, not only defend against it. On trailing earnings the stock looks expensive at about **54x**, but that number is bent by acquisition amortization; on expected earnings for the next year it is closer to **19x**. The question for a new buyer is whether the AI story can lift Constellation's thin organic growth. Below we go through the new role, the quarter behind it, and how Constellation compares with Topicus, Thomson Reuters, CGI, Descartes and OpenText on size, returns, valuation, dividends and analyst targets.

## What happened: a Chief AI Officer from inside the group

On **October 21, 2026**, Constellation announced that **Daan Dijkhuizen** will be its Chief AI Officer and join the company's leadership team. He is not a new hire. He already runs the Topicus operating group inside Topicus.com, Constellation's Netherlands-based spin-off, and he is managing partner of **VMS Ventures**, Constellation's venture arm. He keeps both of those jobs.

- **Main focus:** expand VMS Ventures as the group's vehicle for investing in **AI-native vertical market software** businesses.
- **Second job:** support Constellation's business units as they put AI into their products and internal operations, "with appropriate controls for quality, safety and compliance."
- **Third job:** share best practices across Constellation's operating groups.

Investors liked it. The TSX shares closed at **C$3,236.06** on October 21 and rose to **C$3,408.57** on October 22, a gain of about 5.3% that put Constellation among the day's top TSX movers. They closed Friday at C$3,395.00.

The role does not change this year's earnings. What it changes is the message. Constellation has been treated as a possible AI loser, and a dedicated AI leader with a venture budget tells the market it intends to be one of the buyers in the next wave of software, not just a seller of the old one.

### The backdrop and the Q2 print

The appointment comes after a hard year for software stocks. In January 2026, new AI agent tools set off a sell-off that erased roughly US$285 billion of software and related market value, and by mid-April several software ETFs were down more than 30% as investors priced in the risk that AI would let customers or new rivals replace established software.

Constellation's own business kept growing. In its second-quarter results, reported in August (all figures in US dollars):

- **Revenue of US$3.34B, up 17%**, mostly from acquisitions.
- **Organic growth of 3%**, or **1% after adjusting for currency**.
- **Cash flow from operations of US$477M, up 10%.**
- **Free cash flow available to shareholders of US$345M, up 57%** from US$220M a year earlier; US$1.08B for the first half, up 48%.
- Acquisitions continued, including a **majority stake in DerbySoft**, a hospitality technology company, and the board declared a **US$1.00** quarterly dividend.

The weak spot is organic growth. A 1% currency-adjusted figure means nearly all of Constellation's growth comes from buying companies, which is exactly the part of the story the AI debate puts under pressure.

## Business overview: Constellation vs Canadian software peers

Constellation buys small, specialized software companies that serve one industry each: software for municipalities, transit agencies, hospitals, utilities, schools, private clubs and hundreds of other niches. It almost never sells them. Most revenue is recurring maintenance and subscription fees, because customers rarely replace the systems that run their day-to-day operations. Head office allocates the cash those businesses throw off into more acquisitions.

On the TSX screener, Constellation sits in the **Technology services** sector. Topicus (TOI), its European spin-off, uses the same buy-and-hold model and is run by the new Chief AI Officer. Thomson Reuters (TRI) sells content-powered software to lawyers and accountants. CGI (GIB.A) is an IT services and consulting firm. Descartes (DSG) sells logistics software, and OpenText (OTEX) sells enterprise information-management software. We left out Lumine, Constellation's second spin-off, which would double up on the same model, as well as Kinaxis, RB Global and sub-C$2B names.

## Why it matters: from "AI loser" to "AI buyer"

This year the market treated Constellation as a possible **AI loser**: a collection of older niche software that AI tools could make cheaper to copy or easier to replace. That fear is why a business with steady cash flow still trades below where it was a year ago.

Naming a Chief AI Officer argues for a different story: **an AI buyer and builder**. If it works, AI becomes a new pool of deals for VMS Ventures, and a way to make Constellation's existing businesses more efficient and their products harder to leave. The open question is whether that shows up in organic growth, which is where the market will look first.

## Bull case

1. **Sticky software.** Vertical market software runs core operations, and customers rarely rip it out, which supports high recurring revenue even in a slow economy.
2. **A long record of turning deals into cash.** Constellation's model has produced about a 475% gain over ten years, and it now has a dedicated AI investing arm to widen the deal pipeline.
3. **Growing cash flow.** Free cash flow available to shareholders rose 57% last quarter, which funds more acquisitions without needing big dividends.

## Bear case and risks

1. **Thin organic growth.** About 1% after currency means growth depends on buying companies at sensible prices, and a bigger Constellation needs bigger or more deals to move the needle.
2. **AI-driven competition.** AI could let new rivals build cheaper software quickly, pressuring prices and customer loyalty in the niches Constellation owns.
3. **A fast run-up.** After a 22% jump in a month, a lot of good news may already be priced in, and the stock still screens as one of the priciest on reported earnings.

## What to watch

1. **Third-quarter results** after the close on **November 6, 2026**, with the conference call on **November 9** at 8:00 a.m. ET.
2. **Organic growth**, and whether it rebounds toward its long-run average as RBC expects.
3. **The first AI deals through VMS Ventures**, and how they are priced.
4. **Whether the AI fear trade returns** for software stocks, which would test the October rally.

## Bottom line

Constellation has earned its premium with three decades of disciplined acquisitions and steady cash generation, and the new Chief AI Officer shows it wants to treat AI as an opportunity rather than only a threat. The issue is growth: organic growth of about 1% after currency leaves the story leaning on deals, and the stock has already rallied 22% in a month. At about 19x expected earnings and near the group average on free cash flow, the valuation is not extreme, but the November 6 report needs to show that organic growth is turning. Until then, the AI role is a promise, not a result.
