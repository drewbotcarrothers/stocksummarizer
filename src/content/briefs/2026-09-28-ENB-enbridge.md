## The short version: a bigger crude franchise, a higher yield

Enbridge (TSX: ENB), North America's largest pipeline company, closed at **CA$66.99** on September 24, 2026, after a slide of about **16%** over three months that leaves the stock roughly flat (**−2.7%**) over twelve months. In one September week, Enbridge announced a **US$2.55B** Tallgrass crude deal, a **US$600M** Permian tuck-in, a **C$3B** equity raise and a CEO succession. The new story is **a larger North American crude franchise**, funded partly with fresh shares.

After the drawdown, the indicated yield prints near **5.8%**, the second-highest in its peer group. The question for a new buyer is whether that yield is fair pay for dilution and leverage. Below we go through the deals, the equity raise, and how Enbridge compares with TC Energy, Pembina, Keyera and South Bow on size, returns, valuation, dividends and analyst targets.

## What happened: Tallgrass, Salt Creek and a C$3B raise

On **September 8, 2026**, Enbridge said CEO **Greg Ebel** will retire on **December 31, 2026**, and **Michele Harradence** will become President and CEO on **January 1, 2027**. The next day, **September 9**, it announced two acquisitions:

- **Tallgrass Energy's crude transportation business** for about **US$2.55B**, including a **majority stake in the Pony Express pipeline** into Cushing, Oklahoma.
- **Salt Creek Midstream's** Delaware Basin (Permian) gathering assets for about **US$600M**.
- Management says both deals are **accretive to distributable cash flow per share in year one**.

To help fund them, Enbridge launched a **bought-deal equity offering** of about **C$2.6B**. It closed on **September 14** at **C$3.0B** including the over-allotment, priced near **C$66.85** a share, almost exactly where the stock sat on the screener date.

The deals extend Enbridge's crude network into the U.S. midcontinent and the Permian, but they are **small relative to the company**: Raymond James put them at only about **1.5–2% of enterprise value**. The bigger near-term effect is on the share count. New equity at a depressed price dilutes existing holders, which is a main reason the stock slid.

### Leverage behind the equity raise

The raise makes more sense against the balance sheet. At the second-quarter report, Enbridge's leverage had printed near **5.1x debt to EBITDA**, above its **4.5–5x target band**. Funding the deals with debt would have pushed leverage further from the target, so the company chose equity instead.

That is the trade-off for shareholders: **dilution now in exchange for a balance sheet that heads back toward the band**. The market treated the offering as a dilution reset, and it matters for the valuation below, where Enbridge looks reasonable on earnings but stretched on free cash flow.

## Business overview: Enbridge vs midstream peers

Enbridge moves crude oil and liquids (the Mainline and Line 5 carry Western Canadian crude to U.S. and Eastern Canadian refiners), runs natural gas transmission pipelines, owns gas utilities in Ontario and the U.S., and has a smaller renewable power business. Most of its cash flow comes from **long-term contracts and regulated tolls**, which is why investors treat it like a utility, even though its revenue line swings with commodity volumes.

On the TSX screener, Enbridge sits in the **Industrial services** sector. The peers are the liquid midstream and pipeline names in the same sector: **TC Energy (TRP)**, the gas pipeline giant; **Pembina (PPL)**, a Western Canadian pipeline and processing operator; **Keyera (KEY)**, a gas processing and liquids business; and **South Bow (SOBO)**, the liquids pipeline company spun out of TC Energy. Waste Connections, in the same sector, was left out as not a midstream business.

Still, income investors will want to see the dividend growth path hold after the raise.

## Why it matters: from utility-style pipeline to bigger crude franchise

The old narrative was Enbridge as **a steady Canadian utility-style pipeline with a growing dividend**. The new narrative is **a larger North American crude franchise**: the Mainline, Line 5, Pony Express and Permian gathering, funded partly with fresh equity, under a new CEO in 2027.

Income investors care whether 5.8% is fair compensation for dilution and leverage. Growth investors care whether Tallgrass and Salt Creek are accretive fast enough to justify the raise. The open question is whether a new CEO keeps the same balance of dividend growth and deal-making.

## Bull case

1. **Upgrades after the pullback.** National Bank and BMO both upgraded, with Street targets clustered near C$80, about 19% above the screener price.
2. **Accretive deals.** Management says Tallgrass and Salt Creek add to distributable cash flow per share in year one.
3. **A rare yield.** A 5.8% indicated yield after a 16% three-month slide is unusual for this franchise if leverage returns inside the band.

## Bear case and risks

1. **Dilution with leverage above target.** C$3B of new equity arrived just as leverage printed near 5.1x, above the 4.5–5x ceiling.
2. **Cash-flow optics.** Price to free cash flow near 62x and a GAAP payout near 146% are not cheap or tidy.
3. **Succession and regulation.** A CEO change plus Line 5 and other regulatory risks can keep a discount on the multiple even if the deals close cleanly.

## What to watch

1. **Deal closings.** Timing and integration for Tallgrass and Salt Creek.
2. **Leverage.** Whether debt to EBITDA moves back inside 4.5–5x in the next quarterly reports.
3. **New CEO messaging.** Michele Harradence's first investor update after she takes over on January 1, 2027.
4. **The next dividend declaration** and any update to the growth outlook.

## Bottom line

Enbridge has kept its reputation as a dependable dividend payer and used a tough September to extend its crude network with two accretive deals. The cost was a C$3B share sale at a depressed price, landing while leverage sat above target and the CEO was preparing to hand over. At about 26x trailing earnings and a 5.8% yield, the stock is priced near its peers on earnings but stretched on free cash flow. The next test is whether leverage moves back inside the band and the new assets show up in cash flow per share.
