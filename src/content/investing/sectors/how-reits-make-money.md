A real estate investment trust (REIT) is a landlord you can buy on the TSX. Instead of owning one rental property, unitholders own a slice of hundreds of apartments, warehouses, shopping centres or office towers — and receive a share of the rent as monthly distributions. Whether those distributions are durable depends on **what kind of property** the REIT owns, **how its leases are written** and **how much debt** sits on top.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current yields and company tables, see the living [Canadian REITs deep dive](/sectors/real-estate-reits/). ## Direct answer

REITs make money by collecting rent from tenants, paying property operating costs, and keeping the difference — **net operating income (NOI)**. After interest and overhead, what's left is **funds from operations (FFO)**; after the capital needed to keep buildings leased and maintained, **adjusted FFO (AFFO)** — the best rough measure of distributable cash. Growth comes from two sources: **internal** (higher rents, better occupancy, rent escalators) and **external** (acquisitions and development that earn more than the cost of capital). The main risks are rising interest rates, falling occupancy in weak property types, and leverage.

## Property types

| Property type | Lease length (typical) | What drives rent | Main risk |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Residential (apartments) | ~1 year | Local supply, population, rent rules | Rent control limits, new supply |
| Industrial (warehouses, logistics) | 3–10 years | E-commerce, trade, supply chains | New supply, slowdown in leasing |
| Retail (grocery-anchored, malls) | 5–10+ years | Tenant sales, anchor strength | Weak tenants, store closures |
| Office | 5–10+ years | Employment, return-to-office | Structural vacancy |
| Seniors housing / healthcare | Varies | Demographics, care costs | Operating costs, staffing |
| Diversified | Mix | Mix | Harder to analyze |

Short leases (apartments) let rents adjust quickly in both directions; long leases (retail, office, industrial) lock in income but delay mark-to-market. A REIT with leases rolling well **below** market rents has embedded growth; one with leases **above** market faces rent cuts on renewal.

## Cap rates: why interest rates matter so much

Property values are often estimated as **NOI ÷ capitalization rate**. When interest rates rise, buyers demand higher cap rates, and values fall — even if rent is unchanged. Because REITs use debt, the hit to net asset value (NAV) per unit is amplified:

## External growth: the spread test

Acquisitions and developments only create value per unit if the property's yield beats the REIT's **cost of capital** (a blend of debt cost and the implied cost of issuing units). A REIT whose units trade below NAV usually can't issue equity accretively, so external growth slows — which is why the unit price and the growth plan are linked.

## Metrics to track

| Metric | What it tells you | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Occupancy | Demand for the space | Falling occupancy in a property type |
| Same-property NOI growth | Internal growth | Growth below inflation |
| Renewal spreads (leasing spreads) | Mark-to-market on rents | Negative spreads |
| AFFO per unit and payout | Distribution capacity | Payout above 100% |
| Debt ÷ gross book value, debt ÷ EBITDA | Leverage | Rising leverage, heavy near-term maturities |
| Weighted average interest rate and term | Refinancing risk | Low-rate debt rolling into higher rates |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Interest rates | Falling rates (lower cap rates and interest costs) | Rising rates |
| Supply | Little new construction | Wave of new supply in a market |
| Demand | Population and job growth | Recession, structural shifts (e.g., remote work for office) |
| Capital markets | Units trading above NAV | Units below NAV — growth stalls |
| Policy | Stable rent rules | Rent control changes, tax changes |

## Valuation basics

REITs are usually compared on **price ÷ AFFO per unit**, **price vs NAV**, **implied cap rate** and **distribution yield**, adjusted for property type and leverage. Detailed methods: [valuing REITs: NAV, FFO, AFFO](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/). Distributions often include return of capital, which affects tax and adjusted cost base — another reason not to judge a REIT on yield alone ([why yield alone is a terrible screen](/investing/dividends/why-yield-alone-is-a-terrible-screen/)).
