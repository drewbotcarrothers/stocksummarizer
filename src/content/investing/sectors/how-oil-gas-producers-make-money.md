Oil and gas producers are among the largest companies on the TSX and some of the most volatile. Their share prices often move with oil and gas prices day to day — but over years, what separates winners from laggards is **cost structure, asset quality and capital discipline**. Understanding how a barrel turns into free cash flow makes the sector much less mysterious.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer, not a price forecast. For current company tables and benchmark prices, see the living [Canadian oil & gas producers deep dive](/sectors/oil-gas-producers/). Figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

A producer (the "upstream" part of the energy chain) finds, develops and extracts oil, natural gas and natural gas liquids, then sells them at prices largely set by global and regional markets. Profit per barrel of oil equivalent (boe) is the **netback**: realized price minus royalties, operating costs and transportation. After corporate costs and **sustaining capital** (the spending needed just to hold production flat), what's left is free cash flow for debt reduction, dividends, buybacks and growth. Because prices swing and costs are partly fixed, producers have high **operating leverage** to commodity prices.

## Industry structure in Canada

| Producer type | How it works | Investor implication |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Oil sands — mining | Truck-and-shovel mining of bitumen, upgraded or blended | Very long-life reserves, low decline, high fixed costs |
| Oil sands — in situ (e.g. SAGD) | Steam injected underground to recover bitumen | Long-life, low decline; sensitive to natural gas input costs |
| Conventional and tight oil | Drilled wells, often horizontal with fracturing | Faster payback, but steep declines need continuous drilling |
| Natural gas and liquids (e.g. Montney) | Gas plus condensate and NGLs | Exposure to Canadian gas prices and LNG export growth |
| Integrated producers | Upstream plus refining and marketing | Refining margins can offset weak crude prices |

Many larger Canadian producers are integrated or have long-life oil sands assets; mid-caps are more often conventional oil or gas weighted.

## Prices: benchmarks and Canadian discounts

| Term | Meaning |
|:--|:--|
| WTI | US light crude benchmark, quoted in USD |
| WCS | Western Canadian Select, a heavy-oil blend that trades at a discount to WTI |
| WCS differential | The WTI–WCS gap; wider when export pipeline capacity is tight |
| AECO | Alberta natural gas benchmark, often below US Henry Hub prices |
| CAD/USD | Oil is priced in USD; a weaker loonie raises CAD revenue |

Because Canadian heavy oil and gas sell at regional discounts, pipeline and export capacity matter to producers — the link to [midstream companies](/investing/sectors/how-midstream-pipelines-make-money/).

This works in both directions — and it's why producers publish a **breakeven** (the oil price that covers sustaining capital and the base dividend) and sensitivity tables in their investor materials.

## How producers use the cash

| Priority (typical framework) | Why |
|:--|:--|
| Sustaining capital | Hold production flat against natural decline |
| Base dividend | Set to be affordable at low prices |
| Debt reduction to a target | Resilience through the cycle |
| Variable dividends and buybacks | Distribute excess cash when prices are high |
| Growth projects | Only if returns clear a hurdle rate |

Many Canadian producers adopted a **base-plus-variable** shareholder-return model so the fixed dividend survives downturns. When judging yield, separate the two — see [why yield alone is a terrible screen](/investing/dividends/why-yield-alone-is-a-terrible-screen/).

## Reserves, decline and asset quality

- **Reserves** are disclosed under Canadian rules (NI 51-101): proved (1P) and proved-plus-probable (2P).
- **Reserve life index** = reserves ÷ annual production. Oil sands are measured in decades; shale wells decline fast.
- **Decline rate** — the percentage production would fall with no new drilling. Higher decline means more sustaining capital per barrel.
- **Hedging** — some producers lock in prices for part of output, reducing both downside and upside.

## Valuation basics

Producers are usually compared on **EV/EBITDA** or EV/debt-adjusted cash flow, **free cash flow yield** at a stated oil price, and net asset value based on reserves. Earnings-based P/E can mislead at cycle peaks — see [when P/E misleads](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/) and [EV/EBITDA for Canadian investors](/investing/valuation/ev-ebitda-for-canadian-diy-investors/).
