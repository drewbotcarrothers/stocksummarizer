Pipeline stocks are a staple of Canadian dividend portfolios, prized for steady payouts that often look more like utilities than oil companies. That stability isn't magic — it comes from **how the contracts are written**. Once you understand the contract mix, you can tell which midstream companies are toll roads and which are closer to commodity businesses in disguise.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current yields, valuations and company tables, see the living [Canadian pipelines & midstream deep dive](/sectors/midstream-pipelines/). Figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Midstream companies move, process and store energy between producers and end users: gathering lines, gas processing plants, long-haul oil and gas pipelines, storage, fractionation and export terminals. Most of their revenue comes from **fees** for capacity or throughput, frequently under long-term **take-or-pay** or regulated **cost-of-service** contracts that pay regardless of commodity prices. The more cash flow that is contracted or regulated with creditworthy customers, the more bond-like the business. The rest — volume-based fees, marketing and processing margins — carries more commodity and volume risk.

## Contract types: the core concept

| Contract type | How it pays | Commodity / volume risk | Typical asset |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Take-or-pay | Customer pays for reserved capacity whether used or not | Low (counterparty risk remains) | Long-haul pipelines, export terminals |
| Cost-of-service (regulated) | Tolls set to recover costs plus an allowed return on the rate base | Low; regulatory risk instead | Regulated pipelines, gas utilities |
| Fee-for-service (volume-based) | Fee per unit actually shipped or processed | Medium — volumes can fall | Gathering, processing |
| Commodity-sensitive | Margin on prices or spreads (marketing, NGL frac spreads) | High | Marketing, fractionation |

Federally regulated pipelines in Canada fall under the Canada Energy Regulator; US assets come under US regulators. Regulators can change allowed returns or toll structures, which is the trade-off for low volume risk.

## How the money turns into dividends

Midstream companies often highlight **distributable cash flow (DCF)** — roughly cash from operations minus maintenance capital and preferred dividends — and compare it with dividends paid.

| Line (hypothetical, $M) | Amount |
|:--|--:|
| Adjusted EBITDA | 5,000 |
| − Interest | −1,200 |
| − Cash taxes | −300 |
| − Maintenance capital | −500 |
| − Preferred dividends and other | −200 |
| **Distributable cash flow** | **2,800** |
| Common dividends | 1,900 |
| **DCF payout ratio** | **68%** |

Growth capital — new pipelines and expansions — is usually funded from retained DCF plus debt and sometimes equity. That's why **leverage** (debt ÷ EBITDA) is the other key number. More detail on coverage measures: [free cash flow coverage of dividends](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/).

## Metrics to track

| Metric | What it tells you | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Contracted / regulated share of EBITDA | Cash-flow predictability | Declines as contracts roll off |
| Counterparty quality | Who pays the tolls | A high share of non-investment-grade shippers |
| DCF per share and payout | Dividend capacity | Payout rising toward 100% |
| Debt ÷ EBITDA | Balance-sheet risk | Rising above the company's own target |
| Secured growth backlog | Future EBITDA already contracted | Cost overruns, delays, regulatory setbacks |
| Recontracting schedule | When contracts renew and on what terms | Large renewals in weak markets |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Interest rates | Falling rates (lower financing cost, yield appeal) | Rising rates |
| Volumes | Growing production, LNG and export demand | Producer cutbacks (on volume-based assets) |
| Regulation | Stable, predictable toll decisions | Lower allowed returns, project delays |
| Project execution | On-budget, on-time builds | Cost overruns on megaprojects |
| Commodity prices | Matter less — mostly for the commodity-exposed slice | Weak NGL spreads hit marketing margins |

## Valuation basics

Midstream stocks are commonly compared on **EV/EBITDA**, **price ÷ DCF per share** and **dividend yield** versus their own history and bond yields. A high yield with high leverage and a shrinking contracted share is a warning sign — see [how to avoid dividend traps](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/) and [EV/EBITDA for Canadian investors](/investing/valuation/ev-ebitda-for-canadian-diy-investors/).
