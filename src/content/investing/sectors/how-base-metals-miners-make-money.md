Copper wires the power grid, nickel goes into stainless steel and batteries, zinc galvanizes steel. Canada has a long history in all three — Sudbury's nickel basin, British Columbia's copper mines — and the TSX lists producers of every size. Base metals miners are **price takers**: they can't set the price of what they sell. Their profits come down to **how cheaply they can produce** compared with the market price, and how well they manage the long, risky process of building mines.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current valuations and company tables, see the living [Canadian base metals & mining deep dive](/sectors/base-metals-mining/). All figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Base metals miners dig ore, concentrate it, and usually sell **concentrate** to smelters and refiners, which deduct **treatment and refining charges (TC/RCs)**. Revenue is metal content × market price × payable percentage, plus **by-product credits** (gold, silver, molybdenum, cobalt and others often found alongside the main metal). Profit is the gap between price and cost per pound — measured by **C1 cash cost** and **all-in sustaining cost (AISC)**. Because costs are largely fixed in the short run, earnings swing sharply with metal prices. Long-term value depends on **ore grade, mine life, jurisdiction and project execution**.

## Metrics to track

| Metric | What it tells you | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Production vs guidance | Operational reliability | Repeated guidance cuts |
| Head grade and recovery | Ore quality and processing efficiency | Declining grades as mines age |
| C1 cost and AISC | Position on the industry cost curve | Costs rising faster than peers |
| Reserve life | How long production can last | Short reserve life without replacement |
| Project capex vs original estimate | Execution | Overruns and delays on new mines |
| Net debt ÷ EBITDA at mid-cycle prices | Balance-sheet resilience | Leverage that only looks safe at high prices |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Metal prices | Tight supply, strong demand (grid, construction) | Recession, Chinese demand slowdown, new supply |
| Costs | Lower energy, labour and reagent costs | Cost inflation, declining grades |
| Currency | Weaker C$ (costs in C$, metals priced in US$) | Stronger C$ |
| Smelter terms | Low treatment and refining charges | Rising TC/RCs that shrink payable value |
| Jurisdiction | Stable permitting and tax regimes | Royalty or tax increases, permitting delays, community opposition |

## Valuation basics

Miners are typically compared on **price ÷ net asset value (P/NAV)**, **EV/EBITDA** and **free cash flow yield** at a stated metal price deck. The price assumption drives everything — a "cheap" miner at today's spot price may be expensive at a mid-cycle price. Treat NAVs as ranges, not points ([triangulating multiples, DCF and peer checks](/investing/valuation/triangulating-multiples-dcf-peer-checks/)).
