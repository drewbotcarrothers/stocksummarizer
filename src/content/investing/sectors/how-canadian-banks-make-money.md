Canadian bank stocks are among the most widely held shares in the country, and banks are the single biggest group in the S&P/TSX Composite. Yet many investors who own them couldn't say how a bank actually earns its profit. The business is simpler than it looks — and the risks sit in predictable places.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current numbers, valuations and company-by-company tables, see the living [Canadian banks sector deep dive](/sectors/banks/). All figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

A bank makes money in two main ways: **net interest income** — earning more on loans and securities than it pays on deposits and borrowings — and **non-interest income** — fees and commissions from wealth management, capital markets, card and account fees, and insurance. From that revenue it subtracts operating expenses and **provisions for credit losses** (expected loan losses), then tax. Because banks are highly leveraged, regulators require them to hold minimum **capital**, which limits how fast they can grow and how much they can return to shareholders.

## Industry structure in Canada

| Feature | What it means for investors |
|:--|:--|
| Concentrated: six large banks dominate | Scale, pricing discipline and national branch/digital networks |
| Federally regulated (OSFI) | Strict capital and liquidity rules; the largest are designated domestic systemically important banks |
| Deposit insurance (CDIC) on eligible deposits | Sticky, low-cost funding |
| Diversified "universal" model | Retail, commercial, wealth, insurance and capital markets under one roof |
| Big mortgage books, much of it insured or low loan-to-value | Housing is a key exposure, cushioned by insurance and equity |
| US and international arms at several banks | Growth outside Canada — plus different competition and regulation |

Smaller lenders, credit unions and alternative mortgage lenders exist, but the large banks set the tone for the sector on the TSX.

## The main business lines

| Segment | How it earns | Sensitive to |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Personal & commercial banking | Loan-deposit spread, account and card fees | Rates, housing, employment, business credit |
| Wealth management | Fees on assets under management, advice, brokerage | Market levels, client flows |
| Insurance | Premiums and investment income | Claims, rates |
| Capital markets | Trading, underwriting, advisory, lending to large companies | Market volatility, deal activity |
| US / international banking | Same spread-and-fee model in other markets | Local economies, competition, FX |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Usually helps | Usually hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Interest rates | A steeper yield curve, stable deposits | Inverted curve, deposit competition |
| Economy | Low unemployment, loan demand | Recession — credit losses rise |
| Housing | Stable prices and steady renewals | Sharp price drops with high unemployment |
| Markets | Rising markets lift wealth fees; active deal markets | Prolonged bear markets |
| Regulation | Stable rules | Higher capital requirements reduce buybacks |

## Why capital matters

A bank funds most of its assets with deposits and debt, not shareholders' equity. A small percentage loss on loans can therefore wipe out a large share of equity. Regulators require a minimum **Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1)** ratio — equity relative to risk-weighted assets. Capital above the requirement can fund growth, dividends and buybacks; a shortfall forces the opposite. The metrics to track are covered in [bank operating metrics DIY investors should track](/investing/sectors/bank-operating-metrics-to-track/).

## How investors value banks

Because a bank's "product" is money, investors lean on **price-to-book (P/B)** and **return on equity (ROE)** rather than EV/EBITDA. A bank earning a higher sustainable ROE deserves a higher P/B. Full method: [valuation for banks: P/B, ROE, and why P/E alone fails](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/). On dividends, earnings payout ratio plus capital ratios replace FCF coverage — see [payout ratio explained](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/).
