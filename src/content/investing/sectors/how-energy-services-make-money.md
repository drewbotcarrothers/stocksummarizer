Oil and gas producers sell barrels and gas; **energy services companies sell the work that gets them out of the ground** — drilling rigs, fracturing crews, compression equipment, rentals and engineering. Their customers are producers, so their revenue depends on how much producers decide to spend. That makes services one of the most cyclical corners of the TSX: when producer budgets rise, services profits can rise much faster; when budgets are cut, the reverse happens.

This is an evergreen concept explainer; for producer economics see [energy – producers: how money is made](/investing/sectors/how-oil-gas-producers-make-money/) and the living [oil & gas producers deep dive](/sectors/oil-gas-producers/). All figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Energy services companies earn fees for equipment and crews: **day rates** for drilling rigs, **per-job or per-stage pricing** for completions and pressure pumping, **contract or rental fees** for compression and processing equipment, and project revenue for fabrication and engineering. Revenue roughly equals **activity × utilization × price**. Because rigs, fleets and staff are largely fixed costs, small changes in utilization and pricing drive large changes in profit — **operating leverage**. The key risks are the commodity cycle (through producer capex), overcapacity in the service fleet, customer concentration, and the cost of keeping equipment modern.

## The main sub-segments

| Sub-segment | How it earns | Contract style | Key risk |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Contract drilling | Day rate per rig in operation | Spot or term contracts | Rig utilization, day-rate pressure |
| Completions / pressure pumping | Price per job or fracturing stage | Mostly short-term | Fleet oversupply, intense competition |
| Compression and processing equipment | Equipment sales, rentals, contract operations | Multi-year contracts possible | Project timing, international exposure |
| Rentals and oilfield services | Daily or per-job rentals, wellsite services | Short-term | Activity swings |
| Engineering, fabrication, maintenance | Project and service contracts | Project-based | Cost overruns, backlog gaps |

Contract-based compression and long-term drilling contracts tend to be steadier than spot fracturing work. A company whose revenue comes mainly from recurring contracts can look more like an industrial than a pure cyclical.

## Operating leverage in numbers

Assume a hypothetical driller with **100 rigs**, a **day rate of $30,000** and **$500M of largely fixed annual costs** (crews on standby, maintenance, overhead):

| Utilization (hypothetical) | Rig-days | Revenue | Fixed + variable costs | Operating profit |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 40% | 14,600 | $438M | $500M + $146M | **−$208M** |
| 60% | 21,900 | $657M | $500M + $219M | **−$62M** |
| 80% | 29,200 | $876M | $500M + $292M | **$84M** |

Variable cost assumed at $10,000 per operating rig-day. Moving from 60% to 80% utilization lifts revenue by a third but swings operating profit from a loss to a profit. Day rates typically rise only once utilization is high, which adds a second layer of leverage.

## Metrics to track

| Metric | What it tells you | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Rig utilization and active rig count | Activity level | Falling utilization ahead of budget season |
| Day rates / pricing per job | Pricing power | Discounts to keep equipment busy |
| Contract backlog and term coverage | Visibility of future revenue | Shrinking backlog |
| Maintenance vs growth capex | Cost of keeping the fleet competitive | Heavy reactivation or upgrade spending |
| Customer concentration | Exposure to a few producers | One customer cutting its program |
| Net debt ÷ EBITDA | Ability to survive downturns | Leverage that looks fine only at peak EBITDA |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Commodity prices | Stable or rising oil and gas prices | Price collapses that trigger budget cuts |
| Producer discipline | Steady multi-year programs | Producers prioritizing buybacks over drilling |
| Industry capacity | Fleet attrition, few new rigs | Oversupplied fleets, price wars |
| Infrastructure | New pipelines and LNG export capacity | Takeaway constraints that cap production |
| Technology | High-spec rigs and fleets earning premium rates | Older equipment becoming uncompetitive |

## Valuation basics

Services stocks are usually compared on **EV/EBITDA** and **free cash flow yield** — but at cycle peaks, EBITDA is flattering and the multiple looks cheap. Ask what earnings look like at mid-cycle utilization, a version of the trap described in [when P/E misleads on TSX stocks](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/). Balance sheets matter more than usual: the survivors of a downturn are the ones that didn't over-borrow at the peak.
