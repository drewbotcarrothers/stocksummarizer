"Tech" on the TSX is not one business. A subscription software company, a serial acquirer of niche software firms, an IT consulting giant and a contract electronics manufacturer all sit in the same sector — yet they earn money in very different ways, with very different margins and risks. Before comparing any two Canadian tech stocks, identify **which model** you're looking at.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current valuations and company tables, see the living [Canadian software & tech deep dive](/sectors/software-tech/). All figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Canadian information technology companies mostly fall into five models: **subscription software (SaaS)**, **vertical-market software acquirers**, **IT services and consulting**, **hardware and electronics manufacturing services (EMS)**, and **commerce or payments platforms**. Software earns high gross margins on recurring revenue, so the key questions are growth, retention and how much of the profit is consumed by stock-based compensation. Services and EMS earn thinner margins on contracts and volumes, so execution, utilization and customer concentration matter more. Valuation multiples only make sense when compared within the same model.

## The five models

| Model | How it earns | Margin profile | Key risk |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Subscription software (SaaS) | Recurring subscriptions, usage fees | High gross, variable operating | Slowing growth, churn |
| Vertical-market software acquirers | Buy and hold many niche software firms; maintenance revenue | High, steady cash generation | Running out of attractive acquisitions |
| IT services / consulting | Contracts, managed services, project work | Lower, stable | Utilization, pricing, contract renewals |
| Hardware / EMS | Manufacturing for other companies | Thin | Customer concentration, cycles |
| Commerce / payments platforms | Subscriptions plus a share of merchant sales | Medium | Consumer spending, take-rate pressure |

| ARR growth | (121 − 100) ÷ 100 | 21% |
| Net revenue retention (NRR) | (100 + 12 − 6 − 3) ÷ 100 | 103% |
| Gross revenue retention | (100 − 6 − 3) ÷ 100 | 91% |

NRR above 100% means the existing customer base grows on its own. Definitions vary between companies, so read how each one defines ARR and retention before comparing.

## Profitability: Rule of 40 and stock-based compensation

A common software shortcut is the **Rule of 40**: revenue growth % + free cash flow margin % should be around 40 or more. It's a rough screen, not a law.

| Company (hypothetical) | Revenue growth | FCF margin | Rule of 40 score | SBC as % of revenue |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Fast grower | 35% | 5% | 40 | 15% |
| Balanced | 18% | 22% | 40 | 6% |
| Mature | 6% | 30% | 36 | 2% |

The last column matters. **Stock-based compensation (SBC)** is a real cost paid in shares rather than cash; it boosts FCF and adjusted earnings while diluting shareholders. A company whose FCF margin roughly equals its SBC margin isn't generating much cash for owners. See [reading non-GAAP metrics critically](/investing/analyze/read-non-gaap-metrics-critically/).

## Metrics to track by model

| Model | Metrics | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| SaaS | ARR growth, NRR, gross margin, SBC, FCF margin | Decelerating NRR, rising SBC |
| Vertical software acquirers | Organic growth, capital deployed, return on invested capital | Higher prices paid for deals, negative organic growth |
| IT services | Bookings, backlog, book-to-bill, margin | Book-to-bill below 1 for several quarters |
| EMS / hardware | Revenue by end market, top-customer share, inventory | One customer dominating revenue |
| Commerce / payments | Gross merchandise volume, take rate, merchant growth | Falling take rate |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Interest rates | Falling rates (long-duration cash flows worth more) | Rising rates compress growth multiples |
| Enterprise budgets | Strong IT spending | Budget freezes, longer sales cycles |
| Technology shifts | New demand (AI infrastructure, cloud migration) | Disruption of legacy products |
| Currency | Weaker C$ (many report or earn in US$) | Stronger C$ |
| M&A market | Reasonable prices for acquirers | Competition from private equity for deals |

## Valuation basics

High-growth software is often valued on **EV/sales** or **EV/gross profit**; mature software and acquirers on **P/FCF** or **EV/EBITDA**; IT services and EMS on **P/E** and **EV/EBITDA** like other industrials. Mixing them up is the most common error. Use [P/FCF](/investing/valuation/what-p-fcf-means-canadian-diy/), a [simple DCF](/investing/valuation/simple-dcf-without-fake-precision/), and always check SBC-adjusted cash flow.
