Railroads are some of the most durable businesses on the TSX. Canada has two Class I railroads — **Canadian National (CN)** and **Canadian Pacific Kansas City (CPKC)** — and building a competing transcontinental network today would be close to impossible. That scarcity gives rails pricing power. But volumes still swing with the economy, harvests, trade policy and labour relations, so understanding *what* the trains carry and *how efficiently* they run is the core of any rail thesis.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current valuations and company tables, see the living [Canadian transportation deep dive](/sectors/transportation/). All figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Railroads earn revenue by moving freight — grain, fertilizers, energy products, chemicals, forest products, metals, automobiles and intermodal containers — and charging per carload or container, roughly **volume (revenue ton-miles) × price (revenue per ton-mile)**, plus fuel surcharges. Costs are heavily fixed (track, locomotives, crews, yards), so a railroad with steady pricing and rising volumes gets strong **operating leverage**. Investors judge efficiency with the **operating ratio** (operating expenses ÷ revenue — lower is better) and judge durability by the commodity mix, pricing above inflation and capital discipline.

Lower is better, but compare like with like: fuel prices, accounting for land sales, merger costs and depreciation policies all move the ratio. Many railroads report an "adjusted" operating ratio — read the reconciliation, as you would with any [non-GAAP metric](/investing/analyze/read-non-gaap-metrics-critically/).

## Metrics to track

| Metric | What it tells you | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Revenue ton-miles (RTMs) and carloads | Volume trends by commodity | Broad declines across groups |
| Revenue per RTM (ex-fuel) | Pricing power | Pricing below cost inflation |
| Operating ratio (reported and adjusted) | Efficiency | Rising ratio without a one-off explanation |
| Train speed, terminal dwell | Network fluidity and service | Congestion after weather or volume surges |
| Capex as % of revenue | Reinvestment needs | Spending rising faster than revenue |
| Free cash flow and buybacks | Capital returned to shareholders | Buybacks funded with rising debt |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Economy and trade | Strong imports, exports and harvests | Recession, tariffs, weak crops |
| Pricing | Rate increases above inflation | Competitive or regulatory pressure on rates |
| Fuel | Surcharges that keep pace with prices | Surcharge lag when diesel spikes |
| Labour | Stable agreements | Strikes or lockouts that halt the network |
| Weather | Mild winters, no floods or wildfires | Severe winters, washouts, wildfire closures |

## Valuation basics

Rails usually trade on **P/E**, **EV/EBITDA** and **free cash flow yield**, often at a premium to the market because of their moats. The key question is whether the premium is justified by pricing power and volume growth — see [when P/E misleads](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/) and [how to compare a TSX stock to its US peer](/investing/valuation/compare-tsx-stock-to-us-peer/) (US Class I railroads are the natural comparison).
