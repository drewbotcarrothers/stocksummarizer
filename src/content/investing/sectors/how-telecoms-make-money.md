Canadian telecom stocks were long treated as the safest dividend payers on the TSX: few competitors, monthly bills, sticky customers. The business is still recurring, but it is also **extremely capital-hungry** — networks, spectrum and fibre have to be paid for before a dollar reaches shareholders. Understanding the gap between EBITDA and free cash flow is the key to reading the sector.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current yields, valuations and company tables, see the living [Canadian telecom deep dive](/sectors/telecom/). All figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Telecoms earn recurring monthly revenue from **wireless** plans, **wireline** services (home internet, TV, home phone, business connectivity) and, for some companies, **media** assets. Revenue is roughly subscribers × average revenue per user, minus what leaks out through churn and price competition. Costs are front-loaded: carriers must keep spending on network upgrades, fibre builds and spectrum licences. So the number that matters for dividends is not EBITDA but **free cash flow after capex, interest and lease payments** — and how much debt the company carries while funding it.

## The segments

| Segment | How it earns | Key metric | Typical risk |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Wireless | Monthly plans, device financing, roaming | Postpaid net adds, ARPU, churn | Price competition, regulation |
| Wireline – internet | Monthly broadband subscriptions | Net adds, ARPU, fibre penetration | Fibre overbuild, wholesale access rules |
| Wireline – TV / home phone | Legacy bundles | Subscriber losses | Structural decline (cord-cutting) |
| Business / enterprise | Connectivity, data centres, managed services | Contract wins, margins | Slow growth, competition |
| Media (some companies) | Advertising, subscriptions, sports rights | Ad revenue, rights costs | Cyclical ads, expensive rights |

Wireless and broadband usually carry the economics; legacy TV and home phone tend to shrink over time. When a company says revenue is "flat," check whether growth in wireless and internet is offsetting decline elsewhere.

## Regulation and spectrum

Telecom is a regulated industry in Canada. The **CRTC** sets rules on matters such as wholesale access and consumer protections, and **Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED)** licenses spectrum, often through auctions. Spectrum is a large, lumpy cost — and a recurring one, because each new wireless generation needs more of it. Policy choices aimed at more competition or lower prices can cap pricing power, so regulation is a standing risk in any telecom thesis.

Falling capital intensity after a build program is one of the main ways telecom free cash flow can grow even if revenue is flat. The risk is that the "peak capex" year keeps moving out.

## Metrics to track

| Metric | What it tells you | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Postpaid wireless net adds | Customer momentum | Falling adds despite promotions |
| ARPU / ARPA | Pricing power | Declines as competitors cut prices |
| Churn | Customer stickiness | Rising churn after price changes |
| Capital intensity | Investment load | Guidance that never declines |
| FCF and dividend ÷ FCF | Real dividend capacity | Payout above 100% for years |
| Net debt ÷ EBITDA | Leverage and ratings risk | Rising leverage after spectrum or acquisitions |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Competition | Rational pricing, few national players | Price wars, aggressive new entrants |
| Regulation | Stable rules on wholesale and spectrum | Mandated access, price-focused policy |
| Interest rates | Falling rates (lower refinancing cost) | Rising rates on a leveraged balance sheet |
| Immigration and population | Strong subscriber growth | Slower population growth |
| Capex cycle | Build programs ending | New spectrum auctions, network refreshes |

## Valuation basics

Telecoms are usually compared on **EV/EBITDA**, **price ÷ FCF per share**, **dividend yield** and **leverage**. Because capex is so large, EV/EBITDA can flatter a capital-hungry company — always cross-check [P/FCF](/investing/valuation/what-p-fcf-means-canadian-diy/). A high yield with a payout above FCF is a classic setup for a [dividend trap](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/).
