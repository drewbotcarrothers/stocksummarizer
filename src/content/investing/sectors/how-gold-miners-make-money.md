Canada is home to some of the world's largest gold companies, and gold stocks make up a meaningful slice of the TSX. They can rise much faster than gold itself — and fall much faster. The reason is the business model: a mine has large, partly fixed costs, so small changes in the gold price create big changes in profit.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer, not a gold-price forecast. For current company tables and multiples, see the living [Canadian gold & precious metals deep dive](/sectors/gold-precious-metals/). Figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

A gold miner earns the difference between the gold price and its **all-in sustaining cost (AISC)** per ounce — on-site mining and processing costs, royalties and production taxes, sustaining capital, and corporate overhead. Because costs don't move one-for-one with gold, margins expand sharply when gold rises and shrink fast when it falls. Not every gold stock is a miner: **royalty and streaming** companies finance mines in exchange for a share of output and avoid most operating costs, while **developers** and **explorers** have no production yet and trade on future potential.

## Industry structure: four business models

| Model | How it makes money | Main risks | Typical valuation lens |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Senior / mid-tier producers | Sell gold from operating mines | Costs, grade, mine problems, jurisdiction | EV/EBITDA, P/CF, P/NAV, FCF yield |
| Royalty & streaming | Buy a share of revenue or metal at a fixed low price | Partner mine performance, deal pricing | Premium P/NAV, P/CF |
| Developers | Building a mine — no revenue yet | Financing, construction overruns, permitting | Discount to NAV that narrows as risk falls |
| Explorers | Drilling to find and define deposits | Drill results, dilution from share issues | Ounces in the ground, speculative |

AISC was standardized by industry guidance from the World Gold Council, but companies still differ in what they include. AISC **excludes** growth capital, exploration for new deposits, interest and income taxes — so free cash flow is lower than the AISC margin suggests. By-product credits (copper, silver) can lower reported costs.

Costs aren't truly fixed — energy, labour and royalties often rise with gold — but the leverage is real. That's also why gold stocks can lag gold when cost inflation runs hot.

## What separates good mines from bad

| Factor | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| Grade (grams per tonne) | Higher grade means more gold per tonne moved and lower cost per ounce |
| Recovery rate | Share of gold actually extracted in processing |
| Open pit vs underground | Open pits move more tonnes; underground costs more per tonne but often higher grade |
| Strip ratio (open pit) | Waste rock moved per tonne of ore — higher raises costs |
| Mine life and reserves | How long cash flow lasts before replacement is needed |
| Jurisdiction | Permitting, taxes, political and security risk |
| Reserve replacement | Miners deplete their asset every year; they must find or buy more |

## Valuation basics

Gold producers are typically compared on **price to net asset value (P/NAV)** at a stated gold price, **EV/EBITDA**, **price to cash flow** and **free cash flow yield**. Royalty companies usually trade at premium multiples for their lower cost exposure. Earnings-based P/E swings wildly with gold — see [when P/E misleads](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/) and [relative vs intrinsic valuation](/investing/valuation/relative-vs-intrinsic-valuation/).
