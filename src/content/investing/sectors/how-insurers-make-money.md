Insurance is one of the strangest businesses you can own: customers pay you **first**, and you find out what your product actually cost **later** — sometimes decades later. That timing gap creates two profit engines (underwriting and investing) and two ways to get hurt (mispriced risk and bad investments). Canada has a large, mature insurance sector on the TSX, split between **life insurers** and **property & casualty (P&C) insurers**, and they behave very differently.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current valuations and company tables, see the living [Canadian insurance deep dive](/sectors/insurers/). All figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Insurers collect premiums, set aside reserves for expected claims, and invest the money they hold in the meantime (the **float**). **P&C insurers** (home, auto, commercial) earn an **underwriting profit** when claims plus expenses are less than premiums — measured by the **combined ratio**, where below 100% is profitable — plus investment income on the float. **Life insurers** earn from insurance margins on long-dated policies, investment spreads and, increasingly, **asset and wealth management fees**. The key risks are catastrophe losses and claims inflation for P&C, and interest rates, credit losses and longevity/mortality assumptions for life.

## Life vs P&C at a glance

| Feature | Life insurers | P&C insurers |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Products | Life, health, annuities, group benefits, wealth/asset management | Home, auto, commercial, specialty lines |
| Policy length | Years to decades | Mostly one year, renewed |
| Main profit sources | Insurance margins, investment spread, fee income | Underwriting profit + investment income |
| Big sensitivities | Interest rates, credit spreads, equity markets, longevity | Catastrophes, claims inflation, pricing cycle |
| Federal capital test (OSFI) | LICAT ratio | MCT ratio |
| Key reporting concept | Contractual service margin (CSM) under IFRS 17 | Combined ratio |

Two things to check: whether the insurer **reprices** quickly after bad years (P&C pricing tends to move in "hard" and "soft" market cycles), and whether **prior-year reserve development** is favourable (reserves were conservative) or adverse (they were too low).

## Life: margins, spreads and the CSM

Under **IFRS 17** (adopted by Canadian insurers in 2023), life insurers record a **contractual service margin (CSM)** — the unearned profit on in-force policies — and release it into earnings over the life of the contracts. A growing CSM from profitable new business is a sign of future earnings capacity. Life insurers also earn an investment spread (portfolio yield minus credited rates) and fees from asset management, which makes many of them partly wealth-management businesses.

## Metrics to track

| Metric | Applies to | What it tells you | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Combined ratio (incl. and ex-cat) | P&C | Underwriting discipline | Rising ex-cat ratio |
| Prior-year reserve development | P&C | Reserve conservatism | Repeated adverse development |
| New business CSM, CSM growth | Life | Future earnings capacity | Shrinking CSM |
| Core EPS and core ROE | Both | Underlying profitability | Large gaps between reported and core |
| LICAT / MCT ratio | Life / P&C | Capital strength vs OSFI requirements | Ratios trending toward supervisory targets |
| Book value per share growth | Both | Compounding of owner capital | Flat book value over several years |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Interest rates | Higher rates lift investment income (gradually) | Sharp moves can hurt bond values and some product economics |
| Catastrophes | Quiet seasons | Wildfires, floods, hail, storms |
| Inflation | Moderate | Claims inflation (car parts, rebuild costs) outpacing pricing |
| Credit markets | Stable spreads | Defaults in corporate or real estate loan portfolios |
| Equity markets | Rising markets lift fee income | Falling markets cut wealth-management fees |

## Valuation basics

Insurers are usually compared on **price-to-book (P/B)** versus **ROE**, and on **P/E** using core earnings — similar to the logic in [valuing Canadian banks with P/B and ROE](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/). A P&C insurer that sustains a mid-90s combined ratio and grows book value steadily usually deserves a higher P/B than one with volatile results. Check that "core" earnings exclude only genuinely unusual items — see [reading non-GAAP metrics critically](/investing/analyze/read-non-gaap-metrics-critically/).
