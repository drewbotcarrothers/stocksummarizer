Utility stocks are often described as "bond proxies" — slow, steady, dividend-paying. That reputation comes from a specific business model: in most of their operations, utilities don't set their own prices. A **regulator** decides what they can charge, based on how much capital they've invested and a fair return on it. Once you understand that formula, you can see where utility earnings growth actually comes from — and where the risks hide.

Educational only — not financial advice. This is an evergreen concept explainer; for current yields, valuations and company tables, see the living [Canadian utilities deep dive](/sectors/utilities/). All figures here are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

A regulated utility earns money by investing capital in poles, wires, pipes, substations and plants (the **rate base**), and then charging customers rates that a regulator approves to recover operating costs, depreciation, interest and taxes **plus an allowed return on the equity** portion of that rate base. Earnings grow mainly when the rate base grows. The risks are regulatory (lower allowed returns, disallowed costs, delays), interest rates (financing costs and valuation), and execution on large capital programs. Unregulated parts — merchant or contracted power, energy marketing — behave differently and deserve separate scrutiny.

In Canada, provincial regulators — for example the Ontario Energy Board, the Alberta Utilities Commission or the Régie de l'énergie in Quebec — oversee most distribution and many transmission utilities. Canadian utility holding companies also own large US utilities, where state commissions (and federal regulators for some transmission) play the same role. Each jurisdiction has its own rules, which is why "utility" isn't a single risk profile.

## The revenue requirement, step by step

Assume a hypothetical utility with a **$6.0B rate base**, a deemed capital structure of **40% equity / 60% debt**, an **allowed ROE of 9%**, and a 5% cost of debt:

- Deemed equity = $6.0B × 40% = $2.4B → allowed equity return = $2.4B × 9% = **$216M**
- Deemed debt = $6.0B × 60% = $3.6B → interest = $3.6B × 5% = **$180M**

Two implications follow. First, **earnings track the rate base**: if the rate base grows 6% a year and the allowed ROE and equity thickness hold steady, regulated earnings tend to grow at a similar pace (before share dilution). Second, **utilities can't simply "cut costs to grow"** the way an unregulated company can — savings are often shared with customers at the next rate case.

## Types of utility businesses

| Business type | How it earns | Main risk |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Electric distribution | Regulated return on local wires | Rate decisions, storm costs |
| Electric transmission | Regulated return on high-voltage lines | Project approvals, cost overruns |
| Gas distribution | Regulated return on local pipes | Long-term demand policy (electrification) |
| Contracted generation | Long-term power purchase agreements | Recontracting, counterparty, resource variability |
| Merchant generation | Market power prices | Price swings — behaves more like a commodity business |

The more of a company's earnings that come from the top three rows, the more predictable it tends to be. A utility with a large merchant slice can look cheap on yield for a reason.

## Regulatory lag and what earned ROE tells you

The allowed ROE is a ceiling-ish target, not a guarantee. If costs rise faster than rates (because rate cases are infrequent or a regulator disallows some spending), the utility **under-earns**. If it controls costs within an approved multi-year framework, it may **over-earn** for a while. Comparing **earned ROE vs allowed ROE** by jurisdiction is one of the best ways to judge management and regulatory quality.

## Metrics to track

| Metric | What it tells you | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Rate base growth (5-year plan) | The earnings growth engine | Plans that rely on unapproved projects |
| Allowed vs earned ROE | Regulatory quality and execution | Persistent under-earning |
| Equity thickness | How much of the rate base earns the equity return | Regulator reducing deemed equity |
| Regulated share of earnings | Predictability | Rising merchant or marketing exposure |
| FFO ÷ debt and credit ratings | Balance-sheet capacity to fund capex | Downgrades, heavy reliance on new equity |
| Dividend payout ratio | Room to keep raising | Payout near or above earnings |

## What helps and what hurts

| Driver | Helps | Hurts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Interest rates | Falling rates (cheaper debt, yield appeal) | Rising rates, especially between rate cases |
| Regulation | Constructive rate decisions, formula-based ROEs | Disallowances, lower allowed ROE or equity thickness |
| Capital plan | Approved, well-executed rate base growth | Overruns, large equity issues that dilute per-share growth |
| Weather and storms | Mild years, cost-recovery mechanisms | Severe storms, wildfire liability where rules are unclear |
| Policy | Grid investment, electrification demand | Affordability pressure limiting rate increases |

## Valuation basics

Utilities are usually compared on **P/E**, **EV/EBITDA**, **dividend yield** and **price relative to rate base**, always alongside rate base growth and leverage. A cheap multiple can reflect a tough regulator, a big merchant slice or a stretched balance sheet. Because utility dividends depend on steady access to capital, check the payout and funding plan — see [payout ratio explained](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/) and [dividend growth vs high yield](/investing/dividends/dividend-growth-vs-high-yield/).
