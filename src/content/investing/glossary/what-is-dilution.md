**Dilution** is what happens to your slice of a company when it issues more shares. You own the same number of shares, but each one is a smaller piece of the business — and earnings, cash flow and dividends are split across more shares.

Educational glossary entry — not a recommendation. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Dilution is the reduction in existing shareholders' ownership percentage (and often in per-share earnings) caused by new shares being issued — through equity raises, stock-based pay, convertible securities, warrants, or share-paid acquisitions. Measure it as **new shares ÷ existing shares**. Dilution isn't automatically bad: if the company earns more on the new money than the cost of the shares it issued, per-share value can still rise ("accretive"). The test is always **per share**, not total.

## Where dilution comes from

| Source | How it shows up | Typical size | Where to find it |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Equity raise (bought deal, marketed offering, private placement) | Lump of new shares, often at a discount | Can be 5–20%+ in one go | News release, prospectus supplement on SEDAR+ |
| Stock options, RSUs, PSUs | Slow annual creep | Often 0.5–3% a year | Notes to financial statements; management information circular |
| Convertible debentures / preferreds | New shares if converted | Depends on conversion price | Notes; diluted EPS calculation |
| Warrants | New shares if exercised | Common in small caps and miners | Notes; financing news releases |
| Share-paid acquisitions | Target holders receive your company's shares | Can be large | Deal news release, circular |
| Dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) issued from treasury | Small ongoing issuance, sometimes at a discount | 1–3% a year at some REITs and utilities | Distribution/DRIP disclosure |

## Basic vs diluted EPS

Companies report two earnings per share figures under IFRS (IAS 33). **Basic EPS** uses the weighted average shares actually outstanding. **Diluted EPS** adds shares that would exist if in-the-money options, warrants and convertibles were exercised or converted (options and warrants use the treasury stock method). A wide gap between the two tells you a lot of potential dilution is sitting in the capital structure.

| Hypothetical | Shares | Net income | EPS |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Basic | 100M | $200M | $2.00 |
| Diluted (options + convertible add 8M) | 108M | $200M* | $1.85 |

*Convertible interest saved is added back to net income in a real diluted EPS calculation; ignored here for simplicity.

| Question | Reassuring answer | Warning sign |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Why raise? | Funding a specific, high-return project or deal | Paying down a debt crisis, or no clear use |
| How big? | Small relative to shares outstanding | 20%+, or repeated raises every year |
| At what price? | Near market price | Deep discount, plus warrants sweeteners |
| Per-share math | FFO/EPS/cash flow per share rises after the deal | Totals rise but per-share figures fall |
| Insider participation | Insiders buying in the offering | Insiders selling alongside |
