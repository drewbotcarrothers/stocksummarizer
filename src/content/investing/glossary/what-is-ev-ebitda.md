**EV/EBITDA** compares a company’s **enterprise value (EV)** to its **EBITDA** — a capital-structure-aware multiple used heavily for industrials, telecoms, and many leveraged TSX names.

Educational glossary entry — not a recommendation.

## Direct answer

EV/EBITDA asks: how many years of approximate operating cash earnings (before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation) would it take to “pay for” the whole firm at today’s enterprise value? Lower is not automatically better — growth, cyclicality, and capex intensity matter.

## Pieces of the ratio

**Enterprise value (simplified):** equity market cap + interest-bearing debt − cash (plus adjustments for minorities, leases, etc. when you get precise).

**EBITDA:** earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation — a rough operating profit proxy. Often shown as “adjusted EBITDA” in MD&A; always read the reconciliation.

## When DIY Canadians use it

- Comparing peers with **different debt levels** (P/E alone can mislead).
- Telecom, cable, midstream, and industrial conversations where leverage is normal.
- Cross-checking a P/E story that looks cheap only because of one-time earnings noise.

## When it misleads

- Capex-heavy businesses where EBITDA ignores sustaining investment (pair with FCF).
- Financials (banks/insurers) where EV/EBITDA is usually the wrong primary lens.
- Early-stage miners or software names with negative EBITDA.
- Aggressive “adjustments” that restore EBITDA by excluding recurring costs.

## Practical DIY habit

1. Take EV and EBITDA from the same date/source discipline.
2. Compare to 3–5 true peers.
3. Ask what capex and working capital the EBITDA number is silent about.
4. Cross-check with [FCF](/investing/glossary/what-is-free-cash-flow/) and sector-native metrics.
