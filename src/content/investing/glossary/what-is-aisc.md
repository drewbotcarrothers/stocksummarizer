**All-in sustaining cost (AISC)** is the gold industry's attempt at one honest number for "what it costs to keep producing an ounce." It goes beyond mine-site cash costs by adding sustaining capital, corporate overhead and other recurring costs. It is the most quoted cost metric in gold-miner results — and a **non-GAAP** one.

Educational glossary entry — not a recommendation. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

AISC per ounce = (mine-site cash costs net of by-product credits + royalties and production taxes + sustaining capital + corporate general and administrative costs + sustaining exploration and study costs + reclamation for operating sites) ÷ ounces sold. The framework comes from the **World Gold Council**'s guidance note (first published in 2013, updated in 2018). AISC **excludes** growth capital, new-project exploration, interest, income taxes and dividends, so a miner's free cash flow is lower than "gold price minus AISC" suggests.

## What's in, what's out

| Included in AISC | Excluded from AISC (captured elsewhere) |
|:--|:--|
| Mining, processing and site admin costs | Growth / expansion capital for new mines or major expansions |
| By-product credits (silver, copper) — **subtracted** | Exploration and studies for new projects |
| Royalties and production taxes | Interest and other financing costs |
| Sustaining capital (equipment, waste stripping, tailings lifts) | Income and mining taxes |
| Corporate G&A | Dividends and buybacks |
| Sustaining exploration and studies at operating mines | Reclamation for closed sites (in "all-in costs") |
| Reclamation and remediation accretion for operating sites | Working-capital swings |

The WGC's broader measure, **all-in costs (AIC)**, adds the non-sustaining items such as growth capital. Few investors quote it, but it's closer to the full cash picture.

A 20% fall in the gold price hits the high-cost producer's margin far harder. That's why AISC (and its trend) sits at the centre of any gold-miner comparison.

## How to read AISC critically

- **Check the by-product credits.** A mine with lots of copper or silver can report a low AISC because those sales are subtracted. If copper prices fall, AISC rises.
- **Look at the sustaining vs growth capital split.** Companies decide what counts as "growth"; a rising share labelled growth can flatter AISC.
- **Compare guidance with actuals** over several years — consistent overruns are a signal.
- **Per ounce sold vs produced** — check which denominator the company uses.
- **Reconcile to the statements.** Canadian issuers must reconcile non-GAAP measures under NI 52-112 — see [how to read non-GAAP metrics critically](/investing/analyze/read-non-gaap-metrics-critically/).
