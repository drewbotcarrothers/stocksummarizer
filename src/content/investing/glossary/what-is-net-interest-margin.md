**Net interest margin (NIM)** measures how much a bank earns on its lending and investments after paying for the money it borrows. For Canadian bank investors it's one of the three or four numbers that move earnings most — along with loan growth, credit losses and expenses.

Educational glossary entry — not a recommendation. Worked examples are **hypothetical**; the RBC figure is from its 2025 annual report.

## Direct answer

NIM = net interest income ÷ average interest-earning assets, expressed as a percentage. Net interest income is interest earned on loans and securities minus interest paid on deposits and other funding. If a bank earns $16 billion of net interest income on $1,000 billion of average earning assets, its NIM is 1.6%. Small moves matter: on that balance sheet, a 0.05-point (5 basis point) change in NIM is $500 million of annual revenue. For scale, Royal Bank of Canada reported an all-bank NIM of **1.62%** on average earning assets for fiscal 2025.

## What moves NIM

| Driver | Pushes NIM up | Pushes NIM down |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Policy rates | Rising rates when assets reprice faster than deposits | Falling rates when loan yields reset down faster than deposit costs |
| Deposit mix | More chequing and low-rate savings deposits | Shift to GICs and high-interest savings |
| Asset mix | More credit cards and business loans | More low-spread mortgages and liquid securities |
| Mortgage renewals | Maturing low-rate mortgages renewing at higher rates | Renewing into lower rates |
| Competition | Rational pricing | Deposit or mortgage price wars |
| Hedging | Gains as hedges roll | Hedges locking in older rates |

## Reading NIM in Canadian bank results

| Check | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| **Which NIM?** All-bank vs a segment (e.g., Canadian personal banking) | Segment NIMs are usually much higher than the all-bank figure, which includes low-spread trading and treasury assets |
| **Definition** | Banks differ: some use average earning assets, some exclude trading assets; compare a bank against its own history first |
| **Basis points, not percent** | A "4 bp" move sounds tiny but can be hundreds of millions of dollars |
| **Volume × margin** | Net interest income growth = asset growth and NIM change; one can hide the other |
| **Management's outlook** | Look for NIM guidance and rate-sensitivity tables in the MD&A |

NIM isn't profit. A bank can widen NIM by lending to riskier borrowers and then give it back in **provisions for credit losses**. Read NIM together with credit and efficiency — see [bank operating metrics to track](/investing/sectors/bank-operating-metrics-to-track/).
