**Owner earnings** is Warren Buffett's name for the cash a business could hand its owners each year **without shrinking**. It's like free cash flow, except it subtracts only the capital spending needed to maintain the business — not the spending to grow it.

Educational glossary entry — not a recommendation. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Owner earnings = reported earnings + depreciation, depletion, amortization and other non-cash charges − the average annual capital spending needed to maintain the company's competitive position and unit volume (plus any extra working capital that requires). The definition comes from Buffett's **1986 Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letter**. Its strength is conceptual: it separates "staying in place" from "growing." Its weakness is practical: companies don't report maintenance capex, so **you have to estimate it**.

## Owner earnings vs related measures

| Measure | Formula (simplified) | Strength | Weakness |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Net income | Revenue − all expenses (accrual) | Audited, standard | Depreciation may not match real reinvestment needs |
| EBITDA | Earnings before interest, taxes, D&A | Easy cross-company comparisons | Ignores capex entirely |
| Free cash flow (FCF) | Operating cash flow − **all** capex | Hard cash, reported inputs | Penalizes companies investing for growth |
| **Owner earnings** | Net income + non-cash charges − **maintenance** capex (± working capital) | Shows the cash a steady-state business throws off | Maintenance capex is an estimate |

## How to estimate maintenance capex

| Approach | How it works | Caveat |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Management disclosure | Some companies split "sustaining" from "growth" capital in the MD&A (miners and energy producers often do) | Company-defined; check the trend |
| Depreciation as a proxy | Assume maintenance capex ≈ D&A | Understates in inflationary periods; overstates after big acquisitions (amortized intangibles) |
| Flat-sales years | Look at capex in years when volumes didn't grow | Few clean years exist |
| Capex-to-sales ratio | Apply a long-run "maintenance" ratio to revenue | Crude; needs industry judgment |

Whatever method you use, write it down and keep it consistent — then the number is useful for comparing years and peers, even if it isn't precise.

## Where it fits for Canadian DIY investors

- **Asset-heavy TSX sectors** (rails, pipelines, telecom, producers) have large maintenance needs; owner earnings will sit well below EBITDA. See [rails](/investing/sectors/how-railroads-make-money/) and [telecom](/investing/sectors/how-telecoms-make-money/).
- **Serial acquirers** have big amortization of acquired intangibles; adding it back without thought can overstate owner earnings.
- **Valuation:** owner earnings per share is a natural input to a [simple DCF without fake precision](/investing/valuation/simple-dcf-without-fake-precision/) or a price-to-owner-earnings ratio, read like [P/FCF](/investing/valuation/what-p-fcf-means-canadian-diy/).
