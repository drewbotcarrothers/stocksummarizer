**Return on equity (ROE)** measures how much profit a company earns for each dollar of shareholders' equity. It's one of the most quoted quality metrics — and one of the easiest to misread, because debt, buybacks and write-downs can push it up without the business getting any better.

Educational glossary entry — not a recommendation. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

ROE = net income ÷ average shareholders' equity (for common shareholders, use net income available to common ÷ average common equity). A 15% ROE means the company earned $15 for every $100 of book equity over the year. Higher is generally better **if** it comes from margins and efficient use of assets rather than from leverage, and if it's sustainable. Compare ROE with peers in the same industry and over several years; for banks, read it alongside P/B and capital ratios.

## When ROE misleads

| Situation | What happens to ROE | Check instead |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Heavy debt | Inflated by leverage | Net debt / EBITDA, ROIC |
| Large buybacks | Equity shrinks, ROE rises | Per-share growth, ROIC |
| Write-downs or accumulated losses | Tiny or negative equity makes ROE meaningless | Use ROIC or return on assets |
| One-time gains | Net income spikes | Adjusted or multi-year average ROE |
| Acquisitive companies | Goodwill inflates equity, ROE looks low | Return on tangible equity |

## How Canadian DIY investors use it

- **Banks and insurers:** ROE is a primary lens; it drives what P/B multiple a bank deserves. See [valuation for banks: P/B, ROE and why P/E alone fails](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/) and [bank operating metrics](/investing/sectors/bank-operating-metrics-to-track/).
- **Quality screens:** a multi-year ROE floor is a common filter — see [screener literacy](/investing/brokers-tools/screener-filters-that-match-your-process/).
- **Cross-checks:** pair ROE with [free cash flow](/investing/glossary/what-is-free-cash-flow/) and leverage before calling a business "high quality".

By the numbers in the filings: net income is on the income statement; equity on the balance sheet (average the opening and closing balances). Many Canadian banks report ROE in their quarterly results — check how each defines it, since some use adjusted net income.
