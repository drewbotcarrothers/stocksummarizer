**Free cash flow (FCF)** is the cash a business generates after the capital expenditures needed to maintain and grow the asset base — roughly “cash earnings left after keeping the lights on and investing for tomorrow.”

For DIY investors, FCF is often a better dividend-safety and valuation companion than net income alone. Educational definition page — not advice.

## Direct answer

A common simple definition is operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Positive FCF means the business is generating cash beyond its reinvestment needs in that period; negative FCF can be fine for growth companies investing heavily — or a warning for mature payers stretching distributions.

## Intuition, not accounting theatre

Start from the cash flow statement:

1. **Cash from operations** — cash generated by the business after working-capital moves.
2. Subtract **capex** (often the “purchase of property, plant and equipment” line, sometimes split maintenance vs growth in MD&A).
3. What remains is a practical FCF sketch.

Companies and analysts quibble over definitions (leases under IFRS 16, growth vs maintenance capex, stock-based compensation). Write down *which* definition you are using in your notes.

## Why FCF matters on the TSX

- **Dividend coverage:** FCF coverage often beats earnings payout for capital-intensive names.
- **Valuation:** P/FCF and FCF yield sit beside P/E when earnings are noisy.
- **Capital allocation:** Buybacks, debt paydown, and M&A are funded with real cash, not vibes.

## Pitfalls

- Treating one strong FCF quarter as a permanent regime.
- Ignoring working-capital swings that reverse next quarter.
- Comparing FCF across banks/insurers with the same lens as operating industrials (financials need different metrics).
- Forgetting growth capex that is optional vs maintenance that is not.
