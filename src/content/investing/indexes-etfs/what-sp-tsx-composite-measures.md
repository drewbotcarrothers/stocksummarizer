"The TSX was down 1.7% today" is shorthand for a very specific calculation: the change in the **S&P/TSX Composite Index**. It's the headline benchmark for Canadian stocks — and the yardstick most DIY stock pickers should measure themselves against. But it isn't "the average Canadian stock," and knowing how it's built changes how you read it.

Educational only — not financial advice. Rules summarized below come from S&P Dow Jones Indices' published methodology (see Sources); check the current document before relying on any detail.

## Direct answer

The S&P/TSX Composite measures the **combined float-adjusted market value** of the largest and most liquid Canadian companies listed on the Toronto Stock Exchange. Bigger companies have bigger weights, but only shares that are actually available to trade (the "float") count. Membership is reviewed **quarterly** (March, June, September, December) against size, price, liquidity and domicile rules. The headline level is a **price index**; dividends are captured in a separate total-return version.

| Rule (simplified) | What it means for you |

| Minimum weight of 0.04% | Small caps fall below the line; the index tilts large |
| C$1 minimum price | Penny-stock prices are excluded |
| Float turnover ≥ 0.50 | Thinly traded stocks are excluded even if large |
| Quarterly review, effective after the third Friday | Additions and deletions cluster in March, June, September and December |

## Float-adjusted market-cap weighting, in plain English

Each company's weight = its **float-adjusted market cap** ÷ the total for all members. Float adjustment strips out shares held by strategic owners (controlling families, governments, other companies) using an **Investable Weight Factor (IWF)**.

| Hypothetical company | Shares outstanding | Price | Strategic holdings | IWF | Float-adjusted cap |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Widely held bank | 1,000M | $80 | 0% | 1.00 | $80.0B |
| Family-controlled retailer | 600M | $50 | 40% | 0.60 | $18.0B |
| Government-anchored utility | 300M | $40 | 25% | 0.75 | $9.0B |

The retailer's full market cap is $30B, but only $18B counts. That's why a company you think of as "huge" can carry less index weight than you'd expect.

## What the headline number is (and isn't)

| Question | Answer |
|:--|:--|
| Does it include dividends? | The headline is a price index; a separate total-return index reinvests dividends |
| Is it equal-weighted? | No — big companies dominate daily moves |
| Is it "the Canadian economy"? | No — it's listed, large, liquid companies; many private firms and small caps are absent |
| Is it capped? | The standard Composite isn't; the S&P/TSX **Capped** Composite limits any one stock to 10% |
| How many stocks? | A couple of hundred or so; the count changes at each review |

For a stock picker, the **total-return** version is the fair benchmark: your returns include dividends, so the index's should too. Comparing a dividend-heavy portfolio to the price index flatters you every year.

## The family: Composite vs TSX 60 vs Capped vs Completion

| Index | What it is | Typical use |
|:--|:--|:--|
| S&P/TSX Composite | Headline broad benchmark | News, broad ETFs, benchmarking |
| S&P/TSX 60 | 60 large, liquid leaders | Large-cap ETFs, futures and options |
| S&P/TSX Capped Composite | Composite with a 10% single-stock cap | Funds with diversification limits |
| S&P/TSX Completion | Composite minus the TSX 60 | Mid-cap exposure |
| S&P/TSX SmallCap | Smaller companies under separate rules | Small-cap benchmarking |

## What the index can't tell you

- **Breadth:** a flat index can hide a day where most stocks fell and two giants rallied. Our [daily market wraps](/market-wrap/) look at sector moves for that reason.
- **Sector mix:** the Composite leans heavily on financials, energy and materials compared with US benchmarks — see [why Canada is not the S&P 500](/investing/indexes-etfs/canada-vs-sp-500-sector-weights/).
- **Valuation:** an index level says nothing about whether stocks are cheap. Use [P/E](/investing/valuation/what-pe-means-canadian-diy/) and [when P/E misleads](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/) at the stock level.
- **Your risk:** a five-stock portfolio can lag or beat it by wide margins in any year.

## How DIY investors should use it

1. Benchmark your stock sleeve against the **total-return** Composite (or a broad Canadian equity ETF as a proxy) over 3–5 years, not months.
2. Before buying a stock, note its sector's weight in the index — owning a lot more or less is an active bet.
3. If you hold a broad Canadian ETF *and* individual TSX stocks, check the overlap — see [Canadian equity ETFs vs owning individual stocks](/investing/indexes-etfs/canadian-equity-etfs-vs-individual-stocks/).
