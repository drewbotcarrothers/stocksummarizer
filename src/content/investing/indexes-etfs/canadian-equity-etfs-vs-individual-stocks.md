Buying a Canadian equity ETF is like buying the whole market stall; picking individual TSX stocks is choosing each box yourself. One is simpler and cheaper to get "right enough." The other gives you control — and the full consequences of your choices. This guide lays out the trade-offs without pretending one answer suits everyone.

Educational only — not financial advice. No specific fund or stock is recommended; figures are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

A broad Canadian equity ETF gives you hundreds of TSX companies in one trade, at a low yearly cost, with returns very close to the index. Individual stocks give you control over what you own, no ongoing fund fee, and the chance to beat the index — but also the real chance to lag it badly, plus more time and discipline required. For most DIY investors the practical answer is **both**: an ETF core with a capped stock sleeve (see [core-satellite](/investing/process/core-satellite-portfolio-basics/)).

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Canadian equity ETF | Individual TSX stocks |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Diversification | Hundreds of holdings in one trade | Depends on you — 15–25 names for reasonable spread |
| Ongoing cost | MER + TER every year | No fund fee; trading costs and spreads |
| Expected result vs index | Index minus a small tracking difference | Anywhere from far below to far above |
| Control | None over holdings | Full — avoid sectors, size by conviction |
| Time required | Minimal | Ongoing research and quarterly follow-up |
| Tax control (taxable account) | Fund distributes; you can't choose which gains to realize | You choose when to sell, harvest losses, defer gains |
| Dividend tax credit | Eligible dividends flow through | Eligible dividends direct |
| Behavioural risk | Lower — fewer decisions | Higher — every headline is a decision |
| Sector concentration | Matches the index (heavy in financials, energy, materials) | Can be lower — or much higher |

## Outcome dispersion: the real difference

The ETF's result is narrow and predictable *relative to the market*. A stock portfolio's result is a wide range — and it gets wider as the number of holdings falls.

## Costs, honestly compared

| Cost (hypothetical, $50,000 for one year) | ETF | 20 individual stocks |
|:--|--:|--:|
| MER + TER (0.10% total) | $50 | $0 |
| Initial trades (commission-free broker) | $0 | $0 |
| Bid-ask spread on entry | ~$10 | ~$40 |
| Ongoing trades (rebalancing, follow-ups) | ~$0 | Varies |
| Your time (research and monitoring) | ~1 hour | Many hours |

Low-cost broad ETFs are cheap enough that cost alone rarely decides it. Time, temperament and the overlap risk do. See [MER, TER, and tracking difference](/investing/indexes-etfs/mer-ter-tracking-difference/).

## The overlap problem

If you own a TSX index ETF **and** the big banks, pipelines and railways directly, you hold them twice. That's fine if it's intentional — it's an overweight — but many investors don't realize how concentrated they've become. Check the ETF's top 10 ([how to read an ETF factsheet](/investing/indexes-etfs/how-to-read-etf-factsheet/)) against your stock list.

| If... | Lean toward |
|:--|:--|
| You want market returns with minimal effort | Broad ETF only |
| You enjoy research and have a written process | ETF core + stock sleeve |
| You want to avoid certain sectors or companies | Individual stocks (or a screened ETF) |
| You're investing in a taxable account with large gains to manage | Individual stocks offer more timing control |
| You tend to react to headlines | ETF core, keep the sleeve small |
