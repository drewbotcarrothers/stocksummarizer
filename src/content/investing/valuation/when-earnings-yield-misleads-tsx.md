Earnings yield inherits every weakness of P/E, because it is the same number upside down. Those are covered in [when P/E misleads on TSX stocks](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/) (cyclicals, currency, REITs, insurers, depreciation, one-offs, holding companies) and are not repeated here. This post covers the traps that are specific to the **yield form**: the comparison with bond and GIC yields, how a yield exaggerates peak earnings, what negative yields do and do not tell you, and the blind spots of the EBIT ÷ EV variant. It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Earnings yield misleads when you (1) compare it one-for-one with a bond or GIC yield, (2) read a high yield on a cyclical at peak earnings as cheap, (3) trust a yield lifted by one-time gains, (4) rank loss-makers by how negative their yield is, (5) use EBIT ÷ EV for businesses whose capex runs well above depreciation, or (6) compare after-tax and pre-tax yields. Normalize EPS, keep yields on one basis, and cross-check with [FCF yield](/investing/valuation/what-fcf-yield-means-canadian-diy/).

## Trap 1: the bond comparison

"The earnings yield is 7% and a government bond pays 4%, so the stock is cheap." The comparison skips three things: earnings are not paid to you (only dividends are), earnings are risky while the bond's coupon is contractual, and a bond's yield is nominal while earnings can grow with inflation. The gap between the two is a **risk premium you are accepting**, not a free 3%.

| Hypothetical | Stock | Bond |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Yield | 7.0% | 4.0% |
| Cash paid to you | 3.0% dividend yield | 4.0% coupon |
| Growth in payments | Unknown; could be negative | Zero |
| What can go wrong | Earnings fall, dividend cut | Issuer default |

Use the gap only as a relative measure: compare it with the same company's or sector's history, and expect it to move as rates and risk appetite change.

## Trap 2: peak earnings make the highest yields

For cyclical companies (energy, materials, some industrials), trailing earnings are highest at the top of the cycle. The earnings yield is then at its highest exactly when future earnings are most likely to fall.

Fix: use mid-cycle or three-to-five-year average EPS. See [how to read commodity price sensitivity for resource issuers](/investing/analyze/read-commodity-price-sensitivity-resource-issuers/) for building a mid-cycle view.

## Trap 3: one-time gains in EPS

A gain on selling a division, a revaluation of investment property or a tax recovery can lift reported EPS for one year. The yield looks higher, but the gain will not repeat.

| Hypothetical | Reported | Excluding the gain |
|:--|--:|--:|
| EPS | $4.20 | $2.80 |
| Price | $40.00 | $40.00 |
| Earnings yield | 10.5% | 7.0% |

Fix: strip non-recurring items; see [how to spot one-time items in Canadian financial statements](/investing/analyze/spot-one-time-items-canadian-statements/).

## Trap 4: negative yields are rankable, not comparable

A −2% yield and a −10% yield are both losses. Ranking them tells you which loses more relative to its price, not which is cheaper. For loss-making growth companies, switch to [EV/Sales](/investing/valuation/what-ev-sales-means-canadian-diy/) or a cash-runway view instead of reading anything into the size of the negative yield.

## Trap 5: EBIT ÷ EV and the capex gap

EBIT ÷ EV charges depreciation, which is better than EV/EBITDA for asset-heavy businesses. But depreciation is based on historical cost. When a company must reinvest much more than its depreciation (inflation in replacement costs, growth, regulatory spending), EBIT overstates the cash the business can release.

| Hypothetical | Company A | Company B |
|:--|--:|--:|
| EBIT ÷ EV (pre-tax) | 8.0% | 8.0% |
| Capex ÷ depreciation | 1.0x | 2.0x |
| Unlevered FCF yield | 5.5% | 2.5% |
| Read | Earnings close to cash | Earnings well above cash |

Fix: show EBIT ÷ EV next to the unlevered FCF yield from the [EV/FCF set](/investing/valuation/what-ev-fcf-means-canadian-diy/).

## Trap 6: mixed bases

| Mistake | Why it misleads | Fix |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Comparing a pre-tax EBIT yield with an after-tax earnings yield | Tax alone can create a gap of a quarter or more | Use EBIT × (1 − t) ÷ EV |
| Mixing adjusted and reported EPS across peers | Adjustments differ by company | One basis for all |
| Using a US-dollar EPS against a CAD price | Understates the yield | Convert at the average rate |
| Comparing trailing yields with forward yields | Different periods | Label and keep apart |

## 60-second check

| Question | If yes |
|:--|:--|
| Is the company cyclical and near peak earnings? | Normalize EPS before reading the yield |
| Did EPS include a large one-time gain? | Recalculate without it |
| Is the yield being compared with a bond yield? | Read the gap as a risk premium, not a bargain |
| Is capex far above depreciation? | Cross-check with FCF yield |
| Are all peers on the same tax and EPS basis? | If not, rebuild before ranking |
