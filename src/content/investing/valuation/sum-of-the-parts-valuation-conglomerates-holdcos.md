Some of the best-known names on the TSX are holding companies or conglomerates: businesses that own stakes in listed companies, private operating businesses, real estate and investment portfolios under one roof. A single multiple does not work for them, because each piece deserves a different one. Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation values each piece on its own terms, adds them up, subtracts what sits at the top, and compares the result with the share price.

This post builds a SOTP for a hypothetical Canadian holding company and explains why holdcos usually trade at a discount to that sum. The NAV approach for single-asset businesses is in [what Price/NAV means](/investing/valuation/what-price-nav-means-canadian-diy/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

(1) **List every asset**: listed stakes, private businesses, real estate, cash and investments. (2) **Value each one** with the method that fits it: market value for listed stakes, a peer multiple or DCF for private businesses, cap rates or NAV for real estate. (3) **Subtract holdco-level claims**: holdco debt, preferred shares, and the capitalized cost of running head office. (4) Consider **tax** on embedded gains if assets would be sold. (5) Divide by **diluted shares** for NAV per share and compare with the price. (6) Decide whether the **discount** is justified by structure, governance and capital allocation.

## A worked SOTP (hypothetical)

| Part | Method | Inputs | Value (C$M) |
|:--|:--|:--|--:|
| 50% stake in a listed insurer | Market value | 50% × C$8,000M market cap | 4,000 |
| 100%-owned industrial business | EV/EBITDA, less its own debt | C$300M EBITDA × 8.0x − C$600M debt | 1,800 |
| Real estate portfolio | NOI ÷ cap rate, less mortgages | C$120M ÷ 6.0% − C$800M | 1,200 |
| Cash and investment portfolio | Balance sheet | | 500 |
| Holdco debt | Balance sheet | | −1,200 |
| Head-office costs | C$50M a year × 8x | | −400 |
| Preferred shares | Stated value | | −300 |
| **Net asset value** | | | **5,600** |
| **NAV per share (200M diluted shares)** | | | **$28.00** |
| Share price | | | $21.00 |
| **Discount to NAV** | | | **25%** |

## Valuing each kind of part

| Part type | Method | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Listed stake | Market price × shares owned | A control stake may deserve a premium; a large block may sell at a discount |
| Private operating business | Peer multiple or DCF; see [comparable company analysis](/investing/valuation/comparable-company-analysis-canadian-sectors/) | Using a listed peer's multiple without a size or liquidity adjustment |
| Real estate | NOI ÷ cap rate, or appraised NAV | Stale cap rates; see [when Price/NAV misleads](/investing/valuation/when-price-nav-misleads-tsx/) |
| Investment funds or portfolios | Reported fair value | Valuation lag on private holdings |
| Insurance or bank subsidiary | P/B or P/TBV with ROE | Regulatory capital that cannot be moved up to the holdco |
| Holdco debt and preferreds | Book or market value | Guarantees of subsidiary debt |

Two rules keep it honest. Subtract **subsidiary debt** in the subsidiary's line (as above) and **holdco debt** at the top, and never count either twice. And use **look-through** ownership: if the holdco owns 50%, take 50% of equity value, not 50% of enterprise value.

Management presentations often show the headline version. Head office is a real, recurring cost that a buyer of the separate pieces would not pay, and capital gains tax is payable if the holdco ever sells a stake that has risen in value. Which version you use depends on whether you expect assets to be sold.

## Why holdcos trade at a discount

| Reason | Typical effect | Evidence to check |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Head-office costs | Permanent drag | Corporate costs in the segment note |
| Tax on embedded gains | Larger if sales are likely | Deferred tax note |
| Capital allocation record | Discount widens if poor, narrows if strong | Returns on past acquisitions; see [M&A track records](/investing/analyze/analyze-ma-track-records-serial-acquirers/) |
| Controlling shareholder or dual-class shares | Minority investors cannot force change | Management information circular; see [how to read an MIC](/investing/analyze/read-management-information-circular/) |
| Complexity and disclosure | Harder to analyze, fewer analysts | Segment reporting quality |
| Leverage at the top | Holdco debt is subordinated to subsidiary debt | Holdco-only financial statements, if provided |

A discount is not a catalyst. It narrows when something changes: a spin-off, a sale at or near NAV, buybacks below NAV, or simplification. See [how to evaluate spin-offs and corporate separations](/investing/analyze/evaluate-spin-offs-corporate-separations/).

## Track the discount over time

| Year (hypothetical) | NAV per share | Share price | Discount |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Year 1 | $24.00 | $19.20 | 20% |
| Year 2 | $25.50 | $19.90 | 22% |
| Year 3 | $26.80 | $20.10 | 25% |
| Year 4 (today) | $28.00 | $21.00 | 25% |

NAV per share has compounded at about 5.3% a year; the price has lagged because the discount widened. Compare today's discount with the company's own history before calling it unusually wide.
