A stock that has doubled is not necessarily a business that has doubled. Over any holding period, a share price changes for two reasons: the earnings per share changed, or the multiple investors are willing to pay for those earnings changed. The first comes from the business. The second comes from the market's mood, interest rates and expectations, and it can reverse.

This post shows how to split a stock's return into its parts, how to spot when a price (or a forecast) depends on the multiple continuing to expand, and how to stress-test that. It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Over a holding period, **total return ≈ EPS growth + change in the P/E multiple + dividend yield** (more precisely, price return = (1 + EPS growth) × (1 + multiple change) − 1). If most of a stock's past gain came from **P/E expansion** rather than EPS growth, or if a bullish case only works with a **higher exit multiple** than today's, the return depends on hope rather than the business. Rebuild the forecast with a flat or lower exit multiple and see whether the case still works.

## Decompose the past five years

| Hypothetical stock | 5 years ago | Today | Annual rate |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Share price | $40.00 | $70.00 | 11.8% |
| EPS | $2.00 | $2.60 | 5.4% |
| P/E | 20.0x | 26.9x | 6.1% |
| Dividend yield (average) | | | 2.0% |

The check: 1.054 × 1.061 ≈ 1.118, so EPS growth and multiple expansion together explain the 11.8% price gain. Add the yield for a total return near 13.8% a year.

That is not automatically bad. Re-ratings can be earned when a company improves its return on capital or lowers its risk. But it changes the question for a new buyer at $70: the next five years start from 26.9x, not 20x.

## What does the forward case assume?

Suppose an investor buying at $70 expects about 12% a year including a 2% yield, so roughly 10% a year from price. Analysts expect EPS to grow about 6% a year.

| Exit P/E in 5 years | EPS in 5 years ($2.60 × 1.06⁵) | Price in 5 years | Price return per year |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| 20.0x (back to the old level) | $3.48 | $69.59 | −0.1% |
| 26.9x (unchanged) | $3.48 | $93.66 | 6.0% |
| 32.4x (expands again) | $3.48 | $112.73 | 10.0% |

The forecast quietly relies on another round of multiple expansion. If the multiple only holds, the return is EPS growth plus yield, about 8% a year. If it drifts back toward 20x, five years of EPS growth are absorbed by the de-rating.

## Signs that a price depends on expansion

| Sign | What you see | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Price up far more than EPS | Price CAGR double the EPS CAGR | The multiple did the work |
| P/E near the top of its own history | Above its 75th–90th percentile | Less room to expand; see [historical multiple ranges](/investing/valuation/historical-multiple-ranges-without-anchoring/) |
| Target prices rising faster than estimates | Higher targets, flat EPS forecasts | Targets are assuming a higher multiple |
| "Re-rating" or "scarcity" language | Narrative about a new investor base or index inclusion | Story-driven multiples can fade |
| Falling rates did the lifting | Multiple rose as bond yields fell | Can reverse if rates rise; see [interest rates and multiples](/investing/valuation/interest-rates-change-justified-multiples/) |
| Forward P/E far below trailing | Big EPS jump forecast next year | Check whether the jump is realistic; see [when forward P/E misleads](/investing/valuation/when-forward-pe-misleads-tsx/) |

## When expansion is earned

| Earned re-rating | Hope re-rating |
|:--|:--|
| Return on capital rose and stayed up | Return on capital flat |
| Revenue mix shifted to recurring or contracted | Mix unchanged |
| Balance sheet de-levered | Leverage unchanged or higher |
| Growth runway visibly longer (new markets, capacity) | Growth guidance unchanged |
| Peers with the same change trade at similar multiples | Stock now well above every peer |

## A simple stress test

1. Write down EPS growth you think is reasonable (not the most optimistic estimate).
2. Compute the exit price at **today's** multiple, at the **median of its history** and at a **lower** multiple.
3. Add the dividend yield.
4. Ask: does the investment still make sense if the multiple does not expand?

If the answer is yes, any re-rating is a bonus. If the answer depends on expansion, you are making a market call as much as a business call. This is different from the [mature dividend payer lens](/investing/valuation/value-mature-dividend-payers-beyond-yield/), which builds an expected return forward; here the aim is to spot the reliance on multiple change, looking both back and forward.
