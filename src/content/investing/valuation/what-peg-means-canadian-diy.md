The **PEG ratio** takes a P/E and divides it by the company's expected earnings growth rate. The idea is simple: a stock on 20 times earnings that grows earnings 20% a year may be better value than one on 12 times earnings growing 4%. PEG puts growth into the comparison, which plain P/E leaves out.

This post assumes you know the basics of P/E. If not, start with [what P/E means for Canadian DIY investors](/investing/valuation/what-pe-means-canadian-diy/) and [what forward P/E means](/investing/valuation/what-forward-pe-means-canadian-diy/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

PEG = **P/E ÷ expected annual EPS growth rate**, with growth written as a whole number (12% growth = 12). A PEG near **1.0** is the classic "fair" rule of thumb that Peter Lynch popularized in *One Up on Wall Street*. Below 1 can mean growth is cheap; above 2 can mean you are paying a lot for growth. But PEG is only as good as the **growth estimate** behind it, and it breaks down for cyclicals, loss-makers, very slow growers and big dividend payers, which together make up a large share of the TSX.

## The parts of PEG

| Part | What it is | Where it comes from |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Price | Current share price | Market quote (CAD for TSX listings) |
| EPS | Trailing or forward earnings per share | Filings (trailing) or estimates (forward) |
| P/E | Price ÷ EPS | Your calculation; see [how to calculate P/E in CAD](/investing/valuation/calculate-pe-in-cad-from-filings/) |
| Growth rate | Expected annual EPS growth, usually 3–5 years | Analyst consensus, guidance, or history |
| PEG | P/E ÷ growth rate | Your calculation |

The P/E and the growth rate should describe the **same earnings**: if the P/E uses adjusted EPS, the growth rate should be for adjusted EPS too.

## Reading a PEG (rules of thumb, not rules)

| PEG | Common reading | What to check before agreeing |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Below 0.5 | Very cheap for its growth | Is the growth a one-time rebound or a cyclical peak? |
| 0.5 – 1.0 | Growth looks reasonably priced | How reliable and long-lasting is the growth? |
| 1.0 – 2.0 | Paying up for growth | Is there a moat, recurring revenue, a strong balance sheet? |
| Above 2.0 | Expensive for its growth | Is growth too low for PEG to be useful (utilities, telecoms)? |
| Negative or n/m | Not meaningful | Losses or falling EPS: use other tools |

## The dividend fix: PEGY

Many TSX stocks return most of their profit as dividends, so their EPS grows slowly and their PEG looks high. A common adjustment is **PEGY** (sometimes called dividend-adjusted PEG): **P/E ÷ (growth rate + dividend yield)**.

| Hypothetical company | P/E | EPS growth | Dividend yield | PEG | PEGY |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Utility-style payer | 18.0x | 5% | 5% | 3.60 | 1.80 |
| Growth compounder | 25.0x | 18% | 1% | 1.39 | 1.32 |

The payer looks very expensive on PEG and reasonable on PEGY. Neither number is "right"; PEGY simply credits the cash you receive. Check dividend safety separately with [FCF coverage of dividends](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/).

## Where PEG fits on the TSX

| Sector | PEG usefulness | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Software and tech | Useful once profitable | Growth is the main driver of value |
| Industrials and rails | Moderate | Steady growth, but cycles affect EPS |
| Banks and insurers | Limited | Slow growth; P/B and ROE matter more |
| Utilities, pipelines, telecoms | Low; try PEGY | Low growth, high payouts |
| Energy and miners | Low | Commodity-driven EPS swings |
| REITs | Low | Use price/FFO or price/AFFO instead |
