Mature dividend payers are a large part of the TSX: banks, utilities, pipelines, telecoms, insurers and a long list of steady industrial and consumer names. The habit is to rank them by yield. That skips three questions that decide what you actually earn: how fast the dividend can grow, whether the price you pay is high or low for this company, and whether the dividend is safe.

This post sets out a four-lens valuation card for mature payers and applies it to three hypothetical companies. The dividend discount model is covered in [how dividend discount models fit and fail](/investing/valuation/dividend-discount-models-tsx-income-names/), and yield as a multiple in [what dividend yield means](/investing/valuation/what-dividend-yield-means-canadian-diy/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Value a mature dividend payer through four lenses. (1) **Return:** forward yield + sustainable dividend growth + any expected change in valuation multiple, compared with your required return. (2) **Price:** P/E or P/FCF against the company's own history and peers. (3) **Safety:** payout on free cash flow, leverage and capital needs. (4) **Runway:** where future growth comes from and whether retained earnings can fund it, using growth ≈ return on equity × (1 − payout ratio). The highest yield often fails lens 3 or 4.

## Three hypothetical payers

| Lens and measure | Payer A | Payer B | Payer C |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| **Return:** forward dividend yield | 7.0% | 4.0% | 2.0% |
| Sustainable dividend growth | 0% | 5% | 8% |
| **Price:** P/E today | 15x | 16x | 22x |
| P/E, 10-year average | 17x | 16x | 19x |
| Earnings payout (yield × P/E) | 105% | 64% | 44% |
| **Safety:** dividends ÷ free cash flow | 110% | 65% | 40% |
| Net debt ÷ EBITDA | 5.5x | 3.0x | 1.5x |
| **Runway:** return on equity | 6% | 14% | 14% |
| ROE × (1 − earnings payout) | negative | 5.0% | 7.8% |

## Lens 1: expected return, not yield

A simple estimate of long-run return is yield + growth + annualized change in valuation multiple. Over five years:

| Return build (hypothetical) | Payer A | Payer B | Payer C |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Yield | 7.0% | 4.0% | 2.0% |
| + Growth | 0.0% | 5.0% | 8.0% |
| + P/E moves to 10-yr average over 5 years | +2.5% | 0.0% | −2.9% |
| **Estimated annual return** | **9.5%** | **9.0%** | **7.1%** |
| Main risk to the estimate | Dividend cut | Growth slows | Multiple falls further |

On this arithmetic Payer A looks best. The next two lenses explain why it probably is not.

## Lens 2: price against its own history and peers

A low P/E against the company's own 10-year average can mean the stock is cheap, or that the business is worse than it was. Check what changed: margins, leverage, regulation, competition. For the method, see [how to use P/E in a simple valuation checklist](/investing/valuation/use-pe-simple-valuation-checklist/) and [P/FCF vs peers](/investing/valuation/p-fcf-vs-peers-canada-first-comparison/). For capital-heavy payers such as pipelines and telecoms, EV/EBITDA is usually a better price lens than P/E.

## Lens 3: is the dividend safe?

| Safety check | Comfortable | Watch | Warning |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Dividends ÷ free cash flow | Below 70% | 70–100% | Above 100% for 2+ years |
| Net debt ÷ EBITDA (non-utility) | Below 3x | 3–4.5x | Above 4.5x |
| Dividend funded partly by DRIP or new shares | No | Partly | Mostly |
| Capex needs rising | No | Moderately | Sharply, with no funding plan |

Payer A pays 110% of free cash flow with net debt at 5.5x EBITDA. Its 7.0% yield already prices in some risk of a cut. If it cut by 30%, the yield on today's price would fall to 4.9%, and stocks usually fall on a cut. See [free cash flow coverage of dividends](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/) and [how to avoid dividend traps](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/).

## Lens 4: where future growth comes from

Retained earnings fund growth. A rough sustainable growth rate is **return on equity × (1 − payout ratio)**. Payer B earns 14% on equity and retains 36% of earnings, which supports about 5.0% growth, consistent with its 5% assumption. Payer C's 14% ROE and 56% retention support about 7.8%, close to its 8% assumption. Payer A pays out more than it earns, so its dividend can only grow with more debt or new shares.

| Growth source | Durable? | Check in filings |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Rate base growth (utilities) | Usually | Capital plan and regulatory decisions in the MD&A |
| Contracted projects (pipelines) | If contracted | Backlog, contract terms, counterparty quality |
| Pricing power (consumer, rails) | Varies | [Pricing power language in the MD&A](/investing/analyze/analyze-pricing-power-mdna-language/) |
| Buybacks | Only if funded by FCF | [Share buyback disclosures](/investing/analyze/read-share-buyback-disclosures-canada/) |
| Payout ratio rising | No, it runs out | Payout trend over 5–10 years |

## Total shareholder yield

Some mature payers return cash through buybacks as well as dividends. Add net buybacks (repurchases minus shares issued) to the dividend yield to get total shareholder yield. A company with a 3.5% dividend yield and 2% net buybacks returns more cash than one with a 5% yield that issues 1% new shares a year through its DRIP.

## Putting the card together

| Payer (hypothetical) | Return | Price | Safety | Runway | Reading |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| A | Strong on paper | Below history | Fails | Fails | Yield trap risk; avoid or size small |
| B | Solid | At average | Passes | Passes | Fairly priced compounder |
| C | Good if growth lasts | Above history | Passes | Passes | Quality at a premium; wait for a better price |
