**Price-to-book (P/B)** compares a company's share price with its **book value per share**: the accounting value of what common shareholders would own after all liabilities. It is the classic multiple for banks, and it has its own post for that: [valuation for banks: P/B, ROE, and why P/E alone fails](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/).

This post is about **everything else** where P/B can help: insurers, industrials, steel and forest products, resource issuers and holding companies, all well represented on the TSX. It is educational; figures are illustrative or hypothetical.

## Direct answer

P/B = **share price ÷ book value per share** (or market cap ÷ common equity). Book value is assets minus liabilities, minus preferred shares and non-controlling interests. A P/B of 1.0 means the market values the company at its accounting net worth. P/B is most useful when the balance sheet **reflects the real earning assets** (insurers, asset-heavy industrials, holding companies) and least useful when value sits in brands, software or people that accounting rules keep off the balance sheet. Read P/B together with **return on equity (ROE)**: a company that earns more on its equity deserves a higher P/B.

## What book value includes, and what it misses

| Item | In book value? | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Plant, equipment, mines | Yes, usually at historical cost less depreciation (IAS 16 cost model) | Old assets may be worth far more, or less, than book |
| Land | Yes, at cost | Land bought decades ago can be deeply undervalued |
| Investment property | Often at fair value (IAS 40) | Makes P/B close to price/NAV for real estate owners |
| Goodwill from acquisitions | Yes | Inflates book; can be written down (IAS 36) |
| Internally built brands, software, customer lists | Mostly no (IAS 38) | Asset-light firms look expensive on P/B |
| Insurer future profit on existing policies | No; held as a liability (IFRS 17 contractual service margin) | Many investors look at book plus after-tax CSM |
| Accumulated other comprehensive income | Yes | FX and investment swings move book without any operating change |

## The ROE link (short version)

P/B and ROE are tied together. A simple no-growth version: **fair P/B ≈ ROE ÷ cost of equity**. A business earning a hypothetical 8% ROE when shareholders want 10% "deserves" about 0.8x book; one earning 15% deserves about 1.5x. That is why a low P/B is not automatically cheap: it may simply reflect a low ROE. The full bank treatment, including growth, is in the bank valuation post linked above; see also [what is ROE?](/investing/glossary/what-is-roe/).

## Where P/B fits on the TSX (outside banks)

| Business type | P/B usefulness | Pair it with |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Life and P&C insurers | High | ROE, book plus CSM under IFRS 17, combined ratio for P&C |
| Holding companies | High | Discount to the value of holdings (NAV) |
| Steel, forest products, chemicals | Moderate | Mid-cycle ROE, replacement cost of assets |
| Diversified industrials | Moderate | ROE, goodwill share of book |
| Miners and energy producers | Low to moderate | Price/NAV, reserves, cost curve |
| REITs and real estate | Moderate | Price/NAV, price/AFFO |
| Software and asset-light services | Low | P/E, P/S, FCF yield |

For insurers specifically, see [how to analyze insurers differently from banks](/investing/analyze/analyze-insurers-vs-banks/) and [how insurers make money](/investing/sectors/how-insurers-make-money/).
