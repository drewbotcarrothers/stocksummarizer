Canadian banks usually trade above book value. The standard explanation (a return on equity above the cost of equity justifies a price-to-book above one) is covered in [valuing Canadian banks: P/B, ROE and why P/E alone fails](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/). This post asks the question behind that: **where does the excess return come from**, and why doesn't it show up on the balance sheet?

The short answer is the **franchise**: millions of everyday chequing and savings accounts, payroll deposits, mortgages and cards that give a bank cheap, sticky funding and a customer base to sell more products to. It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

A bank's franchise value is the value of its **low-cost, stable deposit base and customer relationships** beyond what is recorded as assets. Deposits are liabilities on the balance sheet, yet a bank that can fund itself at 1–2% while wholesale markets charge 4–5% earns a spread that competitors without the franchise can't match. That advantage is **not recorded as an asset** (it is internally built, not purchased), so it shows up indirectly: as a higher return on equity and a price-to-book ratio above one. To judge it, look at the **deposit mix, cost of funds, deposit growth and customer retention**, not just headline ROE.

| Funding source | Stickiness | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Chequing and operating accounts | Very high; tied to payroll, bills and cards | Cheapest money a bank has |
| Savings and high-interest accounts | Moderate; rate-sensitive customers move | Cost rises fast when rates rise |
| GICs and term deposits | Locked for a term, then repriced | Predictable but expensive |
| Wholesale funding and covered bonds | Market-dependent | Costliest and can dry up in stress |

The share of funding from the first row is the clearest single sign of franchise strength. Banks disclose deposit breakdowns in their annual reports and supplementary financial packages.

## Two banks, same book value (hypothetical)

| | Bank A (strong franchise) | Bank B (thin franchise) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Operating deposits as share of funding | 45% | 15% |
| Average cost of funds | 2.0% | 3.4% |
| Net interest margin | 2.2% | 1.6% |
| ROE | 14% | 9% |
| Cost of equity (assumed) | 10% | 10% |
| Justified P/B (no-growth shortcut: ROE ÷ cost of equity) | 1.4x | 0.9x |

Same accounting book value, very different value to shareholders. Most of the gap comes from the cost of funds, which comes from the deposit base. For margins, see [what net interest margin is](/investing/glossary/what-is-net-interest-margin/).

## Why it isn't on the balance sheet

| Item | On the balance sheet? | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Deposits (the liability) | Yes, at face value | Money owed to customers |
| Value of the low-cost deposit relationship | No (if built internally) | IFRS doesn't allow capitalising internally generated customer relationships |
| Core deposit intangible | Only after an acquisition | Recognised when a bank buys another bank's deposits |
| Brand, branch network effects, payroll links | No | Internally generated intangibles |

That is why acquisitions are revealing: when one bank buys another, part of the price is often recorded as a **core deposit intangible**, a rare glimpse of what the market will pay for a deposit franchise.

## Signs the franchise is weakening

| Sign | Where to look |
|:--|:--|
| Operating deposits shrinking as a share of funding | Deposit breakdown in the annual report |
| Cost of funds rising faster than peers | Net interest margin commentary in the MD&A |
| Heavier reliance on wholesale funding | Funding and liquidity section |
| Customer attrition or slower account growth | Investor presentations, segment disclosures |
| Digital competitors winning payroll and chequing | Industry data, management commentary |

## Common mistakes

- **Treating ROE as purely a management skill.** Much of it is inherited from the deposit base.
- **Assuming high rates always help.** Higher rates widen margins only if deposit costs lag; rate-sensitive deposits reprice quickly.
- **Comparing Canadian banks with US regional banks directly.** Market structure, mortgage rules and deposit concentration differ.
- **Ignoring credit.** A great franchise can still be hit by loan losses; see [bank operating metrics to track](/investing/sectors/bank-operating-metrics-to-track/).
