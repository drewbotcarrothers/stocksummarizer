Comparing lenders on price to tangible book is a habit on Bay Street, and it works when two conditions hold: the peers are genuinely alike, and you read P/TBV next to the return that tangible equity earns. Skip either and the "cheapest" bank on the screen is usually the one earning the least, reserving the least or holding the least capital.

This guide builds a fair P/TBV peer table for TSX-listed lenders and adds the columns that explain the gaps: ROTCE, CET1, credit costs and how much of equity is goodwill. The general peer method is in [how to use peer tables without cherry-picking](/investing/analyze/use-peer-tables-without-cherry-picking/); the ordinary P/B comparison is in [P/B vs peers](/investing/valuation/pb-vs-peers-canada-first-comparison/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Compare lenders with a **similar business mix and regulator**, with TBV built on **one set of deductions** (software, DTL add-back), **period-end shares**, the **same date** and the **same currency**. Rank P/TBV against the **peer median**, then explain it with **through-cycle ROTCE**, **CET1 ratio**, **provisions for credit losses** and **goodwill and intangibles as a share of common equity**. The cleanest single check is the **justified P/TBV ≈ (ROTCE − g) ÷ (r − g)**: a lender trading below the line may be cheap; one far above it needs a reason.

## Alignment rules

| Rule | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| Similar business mix | Large diversified banks, regional and alternative mortgage lenders, consumer lenders, life insurers and P&C insurers earn very different returns |
| Same regulator and capital measure | OSFI CET1 for banks; LICAT for life insurers; MCT for P&C. US banks report under different rules |
| One TBV definition | Software deducted or not, DTL added back or not, for every peer |
| Period-end shares | Weighted averages distort per-share TBV after buybacks |
| Through-cycle ROTCE | A single strong year flatters a lender at the top of the credit cycle |
| Same date and currency | Some TSX financials report in US dollars |
| IFRS and US GAAP peers kept apart | Credit loss and securities accounting differ |

On this view, the lowest P/TBV in the group is not the cheapest. The required return and growth are illustrative; if you use 11% instead of 10%, every justified multiple falls by about 14%, so focus on the **ranking**, not the exact numbers. The method follows [valuing Canadian banks with P/B and ROE](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/), applied to tangible equity.

## The other columns

| Column | What it explains | Hypothetical read |
|:--|:--|:--|
| CET1 ratio | Capital cushion and room for buybacks or dividend growth | Peer B has the thinnest cushion, which supports its lower multiple |
| PCL (bps) | Credit cost through the cycle | Peer B and Peer D carry riskier books; their ROTCE is less dependable |
| Goodwill + intangibles ÷ common equity | How much of book is acquisition premium | Peer B's P/B is only about 0.9x (1.4x × 65%), so it looks even cheaper on book; goodwill explains most of that gap |
| Allowance ÷ loans (add if available) | Whether TBV is conservatively stated | A thin allowance inflates TBV; see [when tangible book misleads](/investing/valuation/when-tangible-book-misleads-tsx/) |

## Peer groups that should not share a table

| Group | Why it stands apart | Main capital measure |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Large diversified Canadian banks | Wealth, capital markets and insurance alongside lending | CET1 (OSFI, domestic systemically important bank buffers) |
| Smaller banks and alternative mortgage lenders | Narrower books, higher funding costs, different growth | CET1 (OSFI) or provincial regimes |
| Consumer and specialty lenders | Higher yields and higher losses | Leverage and funding mix |
| Life insurers | IFRS 17 CSM, long-dated liabilities | LICAT |
| P&C insurers | Underwriting cycle, catastrophe exposure | MCT |
| US banks | US GAAP, CECL credit losses, Federal Reserve capital rules | CET1 (US rules) |
