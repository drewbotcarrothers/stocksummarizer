If you just need the definition, start with the [EV/EBITDA glossary entry](/investing/glossary/what-is-ev-ebitda/). This page is the **application guide**: how Canadian DIY investors can build the multiple consistently, where it misleads on TSX names, and how to use it alongside leverage and cash flow when comparing stocks.

Educational only — not financial advice. Worked numbers are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

EV/EBITDA prices the **whole business** (equity plus debt minus cash) against operating earnings before depreciation. Use it to compare companies with different debt loads in capital-heavy sectors — pipelines, telecom, rails, industrials, producers. Make the numerator and denominator **consistent** (same period, same lease treatment, same minority-interest scope), compare only within a business model, and always cross-check with free cash flow and net debt/EBITDA.

| EV component | Include? | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Diluted market cap | Yes | Options/convertibles can matter |
| Interest-bearing debt | Yes | Claims ahead of equity |
| Lease liabilities | Yes, if EBITDA is IFRS 16 | IFRS 16 removes lease costs from EBITDA, so add the liability to EV |
| Preferred shares | Usually yes | Common in Canadian utilities, pipelines, banks, insurers |
| Hybrid/subordinated notes | Usually yes | Pipelines and utilities issue these |
| Non-controlling interests | Yes, if EBITDA consolidates 100% | Matching scope |
| Cash | Subtract | Unless it's trapped or needed for operations |

## The IFRS 16 trap (why Canadian numbers can look cheaper)

Since 2019, IFRS 16 puts most leases on the balance sheet. Lease payments that used to be an operating expense now show up as depreciation and interest — so **EBITDA rises** even though nothing changed in the business. If you compare a company's post-2019 EV/EBITDA to its own pre-2019 history, or to a US GAAP peer, without adjusting, the Canadian name can look artificially cheap.

| Hypothetical retailer | Pre-IFRS 16 view | Post-IFRS 16 view |
|:--|--:|--:|
| EBITDA ($M) | 200 | 260 (lease costs of 60 move below EBITDA) |
| EV ($M), ignoring leases | 2,000 | 2,000 |
| EV/EBITDA, inconsistent | 10.0× | **7.7× — looks cheap, isn't** |
| EV incl. lease liabilities of 500 | — | 2,500 |
| EV/EBITDA, consistent | 10.0× | 9.6× |

*Hypothetical. The fix: either add lease liabilities to EV, or subtract lease payments from EBITDA ("EBITDAaL").*

## Where EV/EBITDA fits by TSX sector

| Sector | EV/EBITDA useful? | Better or complementary lens |
|:--|:--|:--|
| [Midstream and pipelines](/sectors/midstream-pipelines/) | Yes — standard | Net debt/EBITDA, distributable cash flow per share |
| [Telecom](/sectors/telecom/) | Yes | FCF after capex and spectrum, leverage |
| [Transportation](/sectors/transportation/) (rails) | Yes | Operating ratio, P/E, FCF |
| [Oil and gas producers](/sectors/oil-gas-producers/) | Yes, at a stated price deck | EV per flowing barrel, FCF yield |
| [Utilities](/sectors/utilities/) | Partly | P/E, rate-base growth, payout |
| [Banks](/sectors/banks/), [insurers](/sectors/insurers/) | **No** | P/B, ROE, P/E ([bank valuation](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/)) |
| [REITs](/sectors/real-estate-reits/) | Sometimes | P/AFFO, NAV |
| [Software](/sectors/software-tech/) (early stage) | Often not (negative EBITDA) | EV/sales, FCF margin |

## Pair it with leverage — always

Two companies at the same EV/EBITDA can carry very different risk. A quick read:

| | Company X (hypothetical) | Company Y (hypothetical) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| EV/EBITDA | 9.0× | 9.0× |
| Net debt / EBITDA | 1.5× | 5.0× |
| Equity share of EV | ~83% | ~44% |
| Effect of a 10% EBITDA drop on equity value (same multiple) | about −12% | about −23% |

*Illustrative math: with more debt, the same drop in business value hits equity harder. That's why "same multiple" doesn't mean "same risk."*

## A DIY workflow

1. Pull EV components and EBITDA from the **same date** (latest quarter-end balance sheet, trailing 12-month EBITDA).
2. Decide reported vs adjusted EBITDA — and read the [non-GAAP bridge](/investing/analyze/read-non-gaap-metrics-critically/).
3. Compute for 3–6 [true peers](/investing/analyze/compare-two-tsx-peers/) the same way.
4. Explain the gaps (growth, quality, leverage, cyclicality) before calling anything cheap.
5. Cross-check with [P/FCF](/investing/valuation/what-p-fcf-means-canadian-diy/) and, for a sanity range, a [simple DCF](/investing/valuation/simple-dcf-without-fake-precision/).
