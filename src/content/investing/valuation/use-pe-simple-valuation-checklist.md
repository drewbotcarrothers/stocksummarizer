P/E is easy to quote and easy to misuse. A short, repeatable checklist turns it into a **disciplined first look** at valuation: it forces you to check the earnings, compare fairly, and end with a range instead of a single "fair price".

This post assumes you already know [what P/E means](/investing/valuation/what-pe-means-canadian-diy/) and [when it misleads](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/). It is educational; the worked example is hypothetical and is not a valuation of any company.

## Direct answer

Run five checks: (1) is the **EPS trustworthy** (diluted, trailing, one-time items removed, cash-backed)? (2) where is P/E versus the company's **own 5–10 year range**? (3) where is it versus the **peer median**? (4) what does the multiple **imply** about growth and risk? (5) apply a **low, mid and high multiple** to normalized EPS to get a value range. If the stock passes all five and still looks cheap, dig deeper with cash flow and balance sheet work.

## The one-page checklist

| # | Check | Pass when | Read |
|--:|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | EPS basis | Diluted, TTM, currency matched | [Calculate P/E in CAD](/investing/valuation/calculate-pe-in-cad-from-filings/) |
| 2 | Earnings quality | Operating cash flow broadly tracks net income | [CFO vs net income](/investing/analyze/read-cfo-vs-net-income/) |
| 3 | One-time items | Large unusual items removed or explained | [One-time items](/investing/analyze/spot-one-time-items-canadian-statements/) |
| 4 | Cycle position | Earnings not at a peak or trough | [Recurring vs cyclical revenue](/investing/analyze/identify-recurring-vs-cyclical-revenue/) |
| 5 | Own history | Current P/E placed in a 5–10 year range, same business mix | Your notes |
| 6 | Peers | Compared with peer median on the same basis | [P/E vs peers](/investing/valuation/pe-vs-peers-canada-first-comparison/) |
| 7 | Implied view | You can state what growth the multiple assumes | Below |
| 8 | Value range | Low, mid and high value per share written down | Below |
| 9 | Cross-check | At least one other method (EV/EBITDA, P/FCF or DCF) | [Triangulating](/investing/valuation/triangulating-multiples-dcf-peer-checks/) |

## What a P/E implies (rough guide)

Earnings yield is the inverse of P/E (1 ÷ P/E). It gives a quick way to see what the market expects.

| P/E | Earnings yield | Rough reading |
|--:|--:|:--|
| 8x | 12.5% | Market expects flat or falling earnings, or sees high risk |
| 12x | 8.3% | Modest growth, average risk |
| 16x | 6.3% | Steady growth or high quality |
| 25x | 4.0% | Strong growth expected for years |
| 40x | 2.5% | Very high growth expected; little room for disappointment |

Compare the earnings yield with a long Government of Canada bond yield. When bond yields are high, the same P/E needs more growth to make sense, because investors can earn more from bonds. This is a rough cross-check, not a formula.

## Choosing the low, mid and high multiples

| Multiple | Where it comes from |
|:--|:--|
| Low | Bottom quartile of the company's own history, or a weak peer |
| Mid | Peer median or long-run average for the company |
| High | Top quartile of history, only if quality has improved |

Use **normalized EPS** (an average or mid-cycle figure) for cyclical companies, not peak earnings. See [owner earnings](/investing/glossary/what-is-owner-earnings/) for a cash-based alternative.
