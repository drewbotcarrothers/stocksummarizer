Comparing price to owner earnings across a peer group is harder than comparing P/E, because every company defines "sustaining" capital its own way and some add back costs others keep. Put five companies' self-reported figures side by side and you are mostly comparing accounting choices. The fix is to rebuild owner earnings for every peer on **your** rules, then compare.

This guide does that for five hypothetical TSX-listed industrial services companies. The definition is in [what is owner earnings?](/investing/glossary/what-is-owner-earnings/), the worksheet is in [how to calculate owner earnings in CAD from filings](/investing/valuation/calculate-owner-earnings-in-cad-from-filings/), and the general peer method is in [how to use peer tables without cherry-picking](/investing/analyze/use-peer-tables-without-cherry-picking/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

(1) Choose peers with **similar assets and capital intensity**. (2) Rebuild owner earnings for each with **one set of rules**: the same maintenance capex method, share-based pay kept as a cost, lease principal subtracted, acquired amortization treated the same way. (3) Compare **price to owner earnings (P/OE)** with the peer median. (4) Explain gaps with **growth, reinvestment returns, leverage and capex honesty** columns. (5) Cross-check with P/FCF so you can see how much growth spending each company is doing.

## Step 1: rebuild on one set of rules

| Rule | Applied to every peer |
|:--|:--|
| Maintenance capex | Average of company "sustaining" figure, PP&E depreciation, flat-year capex ratio and a peer intensity check |
| Share-based compensation | Kept as a cost |
| Right-of-use depreciation | Added back only with lease principal subtracted |
| Acquired amortization | Added back for one-off deals; not for peers that acquire every year |
| Period and currency | Same fiscal period (TTM); C$ at one stated rate for US$ reporters |

## Step 2: the peer table

| Company (hypothetical) | P/OE, company-adjusted | P/OE, your rules | Maint. capex ÷ PP&E dep. | SBC % of OE | 5-yr OE/share growth | Net debt ÷ OE | P/FCF |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Your company | 17.0x | 20.0x | 1.08x | 11% | 6% | 3.0x | 57.5x |
| Peer A | 16.0x | 16.5x | 1.10x | 3% | 4% | 2.0x | 19.0x |
| Peer B | 14.0x | 19.0x | 0.80x | 4% | 3% | 4.5x | 21.0x |
| Peer C | 22.0x | 22.5x | 1.15x | 2% | 10% | 2.5x | 45.0x |
| Peer D | 18.0x | 21.0x | 1.05x | 14% | 7% | 3.5x | 30.0x |
| **Peer median (A–D + you)** | **17.0x** | **20.0x** | | | | | |

Two things stand out. Peer B's company-adjusted figure relied on sustaining capex well below depreciation (0.80x); on consistent rules it moves from the cheapest to the middle of the group. Peer D and your company add back share-based pay in their own non-GAAP numbers; keeping it as a cost raises their multiples.

The P/FCF column tells a different story. Peer C and your company look expensive on free cash flow (45.0x and 57.5x) because both are spending heavily on growth projects. Owner earnings strips that out, which is useful only if those projects earn a good return. That is the question to answer next.

| Gap | What could justify it | What to check |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Higher P/OE with higher growth (Peer C) | Growth capex that earns returns above the cost of capital | Return on incremental capital over 5 years; project disclosures in the MD&A |
| Higher P/OE with same growth | Lower risk: contracts, regulation, market share | Contract length, customer concentration, regulatory regime |
| P/OE near median with weak growth and high debt (Peer B) | Possibly nothing | Covenants, refinancing schedule, capex honesty |
| Lower P/OE (Peer A) | Lower growth, smaller scale | Whether the market is missing durability |

A simple way to read the scatter: draw the trend line, then ask each company above it what extra quality it has, and each company below it what risk the market sees. Peer B is above the line for its growth while carrying the most debt, so on this table it is not the cheap stock its own reporting suggests.

## Peer groups that should not share one table

| Mixed group | Problem |
|:--|:--|
| Rails with trucking or logistics | Rail maintenance capex is far larger and longer-lived |
| Regulated utilities with merchant power | Regulated capex earns an allowed return; merchant does not |
| Producers with royalty or streaming companies | Royalty companies have almost no maintenance capex, so P/OE is not comparable |
| Asset-light software with industrials | Owner earnings ≈ FCF for software; capex split adds little |
| IFRS reporters with US GAAP peers | Lease and capitalization rules differ; adjust first |

## Canada-specific notes

- Many TSX issuers in energy, mining and pipelines disclose sustaining capital; many US peers do not. Use the same estimation method for both.
- US$ reporters: build owner earnings in US$, convert per-share figures at one rate, and divide into the matching price. See [how currency affects valuation when peers report in USD](/investing/valuation/currency-valuation-peers-report-usd/).
- Income trusts and partnerships may have distributable cash flow measures; do not treat them as owner earnings without rebuilding.
