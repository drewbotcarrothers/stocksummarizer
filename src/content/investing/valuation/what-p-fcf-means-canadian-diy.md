**P/FCF** (price to free cash flow) asks how much you pay for each dollar of cash the business generates after reinvestment. It's the cash-based cousin of [P/E](/investing/valuation/what-pe-means-canadian-diy/) — harder to flatter with accounting choices, and closer to what can actually be paid out to shareholders.

Educational only — not financial advice. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

**P/FCF = share price ÷ free cash flow per share** (or market cap ÷ FCF). Its inverse, **FCF yield**, is FCF ÷ market cap. Use trailing-12-month or normalized FCF, check that your FCF definition subtracts capex and IFRS 16 lease principal, and compare within the same business model. Skip it for banks and insurers, and be careful with cyclicals and companies in heavy growth-capex phases.

## The formula and a worked example

| Input (hypothetical) | Value |
|:--|--:|
| Share price | $40.00 |
| Operating cash flow per share | $4.10 |
| − Capex per share | −$1.40 |
| − Lease principal per share | −$0.20 |
| **FCF per share** | **$2.50** |
| **P/FCF** = 40 ÷ 2.50 | **16.0×** |
| **FCF yield** = 2.50 ÷ 40 | **6.25%** |

How to build FCF from the filings: [how to analyze free cash flow](/investing/analyze/free-cash-flow-analysis/). Definition refresher: [FCF glossary](/investing/glossary/what-is-free-cash-flow/).

| P/FCF | FCF yield | How a DIY investor might read it |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 8–10× | 10–12.5% | Market expects decline, cyclical peak, or it's genuinely cheap — find out which |
| 12–18× | 5.5–8% | Common range for mature, stable businesses |
| 20–30× | 3.3–5% | Market expects growth or high quality |
| 30×+ | under 3.3% | Growth must deliver, or FCF is temporarily depressed by investment |

*Ranges are rough orientation, not targets — interest rates and sector norms shift them.*

## P/FCF vs P/E

| | P/E | P/FCF |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Denominator | Accounting earnings | Cash after reinvestment |
| Easier to flatter? | Yes — accruals, adjustments | Harder — but working-capital timing and capex deferral can do it |
| Captures capex intensity? | Only via depreciation | Directly |
| Captures dilution from stock comp? | Via share count | Via share count; SBC itself is added back in operating cash flow |
| Works for banks? | Partly | **No** |

When the two disagree a lot, the gap is the story. A company on 12× earnings but 30× FCF is either investing heavily or not converting earnings to cash. Example in [comparing two TSX peers](/investing/analyze/compare-two-tsx-peers/).

## Where P/FCF works and breaks on the TSX

| Sector | Use P/FCF? | Caveat |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Software and tech | Yes | Consider subtracting stock-based comp |
| Rails, waste, industrials | Yes | Check capex cycle |
| Telecom | Yes | Spectrum auctions make single years lumpy |
| Oil and gas producers | Yes, at a stated commodity price | Use mid-cycle FCF, not peak |
| Pipelines and utilities | With care | Big growth capex depresses FCF; distributable cash flow is the sector norm |
| REITs | Use P/AFFO | Similar idea, sector-specific definition |
| Banks and insurers | No | Use [P/B and ROE](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/) |

## Common mistakes

1. Using operating cash flow and calling it FCF (forgetting capex).
2. Forgetting IFRS 16 lease principal — it flatters Canadian FCF vs older periods.
3. Using one great year for a cyclical business.
4. Ignoring working-capital swings — one quarter of collected receivables can inflate trailing FCF.
5. Comparing across business models instead of within them.

## Triangulate

P/FCF is strongest next to [EV/EBITDA](/investing/valuation/ev-ebitda-for-canadian-diy-investors/) (capital-structure view) and P/E (earnings view), with a [simple DCF](/investing/valuation/simple-dcf-without-fake-precision/) as a sanity check.
