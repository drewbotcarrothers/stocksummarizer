**FCF yield** is free cash flow divided by what you pay for it. You have met it already: it is the inverse of P/FCF, covered in [what P/FCF means for Canadian DIY investors](/investing/valuation/what-p-fcf-means-canadian-diy/), and its unlevered cousin is the inverse of EV/FCF, covered in [what EV/FCF means](/investing/valuation/what-ev-fcf-means-canadian-diy/). This post does not rebuild free cash flow (that lives in [what is free cash flow](/investing/glossary/what-is-free-cash-flow/) and [how to analyze free cash flow for stock decisions](/investing/analyze/free-cash-flow-analysis/)). It covers what the yield form adds: **which yield** you are looking at, how FCF yield sits next to **dividend yield** as a measure of cash-return capacity, and why the **per-share** figure after stock-based compensation is the one owners keep. It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

FCF yield = **free cash flow ÷ price**. On the shareholder level it is FCF to common ÷ market cap (or FCF per share ÷ share price), the inverse of P/FCF. On the business level it is unlevered FCF ÷ enterprise value, the inverse of EV/FCF. Use the yield on price when you are pricing the shares, the yield on EV when peers carry different debt. Compare FCF yield with dividend yield: the gap is roughly what the company can spend on buybacks, debt reduction or growth without borrowing. Subtract stock-based compensation and watch the share count, because FCF per share is what you own.

| Version | Numerator | Denominator | Inverse of | Best for |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Yield on price (levered) | FCF to common shareholders | Market cap | P/FCF | Pricing the shares you buy |
| Yield on EV (unlevered) | FCF before interest, after the tax shield | Enterprise value | EV/FCF | Comparing peers with different debt |
| Owner-adjusted yield on price | FCF to common minus stock-based compensation | Diluted market cap | P/FCF after SBC | Companies that pay staff in shares |

The two main versions differ only because of debt. A company with no debt and no cash has the same yield either way. With debt, the yield on price is usually higher, because shareholders carry the debt risk; see [when FCF yield misleads](/investing/valuation/when-fcf-yield-misleads-tsx/) for the trap that creates.

The dividends ÷ FCF ratio is the [FCF coverage of dividends](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/) check; the yield form just shows it in valuation terms. A higher dividend yield is not a higher return if the FCF behind it is smaller.

## Per share, after stock-based compensation

Free cash flow adds back stock-based compensation (SBC) because it is a non-cash expense. But paying staff in shares is a real cost to existing owners: it either dilutes them or forces buybacks to offset. Two adjustments give the owner's view:

| Hypothetical | Reported | Owner-adjusted |
|:--|--:|--:|
| FCF to common | C$500M | C$500M |
| Less SBC | | −C$60M |
| FCF after SBC | C$500M | C$440M |
| Shares | 200M (weighted average) | 205M (latest diluted) |
| FCF per share | $2.50 | $2.15 |
| Price | $40.00 | $40.00 |
| **FCF yield** | **6.25%** | **5.37%** |

For a utility or a pipeline, SBC is small and the two yields barely differ. For software and some financial companies, the gap can be large. The worksheet is in [how to calculate FCF yield in CAD from filings](/investing/valuation/calculate-fcf-yield-in-cad-from-filings/).

## Where FCF yield fits

| Use it for | Avoid it for |
|:--|:--|
| Steady, cash-generative businesses | Banks and insurers (cash flow is not the operating measure) |
| Comparing with dividend yield and buybacks | Companies in a heavy build phase without a maintenance-capex view |
| Checking that earnings turn into cash | Single years with big working-capital swings |
| Ranking peers, including weak cash generators (yields go negative instead of breaking) | REITs, where AFFO is the cleaner measure; see [valuation for REITs](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/) |
