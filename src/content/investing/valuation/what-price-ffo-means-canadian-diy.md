If you have tried to put a P/E on a Canadian REIT, you have probably seen nonsense: 6x one year, 40x the next, with the same buildings and roughly the same rent. That is because IFRS net income for a REIT includes property revaluations. The multiple REIT investors use instead is **price to funds from operations (P/FFO)**, the real-estate equivalent of P/E.

This post explains what P/FFO tells you, how it differs from P/E and P/AFFO, and how to read it on TSX-listed REITs. What FFO is and how it is defined is in the glossary entry [what is FFO?](/investing/glossary/what-is-ffo/); the full FFO-to-AFFO bridge is in [valuation for REITs: NAV, FFO, AFFO explained](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

P/FFO = **unit price ÷ FFO per unit** (trailing twelve months or next year's estimate). Its inverse, **FFO yield**, is FFO per unit ÷ price. In Canada, most REITs calculate FFO using the industry association **REALPAC**'s definition, which removes fair-value changes on properties, gains on sales and a few other non-cash or non-recurring items from IFRS net income. P/FFO is the cleanest way to compare REIT **earnings power** with price, but FFO is not cash: it is before the capex and leasing costs needed to keep the buildings rented. Pair it with [P/AFFO](/investing/valuation/what-price-affo-means-canadian-diy/) and compare only REITs of the same property type.

Canadian REITs usually measure investment properties at **fair value** under IAS 40, and every change in that value runs through net income. FFO removes those swings, so the multiple tracks the rent-driven business rather than the appraisal cycle.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

| Item | Value |
|:--|--:|
| Unit price | $15.00 |
| FFO per unit (TTM) | $1.20 |
| **P/FFO** | **12.5x** |
| FFO yield | 8.0% |
| AFFO per unit (TTM) | $0.95 |
| P/AFFO | 15.8x |
| Distribution per unit | $0.84 |
| Distribution yield | 5.6% |

The gap between P/FFO (12.5x) and P/AFFO (15.8x) is the recurring spending on capex and leasing that FFO leaves out. In this example it absorbs about a fifth of FFO.

## P/FFO vs P/E vs P/AFFO

| Multiple | Numerator | What it captures | Main weakness for REITs |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| P/E | IFRS net income per unit | Everything, including revaluations | Swings with appraisals; often meaningless |
| **P/FFO** | FFO per unit | Recurring property earnings | Ignores capex and leasing costs |
| P/AFFO | AFFO per unit | Cash-like earnings after recurring capex | No single definition of the capex reserve |
| P/NAV | NAV per unit | Asset value at a cap rate | Depends on the cap rate assumed |

| Point | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| **Per unit, including exchangeable LP units** | Many Canadian REITs have Class B limited partnership units, exchangeable into trust units, that are often classified as liabilities. FFO usually adds back their distributions, so they belong in the unit count |
| **REALPAC basis, with deviations listed** | REITs reconcile FFO to net income in the MD&A as a non-GAAP measure (NI 52-112). Check for adjustments beyond the REALPAC list |
| **FFO is not cash** | Capex, tenant improvements, leasing commissions and straight-line rent are below FFO. Capex-heavy property types look cheaper on P/FFO than they are |
| **Same property type** | Industrial, multi-family, retail, office, seniors housing and diversified REITs have different growth and capex profiles, so their typical multiples differ |

## What a higher or lower P/FFO usually reflects

| Lower P/FFO often reflects | Higher P/FFO often reflects |
|:--|:--|
| Property types with high capex or vacancy risk | Property types with rent growth and low capex |
| High leverage or near-term refinancing at higher rates | Low leverage, long debt maturities |
| Weak per-unit FFO growth, dilutive issuance | Steady per-unit growth from rents and development |
| Distribution at risk (high AFFO payout) | Comfortable AFFO payout with room to grow |
| Discount to NAV the market doubts will close | Development pipeline or rent upside not in current FFO |

A low P/FFO is a question, not an answer. [How to analyze REITs differently from ordinary equities](/investing/analyze/analyze-reits-vs-ordinary-equities/) shows what to check next.

## Canadian vs US REIT FFO

US REITs use the **Nareit** FFO definition under US GAAP, where properties are carried at depreciated cost and real estate depreciation is the big add-back. Canadian REITs under IFRS mostly adjust out fair-value changes instead. The two FFOs are close in spirit but not identical, so cross-border P/FFO comparisons need care. See [how to compare a TSX stock to its US peer fairly](/investing/valuation/compare-tsx-stock-to-us-peer/).
