Every valuation multiple has a numerator and a denominator, and they need to belong to the same group of investors. Enterprise value (EV) is the value of the whole business to everyone with a claim on it: lenders, preferred shareholders, minority partners and common shareholders. Equity value is what belongs to common shareholders alone. Mix the two and you get ratios that look precise and mean nothing.

This post focuses on the bridge between EV and equity value and on when to use each. The basics of EV/EBITDA are in [EV/EBITDA for Canadian DIY investors](/investing/valuation/ev-ebitda-for-canadian-diy-investors/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

**EV = equity value + debt + other claims ahead of common shareholders (leases, preferred shares, minority interests, pension deficits) − cash and non-operating assets.** Use **EV** with measures that belong to all capital providers, before interest: **sales, EBITDA, EBIT, unlevered free cash flow**. Use **equity value** with measures that belong only to common shareholders, after interest: **net income, EPS, levered free cash flow, book value, dividends**. Use EV when comparing companies with different amounts of debt; use equity value when the question is what a share is worth.

| Bridge item (hypothetical, C$M) | Amount | Why it is there |
|:--|--:|:--|
| Market cap, fully diluted | 10,000 | Common equity at market price |
| + Debt (short and long term) | 4,000 | Lenders' claim |
| + Lease liabilities | 800 | Include if EBITDA is after IFRS 16 (excludes lease expense) |
| + Preferred shares | 500 | Claim ahead of common shareholders |
| + Non-controlling (minority) interest | 700 | EBITDA includes 100% of subsidiaries that are not wholly owned |
| + Pension deficit, after tax | 300 | Debt-like obligation |
| − Cash and equivalents | −1,200 | Available to pay down claims (keep operating cash in if needed) |
| − Equity-accounted investments | −600 | Their profit is not in EBITDA |
| **Enterprise value** | **14,500** | |

Going the other way is how you get from a DCF or EV/EBITDA valuation to a value per share: start with EV, subtract every claim, add back non-operating assets, and divide by the fully diluted share count. Each item you forget goes straight into the per-share value. The items Canadian investors most often miss are covered in [net debt adjustments Canadians often miss](/investing/valuation/net-debt-adjustments-canadians-miss/).

## Match the numerator to the denominator

| Measure | Belongs to | Pair with | Wrong pairing |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Revenue | All capital | EV/Sales | Price/Sales on a highly levered company |
| EBITDA, EBIT | All capital (before interest) | EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT | Market cap / EBITDA |
| Unlevered FCF | All capital | EV/FCF | Market cap / unlevered FCF |
| Net income, EPS | Common shareholders | P/E | EV/net income |
| Levered FCF (after interest) | Common shareholders | P/FCF, FCF yield | EV / levered FCF |
| Book value | Common shareholders | P/B | EV/book |
| Dividends | Common shareholders | Dividend yield | EV/dividends |

The consistency rule extends to the bridge items. If you add minority interest to EV, the EBITDA must include 100% of those subsidiaries (it usually does). If you subtract equity-accounted investments, EBITDA must exclude their income (it usually does). If EBITDA is before lease costs, leases must be in EV. The [IFRS quirks post](/investing/valuation/adjust-multiples-ifrs-quirks/) covers the lease case in detail.

## Why the choice changes the answer

Two hypothetical companies have the same EBITDA (C$1,500M) and the same D&A (C$400M), but very different balance sheets.

| Hypothetical | Company A | Company B |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Market cap | 10,000 | 6,000 |
| Net debt | 500 | 5,000 |
| **Enterprise value** | **10,500** | **11,000** |
| Interest expense | 25 | 250 |
| Net income ((1,500 − 400 − interest) × 73.5%) | 790 | 625 |
| **P/E** | **12.7x** | **9.6x** |
| **EV/EBITDA** | **7.0x** | **7.3x** |

B's low P/E is the market charging for leverage: more of the business's value belongs to lenders, and the equity is riskier. P/E alone would suggest B is the bargain. EV/EBITDA shows the businesses are priced about the same, and the real question is whether B's equity deserves a higher required return.

## When each one matters

| Situation | Lean on | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Comparing companies with different leverage | EV multiples | Neutral to capital structure |
| Capital-intensive sectors (pipelines, telecom, rail) | EV/EBITDA plus EV/EBIT or EV/FCF | Debt is a large part of the capital |
| Banks and insurers | Equity multiples (P/B, P/E) | Debt is raw material, not financing; EV is not meaningful |
| Takeover analysis | EV | An acquirer assumes the debt |
| What one share is worth to you | Equity value per diluted share | The final step of any valuation |
| Holding companies | Sum-of-the-parts, then equity | See [SOTP valuation](/investing/valuation/sum-of-the-parts-valuation-conglomerates-holdcos/) |
