PEG looks like a shortcut to growth-adjusted value. On the TSX, where much of the index is cyclical (energy, materials), slow-growing (utilities, telecoms) or dividend-heavy (banks, pipelines), the shortcut often points the wrong way. This post lists the **PEG-specific traps**. For the P/E side of the ratio, the traps in [when P/E misleads on TSX stocks](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/) and [when forward P/E misleads](/investing/valuation/when-forward-pe-misleads-tsx/) still apply and are not repeated here.

It is educational; examples are hypothetical and not about any specific company.

## Direct answer

PEG misleads when the **growth rate** is not a fair picture of future earnings power. The biggest traps are: growth measured from a **low base** or a **cyclical peak**; growth that comes from **buybacks, debt or acquisitions** rather than the business; growth on **adjusted** EPS only; **dividend payers** penalized for paying out profit; and the ratio's math, which treats growth as if it were linear and explodes when growth is close to zero. Use a 3–5 year window, check where the growth comes from, and never use PEG alone.

## The PEG-specific traps

| Trap | What happens | Where it shows up on the TSX | Fix |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Low-base rebound | EPS doubles after a bad year, PEG falls toward 0 | Energy, materials, airlines after a downturn | Use multi-year growth from a normal year |
| Cyclical peak | High growth into a peak; earnings may fall next | Commodity producers, chemicals, steel | Use through-cycle or mid-cycle EPS |
| Buyback-driven growth | EPS grows faster than net income | Mature companies with large buybacks | Compare EPS growth with net income growth |
| Debt-funded growth | Higher leverage lifts EPS and risk | Levered acquirers, some utilities | Check debt and interest coverage |
| Acquired growth | Deals add EPS without organic growth | Serial acquirers, roll-ups | Split organic from acquired growth |
| Adjusted EPS only | Growth excludes recurring "one-time" costs | Many issuers using non-GAAP EPS | Compare with IFRS diluted EPS growth |
| Dividend payers | Low growth because profit is paid out | Banks, pipelines, utilities, telecoms | Use PEGY: P/E ÷ (growth + yield) |
| Near-zero growth | PEG becomes very large or meaningless | Mature, regulated businesses | Use P/E, yield or P/B instead |
| Horizon mismatch | One stock on 1-year growth, another on 5-year | Mixed data sources | Same window for every company |

## Why the math itself can mislead

PEG divides by growth as if each extra point of growth is worth the same amount. In reality, the value of growth depends on how long it lasts, how much reinvestment it needs and interest rates. Two hypothetical companies show the problem:

| | Company X | Company Y |
|:--|--:|--:|
| P/E | 10.0x | 40.0x |
| Expected growth | 5% | 20% |
| PEG | 2.00 | 2.00 |
| What the PEG ignores | Steady, low-risk, high payout | Must sustain 20% for years to justify 40x |

Same PEG, very different risks. PEG also says nothing about the balance sheet, returns on capital or how certain the growth is.

## Quick tests before trusting a PEG

| Test | Pass if |
|:--|:--|
| Base year | Starting EPS was not unusually low or high |
| Path | EPS rose fairly steadily, not in one jump |
| Source of growth | Net income, not just EPS, grew; organic growth explains most of it |
| Basis | Adjusted and IFRS EPS growth are similar |
| Window | 3–5 years, the same for every company you compare |
| Payout | For high payers, PEGY tells a similar story |
| Cross-check | ROE, debt and FCF do not contradict the PEG |
