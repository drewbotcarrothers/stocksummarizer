Owner earnings tells you what a business earns for its owners before it spends anything on growth. That makes it a good base for a valuation checklist, with one condition that is easy to forget: if you value a company on owner earnings **and** assume it will grow, you must pay for that growth. Growth capex is not free just because owner earnings leaves it out.

This one-page checklist continues the hypothetical company from [how to calculate owner earnings in CAD from filings](/investing/valuation/calculate-owner-earnings-in-cad-from-filings/): owner earnings of **$2.30 per diluted share**, a **$46.00** share price, so **20.0x** price to owner earnings. The definition is in [what is owner earnings?](/investing/glossary/what-is-owner-earnings/). It is educational; all figures, including the required return, are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

(1) **Gate:** use owner earnings only where maintenance and growth spending can be separated. (2) Build owner earnings on **your own consistent rules**. (3) Pass four **quality gates**. (4) Compute the **no-growth value**: owner earnings ÷ required return. (5) Add growth only through **reinvestment × return on that capital**, and fund it out of owner earnings. (6) Produce a **bear / base / bull range**, then reverse-engineer what today's price assumes.

## Step 1: gate

| Business type | Use owner earnings? |
|:--|:--|
| Mature, capital-heavy (rails, pipelines, telecom, industrial services) | Yes, the split matters most here |
| Producers that disclose sustaining capital | Yes, after adding back anything the definition leaves out |
| Asset-light software or services | Optional; owner earnings ≈ free cash flow |
| Early-stage, loss-making, or no split possible | No; see [how to value growth stocks when earnings are negative](/investing/valuation/value-growth-stocks-negative-earnings/) |
| Banks and insurers | No; capital, not capex, is the constraint |

## Step 2 and 3: consistent basis and quality gates

| Gate (hypothetical company) | Test | Result |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Maintenance capex ÷ PP&E depreciation, 5-yr | At least about 1.0x | 1.08x, pass |
| Owner earnings ÷ net income, 5-yr | Explainable by D&A and capex mix | 1.28x, explained by acquired amortization, pass |
| Share-based pay as % of owner earnings | Below about 15% and kept as a cost | 11%, pass |
| Net debt ÷ owner earnings | Below about 4x | 3.0x, pass |

If a company fails a gate, either adjust owner earnings (for example, raise maintenance capex) or stop and use another method.

## Step 4: the no-growth value

If the company stopped growing and paid out all owner earnings forever, its value per share would be owner earnings ÷ required return.

| No-growth value (hypothetical) | Value |
|:--|--:|
| Owner earnings per share | $2.30 |
| Required return, r (illustrative) | 8.5% |
| **No-growth value (2.30 ÷ 0.085)** | **$27.06** |
| Share price | $46.00 |
| **Share of price that depends on growth** | **41%** |

That last line is the key output of this checklist: $18.94 of the $46.00 price (41%) is a bet that future growth spending will earn more than 8.5%.

## Step 5: growth that has to be paid for

Growth comes from reinvesting part of owner earnings at some return on invested capital (ROIC). If the company reinvests a share *b* of owner earnings at a return *ROIC*, growth is roughly *g = b × ROIC*, and only the rest, *(1 − b)*, is available to owners. Value per share ≈ owner earnings × (1 − b) × (1 + g) ÷ (r − g).

| Scenario (hypothetical) | Reinvested, b | ROIC on growth | Growth, g | Paid to owners | Value per share |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| No growth | 0% | n/a | 0.0% | $2.30 | $27.06 |
| Bear | 40% | 7.5% | 3.0% | $1.38 | $25.84 |
| Base | 40% | 11.0% | 4.4% | $1.38 | $35.14 |
| Bull | 45% | 13.0% | 5.85% | $1.27 | $50.53 |

The bear case is worth **less** than not growing at all. When growth capital earns less than the required return, every dollar reinvested destroys value even though owner earnings rises. This is why the reinvestment return matters more than the growth rate itself. Growth capex has averaged about 40% of owner earnings over five years for this hypothetical company, although it was 63% this year.

## Step 6: reverse check

What does $46.00 assume? Holding reinvestment at 40% and r at 8.5%, the price implies growth of about **5.3%** a year, which requires a return of about **13.3%** on growth capital. Compare that with the company's record: if returns on past growth projects (incremental operating income ÷ incremental invested capital over five years) were nearer 11%, the price is already paying for a better future than the past delivered.

| Sensitivity of base value to r | r = 8.0% | r = 8.5% | r = 9.0% |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Base case (b 40%, ROIC 11%) | $40.02 | $35.14 | $31.32 |

## Cross-checks

| Cross-check (hypothetical) | Value | Reads as |
|:--|--:|:--|
| P/E | 25.6x | Expensive on accounting earnings, partly due to acquired amortization |
| P/FCF | 57.5x | Heavy growth spending this year |
| Owner earnings yield | 5.0% | Below the 8.5% required return, so growth must deliver |
| Management's capital allocation record | Check the MD&A and past project returns | Decides whether the bull case is credible |
