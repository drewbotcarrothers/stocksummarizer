"It trades at 10x and its ten-year average is 15x, so it should go back to 15x." That sentence sounds like analysis, but it is really an assumption. Valuation multiples do revert sometimes, especially when a temporary scare passes. They also step down and stay down when the business, the interest-rate backdrop or the accounting has changed.

This post is about the second case: when not to expect reversion. The how-to of building percentile bands on a company's own history is in [how to use historical multiple ranges without anchoring blindly](/investing/valuation/historical-multiple-ranges-without-anchoring/). It is educational; every figure below is hypothetical, not a statement about any real company.

## Direct answer

Mean reversion in multiples assumes the **drivers behind the old average are still in place**: similar return on capital, similar growth, similar risk and a similar interest-rate regime. When any of those has changed, the old average is the wrong anchor. Before treating a "below average" multiple as upside, ask what has changed in **structure, rates, business mix and measurement**, and recompute what multiple today's business can justify.

## The myths

| Myth | Why it fails | What to check instead |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "Every multiple reverts to its long-run average" | Averages blend different regimes; the business may have changed | Split history at real breaks (acquisition, disruption, rate regime) |
| "Cheap vs history means cheap" | A lower multiple can be fair if returns or growth fell | Recompute a justified multiple from today's ROIC and growth |
| "The sector average is the floor" | Sector mix changes as companies enter, merge or get disrupted | Compare to a peer set you have rebuilt, not a headline index |
| "Reversion happens within a year or two" | Even valid reversion can take many years, or never arrive | Make your return case work without a re-rating |
| "The low multiple is caused by sentiment" | Sometimes the market is pricing a real structural decline | Read MD&A for market share, pricing and capital intensity trends |

## What a changed business can justify

A simple way to see how much the old average depends on old fundamentals is the value-driver form of a justified P/E:

*Justified P/E = (1 − g ÷ ROIC) ÷ (r − g)*

where g is long-run growth, ROIC is the return on new investment and r is the required return.

| Hypothetical company | Old business | Changed business |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Return on new investment (ROIC) | 14% | 8% |
| Long-run growth (g) | 4% | 2% |
| Required return (r) | 8.5% | 8.5% |
| Reinvestment rate (g ÷ ROIC) | 28.6% | 25.0% |
| **Justified P/E** | **15.9x** | **11.5x** |

The old business supported roughly the 15x to 16x that sits in the long-run average. The changed business supports about 11.5x. At 10x the stock may still be modestly cheap, but the "back to 15x" case implies a return to fundamentals that the company has not shown.

| Reason | TSX-style example (generic) | What it does to the multiple |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Structural change | A media or retail business losing share to digital rivals | Lower ROIC and growth lower the justified P/E permanently |
| Rate regime | Long-duration payers after bond yields reset higher | Higher r lowers every justified multiple; see [how interest rates change what multiples can justify](/investing/valuation/interest-rates-change-justified-multiples/) |
| Business mix | A company that sells a high-multiple division or buys a low-multiple one | The blended average no longer describes the current mix |
| Measurement | IFRS 16 lifting EBITDA, a one-off gain inflating EPS, a change in reporting currency | The ratio's denominator changed, so history is not like-for-like; see [IFRS quirks](/investing/valuation/adjust-multiples-ifrs-quirks/) |

Reversion also works in the other direction. A multiple well above history can be justified if the company has genuinely improved, for example by moving to recurring revenue. The test is the same: what changed, and is it durable?

## When reversion is a reasonable expectation

Reversion is more plausible when:

- the drop came from a **temporary, identifiable cause** (a one-quarter miss, a strike, a commodity dip) rather than a trend;
- **ROIC and margins** are intact in the latest annual filings;
- the **balance sheet** can carry the company through the soft patch;
- peers that faced the same issue earlier **recovered** without dilution or a dividend cut;
- your return case still works **without** the re-rating, with reversion as a bonus.

## A short pre-check

| Question | Where to look | Red flag |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Has return on capital fallen for three or more years? | Annual reports, segment notes | Steady ROIC decline with rising capex |
| Did the business mix change? | Acquisition and divestiture notes | Average spans pre- and post-deal years |
| Has the rate backdrop changed since the average was set? | Government of Canada bond yields | Average set mostly in a low-rate decade |
| Is the denominator clean? | [Normalized EPS](/investing/valuation/what-normalized-eps-means-canadian-diy/) | One-off items in the base year |
| Is growth guidance lower than in the past? | MD&A, investor presentations | Guidance cut without a reason you can verify |
