This is the one-page earnings-yield checklist. It assumes you know [what earnings yield means](/investing/valuation/what-earnings-yield-means-canadian-diy/), can [calculate it in CAD](/investing/valuation/calculate-earnings-yield-in-cad-from-filings/), know [when it misleads](/investing/valuation/when-earnings-yield-misleads-tsx/) and have a [peer comparison](/investing/valuation/earnings-yield-vs-peers-canada-first-comparison/). The P/E version of this checklist picks low, mid and high multiples from history and peers; see [how to use P/E in a simple valuation checklist](/investing/valuation/use-pe-simple-valuation-checklist/). This one works from the other direction: it asks **what earnings yield the business should earn** given your required return, its growth and how much it must reinvest to get that growth. It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Pass an eligibility gate (positive, reasonably stable, normalized EPS). Then set a **fair earnings yield** = (r − g) ÷ (1 − g ÷ ROE), where r is your required return, g is long-run earnings growth and ROE is the return on the equity the company reinvests. Fair price = **normalized EPS ÷ fair yield**. Run bear, base and bull cases, compare with today's price and cross-check with EBIT ÷ EV and [FCF yield](/investing/valuation/what-fcf-yield-means-canadian-diy/). The formula's main lesson: growth only adds value when it earns more than the required return.

## The earnings-yield checklist

| # | Step | Question | Pass if |
|--:|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | Eligibility | Positive EPS in most years; not a bank, insurer or REIT? | Yes |
| 2 | Normalize | Mid-cycle or three-to-five-year average, one-offs removed? | Not a peak or trough year |
| 3 | Basis | Reported diluted EPS in CAD (adjusted only as a labelled line)? | Built per the calculation post |
| 4 | Growth | Long-run growth the business can sustain? | Below nominal economic growth over the long run, or explained |
| 5 | Reinvestment | ROE on new investment estimated? | Supported by history, not hope |
| 6 | Fair yield | (r − g) ÷ (1 − g ÷ ROE), bear / base / bull | Within the peer range, or explained |
| 7 | Price range | Normalized EPS ÷ fair yield | Calculated |
| 8 | Leverage check | After-tax EBIT ÷ EV close to the earnings yield? | Gap explained |
| 9 | Cash check | FCF yield and earnings yield tell the same story? | No big contradiction |

## Why the formula has ROE in it

A company that grows by reinvesting must retain part of its earnings. If it reinvests at an ROE of 15% and grows 4%, it needs to retain 4% ÷ 15% ≈ 27% of earnings; the other 73% is available to shareholders. A fair yield is the yield that, combined with growth, delivers your required return. If ROE on new investment equals your required return, growth adds nothing and the fair earnings yield simply equals r.

| Hypothetical, r = 9% | Growth 3% | Growth 5% |
|:--|--:|--:|
| ROE 9% (equals r) | 9.0% | 9.0% |
| ROE 12% | 8.0% | 6.9% |
| ROE 18% | 7.2% | 5.5% |

Read across: faster growth lowers the fair yield (raises the fair P/E) only when ROE is above the required return. This is why a low earnings yield on a low-return business is a warning sign.

The range is wide because small changes in r − g move the answer a lot. Narrow it by checking the base case against the peer median earnings yield and against the company's own history. The same constant-growth logic, with more stages and explicit cash flows, is in [how to build a simple DCF without fake precision](/investing/valuation/simple-dcf-without-fake-precision/).

## Using a bond yield as a sanity check

A government bond yield you look up yourself can anchor r: required return ≈ a risk-free yield plus an equity risk premium. Two cautions. First, the premium is an assumption, not a market quote; write down the one you use. Second, if your r moves every time bond yields move, your fair price will too. Many DIY investors keep r fixed in a range (for example 8–10% in these hypothetical cases) and treat rate moves as a reason to revisit, not to recalculate daily.

## Red flags that override the checklist

| Red flag | Why it overrides |
|:--|:--|
| EPS well above operating cash flow for several years | Earnings quality is low; see [how to read cash flow from operations vs net income](/investing/analyze/read-cfo-vs-net-income/) |
| Growth comes from acquisitions funded with shares | Per-share growth is lower than reported growth |
| ROE propped up by high leverage | Fair yield understates risk |
| Fair yield below about half of r | The answer is mostly the growth assumption |
| Large gap between earnings yield and after-tax EBIT ÷ EV | Leverage is doing the work; see the peer post |
