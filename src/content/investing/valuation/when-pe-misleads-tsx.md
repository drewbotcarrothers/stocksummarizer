The price-to-earnings ratio is the most quoted valuation number in investing, and on the TSX it misleads more often than people expect. Canada's market is heavy in **cyclicals** (energy, materials), **asset-heavy infrastructure** (pipelines, telecom, railways), **fair-value accounting** (REITs, insurers) and **companies that report in US dollars**. Each of these bends P/E in a predictable way.

If you're new to the ratio, start with [what P/E means for Canadian DIY investors](/investing/valuation/what-pe-means-canadian-diy/). Educational only — not financial advice. Figures are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

P/E misleads when **earnings aren't a stable, cash-backed measure of what the business normally earns**. On the TSX that usually means: commodity producers at the top or bottom of the cycle, companies reporting in USD while trading in CAD, REITs and insurers with fair-value or IFRS 17 effects, capital-heavy businesses where depreciation differs from real maintenance spending, quarters with big one-offs, and holding companies. In those cases, switch to mid-cycle multiples, EV/EBITDA, P/FCF, P/B or P/AFFO.

## The seven situations at a glance

| # | Situation | Why P/E misleads | Use instead |
|--:|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | Cyclical at peak or trough | Earnings are temporarily high or low | Mid-cycle P/E, EV/EBITDA at mid-cycle prices, P/NAV |
| 2 | USD reporter, CAD price | Currency mismatch inside the ratio | Convert one side; compare like with like |
| 3 | REITs | Fair-value gains and losses swing net income | [P/AFFO and P/NAV](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/) |
| 4 | Insurers | IFRS 17 and investment-mark volatility | P/B with ROE, core/operating EPS |
| 5 | Heavy depreciation | D&A ≠ maintenance capex | [EV/EBITDA](/investing/valuation/ev-ebitda-for-canadian-diy-investors/), [P/FCF](/investing/valuation/what-p-fcf-means-canadian-diy/) |
| 6 | One-offs (impairments, gains) | Reported EPS isn't recurring | Normalized EPS — reconcile it yourself |
| 7 | Holding companies | Earnings mix of owned stakes, marks, fees | Sum-of-the-parts, discount to NAV |

## 1. Cyclicals: low P/E at the top, high P/E at the bottom

For oil and gas producers, miners, fertilizer and forestry companies, earnings follow commodity prices. When prices are high, earnings are high and the P/E looks low — just when the risk of a downturn is highest. At the bottom, earnings collapse and the P/E looks absurdly high, often near the best entry point.

*Using a mid-cycle EPS (an average over a full cycle, or EPS at a long-run commodity price) gives a steady 12× — a more useful starting point.* Our [oil & gas producers](/sectors/oil-gas-producers/), [base metals](/sectors/base-metals-mining/) and [gold](/sectors/gold-precious-metals/) sector pages show how cyclical multiples swing.

## 2. Currency mismatch

Plenty of TSX-listed companies report in **US dollars**. Data sites usually handle the conversion, but not always — and if you compute P/E yourself from the financial statements, you must convert. A CAD price divided by USD EPS overstates the P/E by the exchange rate.

| Hypothetical | Value |
|:--|--:|
| TSX price | C$54.00 |
| EPS (reported in USD) | US$3.00 |
| Naive P/E (mixed currencies) | 18.0× |
| USD/CAD rate (hypothetical) | 1.35 |
| EPS in CAD | C$4.05 |
| **Correct P/E** | **13.3×** |

## 3. REITs

Fair-value changes on investment properties run through IFRS net income, so a REIT's "EPS" can swing wildly without any change in rent. Use FFO/AFFO-based multiples and NAV — see [valuing REITs](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/) and the [REIT sector page](/sectors/real-estate-reits/).

## 4. Insurers

Since IFRS 17 took effect in 2023, insurer earnings are presented differently, and investment-market movements can still create noise. Many Canadian insurers also publish "core" or "underlying" earnings (non-GAAP). Compare P/B with sustainable ROE as you would for a bank ([bank valuation](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/)), and see the [insurers sector page](/sectors/insurers/).

## 5. Depreciation that doesn't match reality

Pipelines, telecoms, railways and utilities book large depreciation charges. If depreciation is **higher** than the true maintenance spending (common for long-lived assets), earnings understate cash generation and P/E looks expensive. If it's **lower** (assets that need constant reinvestment, such as some telecom networks), P/E flatters. Compare D&A with sustaining capex in the cash-flow statement, then use [free cash flow](/investing/glossary/what-is-free-cash-flow/) or EV/EBITDA. See the [midstream](/sectors/midstream-pipelines/) and [telecom](/sectors/telecom/) pages.

## 6. One-offs

Impairments, asset-sale gains, restructuring charges, tax-rate changes, legal settlements and mark-to-market moves can dominate a single year. Use normalized earnings — but build the normalization yourself from the reconciliation, because management's "adjusted EPS" tends to remove only bad items. [Reading non-GAAP metrics critically](/investing/analyze/read-non-gaap-metrics-critically/) covers this.

## 7. Holding companies

Conglomerates and holding companies own stakes in other businesses, sometimes marked to market and sometimes equity-accounted. Consolidated EPS mixes all of it. A sum-of-the-parts valuation compared with the market cap (the "holdco discount") is usually more informative than P/E.

## When P/E is fine

P/E works reasonably well for stable, profitable businesses whose earnings track cash: many grocers and consumer staples, waste and services companies, and banks *as one input* alongside P/B. Even then, compare it with the company's own history and peers, and cross-check with a DCF — see [triangulating multiples, DCF and peer checks](/investing/valuation/triangulating-multiples-dcf-peer-checks/).
