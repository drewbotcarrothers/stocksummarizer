Comparing cyclical companies on trailing P/E is one of the easiest ways to get a peer table backwards. The company at the top of its cycle shows the lowest P/E; the one in a trough shows the highest. Put every peer on normalized EPS, built the same way, and the ranking often flips.

This guide builds a fair normalized P/E peer table for TSX-listed cyclicals and adds the columns that explain the gaps. The general peer method is in [how to use peer tables without cherry-picking](/investing/analyze/use-peer-tables-without-cherry-picking/); the ordinary P/E comparison is in [P/E vs peers](/investing/valuation/pe-vs-peers-canada-first-comparison/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Build normalized EPS for **every peer with the same rules**: the same full-cycle window, the same treatment of one-offs, share-based pay and acquired-intangible amortization, a statutory tax rate, interest on today's net debt and **today's diluted shares**. Rank P/E on that EPS against the **peer median**. Then explain the gaps with **mid-cycle margin**, **margin volatility**, **leverage at mid-cycle** and **current EPS ÷ normalized EPS** (where each company sits in its cycle). A company that is cheap on normalized P/E and also has the most volatile margins and the most debt is probably fairly priced.

## Alignment rules

| Rule | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| Same industry and cycle | Lumber, steel, cement and chemicals peak at different times |
| Same window, covering a full cycle | A peak-heavy window for one peer flatters it |
| Same one-off rules | Recurring "one-time" costs kept for everyone, or for no one |
| Same tax and interest approach | Statutory rate; today's net debt at today's rates |
| Today's diluted shares | Peers that issued stock look better on old counts |
| Same currency | Several TSX cyclicals report in US dollars |
| Margin × revenue on today's capacity where history is distorted | Acquisitions and closures change the business |

**Current EPS ÷ normalized** is the cycle-position column. Above 1.0, the company is earning more than in a typical year (Peer A, Peer C); below 1.0, less (Peer B, Peer D, your company). Trailing P/E punishes companies in a trough and rewards those at a peak, which is exactly backwards for a long-term holder.

## Leverage changes the story at the trough

Normalized P/E looks through the cycle, but creditors do not. A company with 2.5x net debt to mid-cycle EBITDA can carry well over 4x at the trough, which may force asset sales or equity issues at the worst time. That risk is part of why Peer B and Peer D trade at discounts. Check trough leverage and interest coverage with [how to analyze interest coverage for levered TSX stocks](/investing/analyze/analyze-interest-coverage-levered-tsx/).

| Your company | 1.5x | 2.5x |
| Peer B | 2.5x | 4.2x |
| Peer C | 0.5x | 0.8x |

## Canada-first issues

| Issue | What to do |
|:--|:--|
| US-dollar reporters (common in forest products, metals and energy) | Normalize in US$ and convert at one rate for all peers |
| Softwood lumber duties paid on US exports | Treat as an ongoing cost unless refunds are certain; refunds are one-offs |
| Canadian vs US peers | IFRS vs US GAAP; check impairment and inventory accounting before mixing |
| Canadian combined statutory tax rates differ by province | Use each company's own statutory rate from its tax note |
