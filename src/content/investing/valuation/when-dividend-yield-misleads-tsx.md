As a valuation signal, dividend yield has one big weakness: its denominator is the share price. When the price falls, the yield rises, and a rising yield looks like value. Often it is the market's forecast that the dividend will be cut. This post covers the ways yield misleads when you use it to judge whether a stock is cheap.

The safety checks themselves (payout ratios, FCF coverage, debt, the classic trap list) are covered elsewhere and not repeated: [how to avoid dividend traps on the TSX](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/), [why yield alone is a terrible screen](/investing/dividends/why-yield-alone-is-a-terrible-screen/), [payout ratio explained](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/) and [FCF coverage of dividends](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/). The concept is in [what dividend yield means](/investing/valuation/what-dividend-yield-means-canadian-diy/) and the worksheet in [how to calculate dividend yield in CAD from filings](/investing/valuation/calculate-dividend-yield-in-cad-from-filings/). It is educational; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Dividend yield misleads as a valuation measure when the yield is high **because the price fell** on bad news, when the trailing figure includes **special or variable** payouts or predates a **cut**, when the dividend and price are in **different currencies**, when a "distribution" is partly **return of capital**, when the company's **history no longer applies** (a merger, spin-off or new rate environment) and when **buybacks** or share issuance are ignored. Ask why the yield is where it is before reading it as cheap.

A yield far above the company's own band and its peers is a question: what does the market know or fear? Check recent results, guidance, credit ratings and the payout policy language in the MD&A before treating it as a discount.

## Trap 2: the wrong dividend in the numerator

| Problem | Effect on yield | Fix |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Trailing yield includes a special dividend | Overstated | Regular dividend only |
| Base-plus-variable policy (common among energy producers) | Swings with commodity prices | Base yield, variable as a range |
| Trailing yield after a cut was announced | Overstated | Latest declared rate |
| Trailing yield after a raise | Understated | Latest declared rate |
| US-dollar dividend over a Canadian-dollar price | Understated | Same currency |

## Trap 3: distributions that are partly your own money

REITs, some income funds and some corporations pay distributions that include **return of capital**. A return of capital is not income; it reduces your adjusted cost base. A 7% distribution yield with a large return-of-capital share is a different thing from a 7% dividend yield funded by earnings. The tax breakdown is published each year; see [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/) and [adjusted cost base tracking](/investing/accounts/adjusted-cost-base-tracking-diy-portfolios/).

## Trap 4: history that no longer applies

Comparing today's yield with a ten-year average assumes the company and the rate environment are comparable. They often are not.

| Change | Why the old yield band breaks |
|:--|:--|
| Merger or large acquisition | Different business mix and payout capacity |
| Spin-off | Dividend split between two companies |
| Payout policy reset | A new target ratio changes the "normal" yield |
| Interest rates much higher or lower than during the history | Income stocks reprice against bonds |
| Business in structural decline | A higher yield may be the new normal, not a discount |

## Trap 5: ignoring buybacks and dilution

Dividend yield counts only one way of returning cash. **Shareholder yield** adds net buybacks (buybacks minus shares issued, including through DRIPs and compensation).

| Hypothetical | Company A | Company B |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Dividend yield | 5.0% | 2.0% |
| Net buyback yield | −1.0% (issuing shares via a discounted DRIP) | +3.0% |
| **Shareholder yield** | **4.0%** | **5.0%** |

Company A looks like the better income stock but returns less cash per share overall. See [how to read share buyback disclosures in Canada](/investing/analyze/read-share-buyback-disclosures-canada/) and [how to spot dilution risk](/investing/analyze/spot-dilution-risk-canadian-issuers/).

## Trap 6: comparing yields across sectors

Pipelines, utilities, telecoms and banks have different payout norms, capital needs and growth rates, so a yield that is high for one sector can be normal for another. Compare yields within a peer group and next to growth; across sectors, use yield + growth or a cash-flow multiple instead.

## 60-second check

| Question | If the answer is yes |
|:--|:--|
| Has the price fallen sharply in the last year? | Find out why before reading the yield as cheap |
| Any special, variable or recently changed dividend? | Use the latest regular declared rate |
| Dividend and price in different currencies? | Convert one side |
| Is part of the payout return of capital? | Read the tax breakdown |
| Has the company merged, split or reset its policy? | Do not use the old yield band |
| Is it issuing shares through a DRIP or compensation? | Check shareholder yield |
