Book value is what shareholders' equity is worth on the balance sheet. Tangible book value strips out the parts you cannot touch: **goodwill** from past acquisitions and **other intangible assets** such as software, customer relationships and brands. What remains is a stricter measure of the equity cushion, and it is the version bank and insurance analysts tend to quote.

This page explains what tangible book value (TBV) is, how price to tangible book (P/TBV) differs from price to book, and where it is and is not useful for Canadian DIY investors. The general P/B framework is in [what P/B means](/investing/valuation/what-pb-means-canadian-diy/); bank-specific valuation is in [valuing Canadian banks with P/B and ROE](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

**Tangible common equity = common shareholders' equity − goodwill − other intangible assets.** Divide by common shares outstanding at period end for **tangible book value per share**, then divide the share price by it for **P/TBV**. Pair it with **return on tangible common equity (ROTCE)**, because a multiple of book only makes sense next to the return that book earns. TBV is most useful for **banks, lenders and insurers**, where regulators already deduct goodwill and intangibles from capital, and as a cross-check for **acquisitive companies**. It is close to useless for asset-light businesses, where TBV is often tiny or negative.

## Why strip out goodwill and intangibles?

| Reason | What it means in practice |
|:--|:--|
| Goodwill is not separable | Under IFRS 3 it is the premium paid over the fair value of an acquired business's net assets. It cannot be sold on its own |
| Intangibles may not survive stress | Capitalized software or customer lists may be worth far less to a buyer in a downturn |
| Regulators deduct them | OSFI's Capital Adequacy Requirements deduct goodwill and other intangibles (net of related deferred tax liabilities) from a bank's Common Equity Tier 1 capital |
| It levels acquirers and organic growers | A bank that grew by buying others carries goodwill; one that grew branch by branch does not |
| Impairments are one-way | Under IAS 36, goodwill impairment cannot be reversed, so book value can fall in steps after a bad deal; TBV is unaffected by the write-down because the goodwill was already excluded |

## P/B vs P/TBV vs ROTCE

| Measure | Formula | Best for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| P/B | Price ÷ book value per share | Quick screen; companies with little goodwill |
| P/TBV | Price ÷ tangible book value per share | Banks, lenders, insurers, acquisitive financials |
| ROE | Net income to common ÷ average common equity | Pairs with P/B |
| ROTCE | (Net income to common + after-tax amortization of acquired intangibles) ÷ average tangible common equity | Pairs with P/TBV |

In the example, net income to common of $99M and $7M of after-tax amortization of acquired intangibles give an ROE of about 11.3% on $880M but an ROTCE of about 16.8% on $630M. The higher return on a smaller base is exactly why P/TBV is higher than P/B: both views can describe a fairly priced company. A useful rule of thumb links them: **justified P/TBV ≈ (ROTCE − g) ÷ (r − g)**, where r is your required return and g long-run growth. The full method is in the [tangible book checklist](/investing/valuation/use-tangible-book-simple-valuation-checklist/).

## Canada-first notes

- **Limited recourse capital notes (LRCNs)** and preferred shares issued by Canadian banks and insurers are classified as equity. Remove them before calculating common equity, or TBV per share is overstated.
- Several Canadian banks report **ROTCE** or a similar return on tangible equity as a non-GAAP ratio in their MD&A. Under NI 52-112 they must reconcile it, so read the definition before comparing banks.
- Some TSX-listed financials report in **US dollars**. Convert TBV at the period-end exchange rate before dividing a C$ share price by it.
