"It trades at 11× and its US peer trades at 16× — the Canadian one is cheap." You'll hear versions of that line about banks, railways, grocers, insurers and software names. Sometimes it's right. Often the gap shrinks or disappears once you compare like with like. This guide gives Canadian DIY investors a repeatable way to do a **fair cross-border comparison**.

Educational only — not financial advice. Worked numbers are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Before comparing any multiple across the border, align six things: **currency, accounting standard, lease treatment, share count and classes, tax and payout structure, and business mix**. Then compare two or three multiples that suit the industry (EV/EBITDA, P/FCF, P/B for banks) rather than P/E alone. A "Canadian discount" that survives those adjustments is worth investigating; one that doesn't was an accounting artefact.

### 1. Currency

Many TSX issuers report in **US dollars** (common among miners, energy producers, and some industrials and financials) while their shares trade in Canadian dollars. Mixing a CAD share price with USD earnings inflates or deflates every per-share multiple by the exchange rate.

| Check | What to do |
|:--|:--|
| Reporting currency | Read the first page of the financial statements |
| Trading currency | TSX price is CAD; an interlisted NYSE price is USD |
| Fix | Convert price *or* results so both sides of the multiple use one currency |

### 2. IFRS vs US GAAP

Most Canadian issuers report under **IFRS**; US issuers use **US GAAP**. Some Canadian companies — notably several pipelines and railways — report under US GAAP, which makes their comparison with US peers cleaner. Differences that matter for valuation include lease accounting, development-cost capitalization, some impairment reversals (allowed under IFRS, not US GAAP) and the classification of interest and dividends in the cash-flow statement.

### 3. Leases: the EBITDA trap

Under **IFRS 16**, almost all leases sit on the balance sheet and their cost shows up as depreciation and interest — *below* EBITDA. Under US GAAP (**ASC 842**), operating-lease cost generally stays in operating expenses — *above* EBITDA. A lease-heavy Canadian retailer, airline or restaurant company will therefore show **higher EBITDA** than an identical US peer.

| Hypothetical, $M | Canadian co. (IFRS 16) | US peer (US GAAP) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Revenue | 5,000 | 5,000 |
| Lease cost charged above EBITDA | 0 | 150 |
| Reported EBITDA | 800 | 650 |
| Equity + net debt | 5,200 | 5,200 |
| Lease liabilities | 1,050 | (off EBITDA basis) |
| **EV/EBITDA, consistent basis** | 6,250 ÷ 800 = **7.8×** | 5,200 ÷ 650 = **8.0×** |
| **EV/EBITDA, the common mistake** (Canadian EV without leases) | 5,200 ÷ 800 = **6.5×** | 8.0× |

*Two near-identical businesses. Done consistently, the multiples are within a few tenths of each other. Forget to add IFRS lease liabilities to the Canadian EV and you "discover" a 1.5-turn discount that isn't real.* The [EV/EBITDA guide](/investing/valuation/ev-ebitda-for-canadian-diy-investors/) covers building EV consistently.

### 4. Share count and classes

Use **diluted** shares on both sides. Watch for dual-class structures (more common on the TSX) and exchangeable units or partnership interests that don't appear in the headline share count but do share in the economics.

### 5. Tax and payout structure

Corporate tax rates and the mix between dividends and buybacks differ. US companies often return more cash through buybacks; many Canadian companies favour dividends. Compare **total shareholder yield** (dividends + net buybacks ÷ market cap), not dividend yield alone. And remember investor-level tax: a Canadian holding a US stock faces withholding tax on dividends — see [withholding tax on US dividends](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/).

### 6. Business mix and market structure

A Canadian bank, telecom or grocer often operates in a more concentrated domestic market than its US counterpart. That can justify a *higher* multiple (stability) or a *lower* one (limited growth runway, regulatory risk). Segment and geography mix matter too: a Canadian company with a large US segment is partly a US business already.

| Adjustment | Effect on Canadian multiple | Effect on US multiple |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Convert USD-reported EBITDA at today's rate | +0.4× | — |
| Put leases on one basis (EV and EBITDA) | +0.5× | −0.8× |
| Include exchangeable units / dilution | +0.3× | −0.5× |
| **Total** | **9.0× → 10.2×** | **12.5× → 11.2×** |

The remaining one-turn gap might reflect slower growth, higher leverage or just less investor attention on the TSX. Your job is to decide which — and whether it's likely to persist.

## Pick the right multiple for the industry

| Industry | Better cross-border multiple | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Banks | P/B and ROE | Earnings depend on credit cycle; see [valuing banks](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/) |
| Railways, pipelines | EV/EBITDA, P/FCF | Capital-intensive; D&A distorts P/E |
| REITs | P/AFFO, P/NAV | Fair-value accounting; see [REIT valuation](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/) |
| Software | EV/sales with growth and margins, P/FCF | Stock comp and early-stage losses |
| Miners and producers | EV/EBITDA at mid-cycle prices, P/NAV | Commodity cycle — see [when P/E misleads](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/) |

## A reusable checklist

1. Same currency for price and results?
2. Same accounting basis — or adjusted for leases and one-offs?
3. Diluted shares, all classes and exchangeable units?
4. Same fiscal period (Canadian banks' fiscal year ends October 31; many US peers use the calendar year)?
5. Same multiple, computed the same way on both sides?
6. Gap explained by growth, returns on capital, leverage or market structure?

Record the result in the valuation block of your [stock brief](/investing/analyze/stock-brief-template-bull-bear-watch/) and see [comparing two TSX peers](/investing/analyze/compare-two-tsx-peers/) for the same-market version of this exercise.
