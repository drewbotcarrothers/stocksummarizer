**Earnings yield** is P/E turned upside down: earnings per share divided by the share price. It carries the same information as P/E, but the yield form has three practical advantages for DIY investors: it handles losses without breaking, it averages and ranks more sensibly, and it invites a comparison with interest rates (which is useful only if you know where that comparison breaks).

The earnings side is built exactly as in the P/E set, so this post does not repeat it: see [what P/E means for Canadian DIY investors](/investing/valuation/what-pe-means-canadian-diy/) and [how to calculate P/E in CAD from filings](/investing/valuation/calculate-pe-in-cad-from-filings/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Earnings yield = **EPS ÷ share price** = 1 ÷ P/E. A stock at 20x earnings has a 5% earnings yield; at 10x, 10%. It tells you how much accounting profit each dollar of share price buys today, before any growth. Use it to rank and compare (including loss-makers, which get a negative yield instead of a meaningless P/E), and use the **EBIT ÷ EV** variant when peers carry different debt. Comparing an earnings yield with a bond or GIC yield is tempting but only loosely valid: earnings are not paid out like a coupon, can grow or shrink, and carry business risk.

## Why the yield form is handy

| Situation | P/E | Earnings yield |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Company with a small loss | Undefined or shown as "n/m" | Negative, and it sorts below every profitable peer |
| Company with near-zero profit | Huge P/E that wrecks an average | Near zero, sits quietly at the bottom |
| Averaging a peer group | Skewed by one extreme multiple | Linear, so averages and medians behave |
| Comparing with a required return | Needs conversion | Already a percentage |

The ranking advantage is why screens and academic studies often use earnings yield rather than P/E. The [peer comparison](/investing/valuation/earnings-yield-vs-peers-canada-first-comparison/) uses it that way.

## Earnings yield vs bond and GIC yields

The common shortcut is "a 6% earnings yield beats a 4% bond yield, so stocks are cheap." The comparison has some logic (both are a return per dollar invested) but four differences make it loose.

| Difference | Earnings yield | Bond or GIC yield |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Paid to you? | No: part is paid as dividends, the rest is reinvested or used for buybacks | Yes, as coupons or interest |
| Grows? | Can grow with the business and inflation, or fall | Fixed in dollars until maturity |
| Risk | Business risk; earnings can disappear | Credit risk only (none for insured GICs within limits) |
| Accounting | IFRS profit with estimates and one-offs | Contractual |

A useful way to use it: treat the gap between an earnings yield and a government bond yield you look up yourself as a rough, **changing** risk premium, and compare that gap with the company's own history. Do not treat any single gap as proof of cheapness. The traps are in [when earnings yield misleads on TSX stocks](/investing/valuation/when-earnings-yield-misleads-tsx/).

## The EBIT ÷ EV variant

Earnings yield is measured after interest, so two identical businesses with different debt show different yields. **EBIT ÷ EV** (operating profit divided by enterprise value) fixes that by putting all capital providers on one side, the same idea as [EV/EBITDA](/investing/valuation/ev-ebitda-for-canadian-diy-investors/). It differs from EV/EBITDA in one important way: EBIT is **after depreciation and amortization**, so it charges asset-heavy businesses for wearing out their assets. It is also before tax, which matters when comparing companies with different tax rates.

## Where earnings yield fits

| Use it for | Avoid it for |
|:--|:--|
| Ranking a peer group, including small loss-makers | Deep cyclicals at peak or trough earnings without normalizing |
| A quick sense of "profit per dollar" before growth | Banks and insurers, where book value and ROE matter more |
| Comparing with your own required return | REITs, where FFO and AFFO replace EPS; see [valuation for REITs](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/) |
| Spotting when the market prices in a lot of growth (low yield) | Companies where cash flow and earnings diverge a lot; use [FCF yield](/investing/valuation/what-fcf-yield-means-canadian-diy/) |
