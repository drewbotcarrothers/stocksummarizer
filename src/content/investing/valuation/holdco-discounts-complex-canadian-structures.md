Canada has a long tradition of holding companies: family-controlled groups, insurance-led conglomerates, asset managers with listed affiliates and companies that own controlling stakes in other public companies. Many of them trade below the value of what they own. That gap, the **holdco discount**, is one of the most debated numbers in Canadian investing.

How to build the sum-of-the-parts value itself is covered in [sum-of-the-parts valuation for conglomerates and holding companies](/investing/valuation/sum-of-the-parts-valuation-conglomerates-holdcos/). This post focuses on the **discount**: how to measure it properly, what drives it and when it might narrow. It is educational; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

A holdco discount is the gap between a holding company's **market value** and the **net asset value (NAV) of its holdings** after holdco-level debt and cash. Measure it against an **adjusted NAV** that also subtracts the capitalised cost of running the head office. Persistent discounts usually reflect **cost leakage, tax on embedded gains, control and governance concerns, and doubts about how future cash will be used**, plus complexity and lower liquidity. A discount is not automatically a bargain; it narrows when a driver changes (buybacks at the discount, simplification, asset sales, a change in control), not just because it is wide.

| Driver | Why investors discount | Evidence to look for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Cost leakage | Head office costs, management fees or performance fees come out before shareholders benefit | Holdco expense line; fee agreements with related managers |
| Tax friction | Selling or distributing holdings could trigger tax on large accumulated gains | Deferred tax liabilities in the notes |
| Control and governance | Multiple-voting shares or a controlling family limit outside influence | See [how to value dual-class share structures](/investing/valuation/value-dual-class-share-structures/) and [related-party disclosures](/investing/analyze/read-related-party-disclosures/) |
| Capital allocation | Cash from holdings may fund new ventures instead of returning to shareholders | Track record of acquisitions; see [M&A track records](/investing/analyze/analyze-ma-track-records-serial-acquirers/) |
| Complexity and liquidity | Harder to analyse; fewer analysts; smaller float | Number of segments and listed affiliates |
| Double leverage | Debt at both the holdco and the subsidiaries | Parent-only and consolidated debt compared |

## What can narrow it

| Catalyst | Mechanism |
|:--|:--|
| Buybacks below NAV | Each share repurchased at a discount raises NAV per remaining share; see [buybacks per-share math](/investing/valuation/buybacks-per-share-valuation-math/) |
| Simplification | Collapsing layers, buying out minorities or listing a hidden asset |
| Spin-offs or asset sales | Turning a holding into cash or directly held shares; see [spin-offs and corporate separations](/investing/analyze/evaluate-spin-offs-corporate-separations/) |
| Lower costs | Smaller head office or fee changes reduce leakage |
| Change in control or succession | Can lead to strategy changes, for better or worse |

## Read the history, briefly

Look at the discount over 5–10 years before judging today's level. If it has ranged between 15% and 35% with no structural change, a 20% discount is middle of the range, not a signal. If it has widened after a costly acquisition or a fee change, the market may be pricing a real deterioration. Use percentile thinking rather than a single average; see [how to use historical multiple ranges without anchoring](/investing/valuation/historical-multiple-ranges-without-anchoring/).

## Common mistakes

- Using headline NAV and ignoring head office costs.
- Counting subsidiary cash as if the holdco could freely use it.
- Assuming the discount will close "eventually" with no catalyst.
- Mixing book values for private holdings with market values for listed ones without saying so.
