Most valuation work assumes a company keeps operating. Liquidation value asks a harder question: if the business stopped, sold its assets and paid its creditors in order, what would be left for each class of investor? For healthy companies the answer barely matters. For a company in distress, with covenant breaches, a going-concern warning or a strategic review, it can be the most useful number you have, mostly because it tells you who gets paid and who does not.

This post explains liquidation thinking as a literacy tool: haircuts by asset class, the order claims are paid, and why common shareholders usually sit at the end of the line. It is educational only; it is not a recommendation to buy or sell any distressed security, and every figure is hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Liquidation value = **realistic sale proceeds of the assets** (book value after haircuts) **minus wind-down costs**, then paid out **in priority order**: secured lenders, priority claims (such as certain employee and government amounts), unsecured creditors, then preferred shares, then common shares. Hard assets like cash and receivables recover most of their book value; inventory and equipment recover less; goodwill and intangibles usually recover close to nothing. In most real Canadian insolvencies the money runs out before it reaches common equity, which is why a low price-to-book ratio is not, on its own, a margin of safety.

The exact order is set by Canadian insolvency law (the *Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act* and, for larger restructurings, the *Companies' Creditors Arrangement Act*) and by each debt agreement. A restructuring is often negotiated rather than a straight liquidation, but the liquidation waterfall is the reference point every party bargains against.

## How to use it as an investor

1. **Rebuild the balance sheet with haircuts.** Work line by line from the notes, not from the summary balance sheet.
2. **List every claim ahead of common shares.** Include leases, preferred shares and off-balance-sheet guarantees; see [net debt adjustments Canadians often miss](/investing/valuation/net-debt-adjustments-canadians-miss/) and [off-balance-sheet commitments](/investing/analyze/analyze-off-balance-sheet-commitments/).
3. **Check security and covenants.** Who holds a lien on which assets? See [how to read debt notes and covenant language](/investing/analyze/read-debt-notes-covenant-language/).
4. **Compare recovery to market prices.** If the bonds trade at 40 cents and your waterfall says unsecured creditors recover 50, the market is telling you something about asset values or timing you should take seriously.
5. **Treat the equity as an option, not a floor.** If the waterfall leaves nothing for common shares, any value in the stock depends on the company avoiding liquidation altogether.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating low price-to-book as a margin of safety.** Book value includes goodwill, and creditors come first.
- **Using book value for specialised equipment.** A half-built plant may be worth scrap value to anyone else.
- **Ignoring time and cost.** Liquidations take months or years, and fees come off the top.
- **Forgetting dilution in a restructuring.** Creditors often swap debt for most of the new equity; see [how dilution changes per-share valuation math](/investing/valuation/dilution-per-share-valuation-math/).
