Owner earnings is popular with long-term investors because it asks the right question: how much cash could this business hand its owners each year without shrinking? The trouble is that the answer depends on judgment calls the company does not make for you. On TSX stocks, where capital-heavy sectors such as rails, pipelines, telecom, forestry, mining and energy make up much of the index, a small change in a maintenance capex estimate can move the result by nearly a third.

This post covers the common ways owner earnings misleads and how to check each one. The definition is in [what is owner earnings?](/investing/glossary/what-is-owner-earnings/) and the worksheet is in [how to calculate owner earnings in CAD from filings](/investing/valuation/calculate-owner-earnings-in-cad-from-filings/). It is educational; all figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Owner earnings misleads when (1) **maintenance capex is set too low**, often by adopting management's "sustaining" definition; (2) **inflation** makes historical-cost depreciation a poor guide to replacement spending; (3) **add-backs** creep in: share-based compensation, right-of-use depreciation without lease payments, or acquired amortization at a company that must keep acquiring; (4) **one year** is treated as typical when margins or capex were unusual; and (5) **working capital** needs are ignored. Each one makes the business look cheaper than it is.

## How much the judgment calls matter

Take the hypothetical company from the calculate post: owner earnings of C$230M, 100M diluted shares, a C$46 share price, so 20.0x price to owner earnings.

## Trap 1: maintenance capex that is too thin

Companies define "sustaining" or "maintenance" capital themselves. A miner's sustaining capital may exclude capitalized waste stripping or new tailings capacity; an energy producer's may exclude the drilling needed to offset decline; a retailer's may exclude store refreshes that competitors force on it. These are non-GAAP measures under NI 52-112, so the definition and reconciliation must be disclosed, but the company chooses what goes in.

| Check | What to look for |
|:--|:--|
| Sustaining capex ÷ PP&E depreciation, 5 years | Persistently below 1.0x while volumes hold flat is a warning |
| Average asset age (accumulated depreciation ÷ gross PP&E) | Rising age means spending is not keeping up |
| Definition changes | Items moved from "sustaining" to "growth" between years |
| Production or volume trend | If volume falls while "sustaining" spending is called sufficient, it was not |

## Trap 2: inflation and replacement cost

Depreciation is based on what an asset cost when it was bought. Replacing it costs today's price.

| Hypothetical asset | Value |
|:--|--:|
| Original cost, 10 years ago | C$1,000M |
| Useful life | 20 years |
| Annual depreciation | C$50M |
| Replacement cost today (35% higher) | C$1,350M |
| Depreciation at replacement cost | C$67.5M |
| **Shortfall if you use depreciation as the maintenance proxy** | **C$17.5M a year** |

This matters most for long-lived assets (rail track, pipe, towers, mills) where the gap between purchase and replacement is decades. It is one reason Buffett described owner earnings as an estimate that requires judgment.

## Trap 3: add-backs that are real costs

- **Share-based compensation.** It dilutes owners. Adding it back makes owner earnings look better than the cash owners keep. Keep it as a cost, or subtract the cash spent on buybacks to offset dilution.
- **Right-of-use depreciation.** Under IFRS 16 it replaces rent. If you add it back, subtract lease principal payments.
- **Acquired amortization at serial acquirers.** Adding it back is right for a one-off acquisition. If the business needs to keep buying companies to stand still, average acquisition spending is closer to maintenance capex. See [serial software acquirers on the TSX](/investing/analyze/analyze-serial-software-acquirers-tsx/).

## Trap 4: one year is not a business

Owner earnings from a peak year in lumber, metals or oil, or a year when the company deferred maintenance, is not a base for valuation. Average over a cycle or use mid-cycle margins, as in [how to calculate normalized EPS](/investing/valuation/calculate-normalized-eps-in-cad-from-filings/). Also watch for capex that was simply pushed into next year; the MD&A capital program section usually says so.

## Trap 5: working capital and other cash needs

A distributor or manufacturer whose prices rise with inflation needs more inventory and receivables just to sell the same volume. Ignoring that understates the cash needed. Other omissions to check: reclamation and decommissioning spending (IAS 37 provisions) for miners and producers, and pension contributions above the expense (see [evaluating pension and long-term liabilities](/investing/analyze/evaluate-pension-long-term-liabilities/)).

## A six-question check

| Question | If the answer is no |
|:--|:--|
| Is maintenance capex at least close to PP&E depreciation over five years? | Raise your estimate or explain why |
| Did you treat share-based compensation as a cost? | Subtract it |
| If you added back right-of-use depreciation, did you subtract lease principal? | Subtract it |
| Is the year representative of the cycle? | Use an average or mid-cycle year |
| Did you include working capital and reclamation needs? | Add them |
| Would a peer-based maintenance estimate give a similar answer? | Investigate the gap |
