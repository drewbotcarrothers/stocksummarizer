Open any valuation of a Canadian oil producer, gold miner or copper developer and you will find dozens of inputs: reserves, production profiles, operating costs, capital spending, royalties, taxes, discount rates. One input usually outweighs all the others combined: the **commodity price deck**, the assumed oil, gas or metal price for each future year. Change that one line and the "fair value" can double or halve.

This post explains why commodity assumptions dominate resource valuations, how to read the price deck behind a number, and how to stress-test it. The mechanics of price-to-NAV are covered in the Price/NAV set, starting with [what price/NAV means for Canadian DIY investors](/investing/valuation/what-price-nav-means-canadian-diy/). It is educational; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Resource companies have high **operating leverage to the commodity price**: costs are largely fixed, so a 10–15% change in price can move cash flow and NAV by 30–50% or more. That makes the price deck the single biggest driver of any DCF or NAV. Before trusting a valuation, find **which deck it uses** (flat, strip, consensus or company), **compare it with the current futures strip and long-run cost curves**, and **re-run value at several prices**. A stock that only looks cheap at a high price deck is a bet on the commodity, not a value call.

Why the leverage? If a producer's all-in cost to produce and sustain a barrel is US$50, its margin at US$60 is US$10. At US$70 the margin doubles to US$20. A 17% price rise doubled the cash margin. Gold miners work the same way with all-in sustaining costs; see [what AISC is](/investing/glossary/what-is-aisc/) and [analyze gold miners beyond the spot price](/investing/analyze/analyze-gold-miners-beyond-spot-price/).

## Which price deck is behind the number?

| Deck type | What it is | Watch out for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Flat (constant) price | One price for every year, sometimes in real terms | Easy to compare; ignores the shape of the futures curve |
| Futures strip | Market prices for delivery in future years | Liquid only a few years out; can be in backwardation or contango |
| Consensus deck | Average of analyst forecasts | Tends to lag spot moves; herd behaviour |
| Company or reserve evaluator deck | Used in reserve reports and impairment tests | Disclosed in the annual report or AIF; may differ from what the market uses |
| Long-run "incentive" price | Price needed to justify new supply | Useful anchor, but cycles can stay far from it for years |

Canadian producers disclose the price forecasts used by their independent reserve evaluators in the annual information form under NI 51-101; see [how to read an annual information form](/investing/analyze/how-to-read-annual-information-form/).

## Stress-test checklist

1. **Find the deck.** Is the target price or NAV built on a flat price, the strip or consensus?
2. **Compare with today.** Is the deck above or below current spot and the strip?
3. **Re-run at three prices.** A low case near the industry's cost of supply, a base case near the strip and a high case.
4. **Check the break-even.** At what price does free cash flow cover sustaining capital, interest and the dividend? See [read commodity price sensitivity disclosures](/investing/analyze/read-commodity-price-sensitivity-resource-issuers/).
5. **Check hedges.** Hedging changes near-term cash flow, not long-run value.
6. **Check currency.** Most commodities are priced in US dollars while many costs are in Canadian dollars; a weaker loonie raises CAD margins. See [currency and peers that report in USD](/investing/valuation/currency-valuation-peers-report-usd/).

## Sensitivity disclosures you can use (illustrative format)

| Change in assumption | Effect on annual funds flow | Where disclosed |
|:--|:--|:--|
| US$1/bbl WTI | ± C$X million | MD&A sensitivity table |
| US$0.10/Mcf natural gas | ± C$X million | MD&A sensitivity table |
| US$0.01 change in USD/CAD | ± C$X million | MD&A sensitivity table |
| Differential (e.g., WCS) widens US$1/bbl | ∓ C$X million | MD&A sensitivity table |

Many TSX producers publish a table like this each quarter. Multiplying it out across your three price cases is a fast way to see how much value depends on the deck.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing P/NAV across companies using different decks.** Normalise to one deck first; see [price/NAV vs peers](/investing/valuation/price-nav-vs-peers-canada-first-comparison/).
- **Treating today's spot price as the long-run price.** Spot at the top of a cycle inflates every valuation.
- **Calling a stock "cheap" on trailing earnings at a cyclical peak.** See [when P/E misleads on TSX stocks](/investing/valuation/when-pe-misleads-tsx/).
- **Ignoring the discount rate debate.** Analysts often argue about 5% vs 8% discount rates while the price deck matters more.
