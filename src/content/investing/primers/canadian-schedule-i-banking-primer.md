"Schedule I bank" sounds like jargon, but it's the legal category that covers every bank most Canadian stock investors own: Royal Bank, TD, Scotiabank, BMO, CIBC and National Bank. This primer explains what Schedule I status means, how Canada's biggest banks are regulated, and why those rules shape returns, dividends and buybacks.

Educational primer — not a recommendation. Regulatory figures are as published by OSFI and the federal government; check the sources for updates.

## Direct answer

Under the federal **Bank Act**, Canadian banks are listed in three schedules. **Schedule I** banks are domestic banks — not subsidiaries of foreign banks — and include the "Big Six." **Schedule II** banks are Canadian subsidiaries of foreign banks; **Schedule III** are branches of foreign banks operating in Canada. The Big Six are also designated **domestic systemically important banks (D-SIBs)** by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), which means extra capital requirements — including the Domestic Stability Buffer, set at **3.0%** of risk-weighted assets since June 19, 2026, for a total CET1 expectation of **11.0%**. Large Schedule I banks must also be **widely held**: with equity of $12 billion or more, no single investor can own more than 20% of any class of voting shares or 30% of any class of non-voting shares.

## The three schedules

| Schedule | Who | Examples of what it means for investors |
|:--|:--|:--|
| I | Domestic banks (including the Big Six and smaller domestic banks) | The TSX-listed bank stocks most DIY investors own; ownership limits apply to large banks |
| II | Subsidiaries of foreign banks incorporated in Canada | Usually not separately listed; parent is a foreign bank |
| III | Branches of foreign banks (authorized foreign banks) | Mostly wholesale/corporate lending; restrictions on taking small retail deposits |

OSFI publishes the current list of banks in each schedule.

## Who regulates what

| Body | Role | What investors should watch |
|:--|:--|:--|
| OSFI | Prudential regulator: capital, liquidity, mortgage underwriting rules (Guideline B-20), stress tests | Capital decisions (DSB in June and December), guideline changes |
| Department of Finance | Bank Act and ownership policy; approves major mergers with the Minister of Finance | Bank Act reviews, merger policy |
| Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) | Consumer protection, fees and complaints | Fee and conduct rules that hit revenue |
| Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) | Insures eligible deposits up to $100,000 per insured category per member; resolution authority | Bail-in regime for large banks |
| Bank of Canada | Monetary policy, lender of last resort | Policy rate drives net interest margin |

**Why this matters for the stock:** capital above OSFI's expectation is what funds buybacks and dividend increases. When OSFI raises the buffer, banks often slow buybacks; when it lowers it (as in June 2026), they have more room. Read the CET1 ratio in every quarterly report and compare it with the 11.0% expectation.

## What Schedule I / D-SIB status means for valuation

| Feature | Upside for shareholders | Downside for shareholders |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Concentrated market (six large banks) | Pricing discipline, high and stable ROE | Political pressure on fees and competition policy |
| Widely held rule | No controlling shareholder can take over cheaply | Takeover premium is unlikely; consolidation among the Big Six faces high hurdles |
| D-SIB capital surcharge and DSB | Resilience in downturns | Lower leverage than global peers caps ROE |
| Bail-in regime | Lower systemic risk | Certain senior debt can be converted to equity in a failure — shareholders are first in line for losses |
| Mortgage rules (B-20) and insured mortgages | Lower credit losses on insured books | Slower loan growth when rules tighten |

## How to use this when analysing a bank stock

1. Check the CET1 ratio against OSFI's 11.0% expectation and the bank's own target.
2. Read the capital management section for buyback plans and dividend policy.
3. Compare ROE and P/B with peers — see [valuation for banks: P/B, ROE](/investing/valuation/valuing-canadian-banks-pb-roe/).
4. Track net interest margin and credit losses — see [what is NIM?](/investing/glossary/what-is-net-interest-margin/) and [bank operating metrics](/investing/sectors/bank-operating-metrics-to-track/).
5. Note OSFI's June and December DSB decisions in your calendar.
