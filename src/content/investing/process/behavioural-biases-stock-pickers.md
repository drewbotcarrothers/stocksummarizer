Look at the two orange circles in the photo above. They are the same size; the surrounding circles make one look bigger. Knowing that doesn't make the illusion go away — and investing biases work the same way. Understanding them is not enough to stop them. What helps is **process**: rules and records that catch the bias before it turns into a trade.

Educational only — not financial advice. Example trades and journal results below are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

The biases that hurt stock pickers most are the ones that change **what you buy, how much, and when you sell**: overconfidence (sizing too big, trading too often), confirmation bias (only reading the bull case), anchoring (fixating on your purchase price or an old high), loss aversion and the disposition effect (selling winners early and holding losers too long), recency bias (extrapolating the last few quarters), home bias (owning what's familiar), and herding/FOMO (buying because a stock is popular). The practical defences are a written thesis with kill criteria before every buy, a pre-mortem, position-size caps, scheduled reviews instead of constant checking, and a decision journal you actually score.

## The biases, ranked by damage

| Bias | How it shows up | What it costs | Countermeasure |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Overconfidence | Big positions, frequent trades, narrow forecasts | Concentration losses, trading costs | Position caps; track forecast accuracy |
| Confirmation bias | Reading only bullish analysis, dismissing bad news | Missing a broken thesis | Write the bear case first; seek the best opposing view |
| Anchoring | "I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid" | Holding a deteriorating business | Re-underwrite from today's price and facts |
| Loss aversion / disposition effect | Selling winners quickly, holding losers | Cutting compounding, keeping mistakes | Pre-set sell criteria for both directions |
| Recency bias | Projecting the last 2–3 quarters forever | Buying peaks, selling troughs | Use 5–10 year data and mid-cycle numbers |
| Home bias | Mostly Canadian banks and pipelines | Hidden concentration | Look-through exposure check |
| Herding / FOMO | Buying what's trending in the news or online | Paying peak multiples | A 48-hour waiting rule plus a written thesis |
| Sunk-cost fallacy | Averaging down to "get back" a loss | Larger losses on broken ideas | Add only if the thesis is intact and sizing allows |

Prospect theory research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky found that people tend to feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains — often estimated at roughly twice as strongly. That asymmetry is the engine behind the disposition effect: selling a winner feels good, and selling a loser means admitting a loss.

If your high-conviction ideas don't outperform your low-conviction ones, your conviction isn't adding information — and position sizes should reflect that ([position sizing for DIY stock pickers](/investing/process/position-sizing-diy-stock-pickers/)).

1. **Thesis first.** Use a [bull / bear / watch brief](/investing/analyze/stock-brief-template-bull-bear-watch/). If you can't write the bear case, you don't understand the stock yet.
2. **Pre-mortem.** Imagine it's two years later and the investment failed. Write the three most likely reasons. Turn them into watch items.
3. **Size and sell rules before buying.** Decide the maximum position, and what would make you sell — thesis broken, valuation stretched, better use of capital — not a price that "feels" right.
4. **Journal.** Record date, price, confidence, and the two or three facts the thesis depends on.
5. **Review on a schedule.** Quarterly, after results, is usually enough. Daily price checking feeds recency bias and overtrading.

## Canadian-specific traps

| Trap | Bias behind it | Defence |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "Canadian banks always recover" | Recency and home bias | Study full credit cycles, not the last decade; check [bank operating metrics](/investing/sectors/bank-operating-metrics-to-track/) |
| Chasing a high pipeline or telecom yield | Anchoring on yield, loss aversion | [Payout and FCF coverage](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/) before yield |
| Holding a resource stock "until oil comes back" | Anchoring, sunk cost | Re-underwrite at mid-cycle prices |
| Trading actively inside a TFSA after a hot streak | Overconfidence | Long-term satellites only; see [TFSA for stock investors](/investing/accounts/tfsa-for-stock-investors/) |
