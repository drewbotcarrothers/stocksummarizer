Most investors spend hours deciding what to buy and minutes deciding when to sell. That imbalance is where a lot of damage happens: holding a broken idea because it's down, or dumping a good one because it's noisy. The fix is simple and unglamorous — **write your sell criteria before you buy**, while you're still thinking clearly.

Educational only — not financial advice. Every example below is **hypothetical**. This post goes deeper on one countermeasure from [behavioural biases that hurt stock pickers most](/investing/process/behavioural-biases-stock-pickers/): pre-committed exit rules.

## Direct answer

Before you place a buy order, write down (1) the thesis in two sentences, (2) the catalysts you expect and roughly when, and (3) **kill criteria** — specific, checkable facts that would prove the thesis wrong. Add a valuation ceiling, a position-size limit, and a time stop. Then review only at defined triggers (earnings, major filings, large price moves) and ask one question: *has a kill criterion been met?* If yes, exit or cut back unless you can write down why the criterion was wrong — before looking at your gain or loss.

| Bias | What it does to sell decisions | How written criteria counter it |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Disposition effect | Sell winners too early, hold losers too long | The criterion, not the P&L, decides |
| Anchoring | Wait to "get back to even" | Your purchase price doesn't appear in any criterion |
| Confirmation bias | Explain away bad news | The disproof was defined before you were invested |
| Sunk cost | "I've done so much research" | Research time isn't a reason to own |
| Recency | Panic on one bad quarter | Kill criteria usually require a trend, not one data point |

## The five types of sell criteria

| Type | What it tests | Example wording (hypothetical) |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Thesis break** (kill criterion) | Did the reason to own disappear? | "Exit if same-store sales growth is negative for two consecutive quarters." |
| **Valuation ceiling** | Is the upside priced in? | "Trim if price exceeds my high-case value of $68." |
| **Time stop** | Did the catalyst arrive? | "If the new mine isn't producing by Q4 next year, reassess from zero." |
| **Size limit** | Has it grown too big? | "Trim back to 6% if the position exceeds 10% of the portfolio." See [position sizing](/investing/process/position-sizing-diy-stock-pickers/). |
| **Opportunity cost** | Is there a clearly better use of the capital? | "Replace only if the new idea scores higher on my brief template and fits the sector cap." |

Price drops are deliberately missing from the list. A falling price is a **review trigger**, not a sell criterion — it tells you to check the kill criteria, not to sell.

## Writing kill criteria that actually work

Good kill criteria share four properties:

| Property | Weak version | Strong version |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Specific** | "If growth slows" | "If organic revenue growth falls below 3% for two quarters" |
| **Observable** | "If management loses focus" | "If the CEO or CFO leaves without a named successor" |
| **Linked to the thesis** | "If the stock falls 20%" | "If the dividend payout ratio on free cash flow exceeds 100% for a full year" |
| **Sourced** | "If things look bad" | "As reported in the MD&A or quarterly results" — see [how to read an MD&A](/investing/analyze/how-to-read-mdna/) |

Aim for **two or three** kill criteria per stock. More than five usually means the thesis itself is fuzzy.

### Worked example: a hypothetical Canadian pipeline holding

| Field | Entry (written before buying) |
|:--|:--|
| Thesis | Contracted, fee-based cash flows support a growing dividend; a large expansion project adds cash flow in two years. |
| Catalysts | Project in service by end of next year; annual dividend increase. |
| Kill criteria | (1) Distributable cash flow payout ratio above 90% for four straight quarters. (2) Net debt to EBITDA above the company's own stated ceiling with no plan to fix it. (3) Project cancelled or written down. |
| Valuation ceiling | Trim if price exceeds my high-case value. |
| Size limit | Max 8% of portfolio. |
| Time stop | If the project slips more than 12 months, re-underwrite from scratch. |
| Review triggers | Quarterly results, any material change report, a 20% price move. |

Keeping a log of *why* you sold is as useful as the criteria themselves. After a year or two you'll see which criteria fire, which you ignore, and whether improvised exits helped or hurt.

## A review routine that respects the plan

| Trigger | What to do |
|:--|:--|
| Quarterly results | Check each kill criterion against the new numbers; update the brief |
| Material change report or big news | Ask whether a criterion is affected; most news isn't decisive |
| Price down 20%+ (hypothetical threshold) | Re-read the thesis and kill criteria before looking at the chart again |
| Criterion met | Exit or reduce unless you can write a specific reason the criterion was wrong |
| Changing a criterion | Allowed only with a written reason, dated, and not on the day of a big price move |

In a taxable account, also note the tax side before selling — capital gains, [adjusted cost base](/investing/accounts/adjusted-cost-base-tracking-diy-portfolios/) and, if you plan to buy back, the [superficial loss rule](/investing/accounts/superficial-loss-rule-stock-swaps/). Tax should inform the *how* of an exit, rarely the *whether*.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Using only a price stop | Sells good businesses on noise, holds bad ones that drift | Pair price triggers with thesis-based criteria |
| Vague criteria | Easy to rationalize | Make each one specific and observable |
| Moving the goalposts | Turns criteria into decoration | Change only with a dated, written reason |
| No time stop | Dead money ties up capital for years | Put a date on the catalyst |
| Criteria in your head | Memory is edited by outcomes | Keep them in the stock's brief or journal |
