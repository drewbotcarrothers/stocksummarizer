Most Canadian stock investors meet the Canada–US tax treaty through one number: the 15% withheld from US dividends. The treaty does more than that. It decides which country taxes your gains, why an RRSP gets different treatment from a TFSA, and how a large US portfolio is treated for US estate tax. Knowing which article does what helps you read your broker statements and avoid paying more than you need to.

Educational only — not tax advice. Treaty treatment depends on your residency, citizenship and paperwork. US citizens and green-card holders living in Canada face different rules and should get cross-border advice. Examples are **hypothetical**. The account-by-account withholding table is in [withholding tax on US dividends: TFSA vs RRSP vs taxable](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/) and isn't repeated here.

## Direct answer

For a Canadian resident who isn't a US person, the treaty: cuts US withholding on **dividends** from the US statutory 30% to **15%** (Article X), as long as your broker has a valid **W-8BEN** on file; **exempts dividends paid to RRSPs and RRIFs** (Article XXI) but not TFSAs, RESPs or FHSAs; generally leaves **capital gains on US shares taxable only in Canada** (Article XIII); sharply limits US tax on most **interest**; and gives a **prorated unified credit against US estate tax** (Article XXIX B). Canada then gives a **foreign tax credit** for US tax paid in a taxable account.

| Article XXIX B (Taxes Imposed by Reason of Death) | US estate tax on Canadian residents | Prorated unified credit and other relief |

## The W-8BEN: the form that triggers the 15% rate

The treaty rate isn't automatic. US payers withhold at 30% unless your broker has a valid W-8BEN certifying you're a Canadian resident. A W-8BEN generally stays valid until the end of the third calendar year after it's signed, unless your circumstances change.

If you see 30% withheld on a US dividend, the fix is usually paperwork, not a tax return. Ask your broker to confirm the form is on file for every account, including newly opened ones.

## Capital gains: Canada taxes them, the US generally doesn't

When you sell US-listed shares at a gain, the US generally doesn't tax the gain for a Canadian resident who isn't a US person, under Article XIII. You report the gain in Canadian dollars on your Canadian return, with half included in income in a taxable account. There's no US withholding on sale proceeds for ordinary listed shares. That's why US growth stocks with little or no dividend raise few treaty issues; see [account location for growth stocks you expect to compound](/investing/accounts/account-location-growth-stocks/).

## Why the RRSP is different from the TFSA

Article XXI exempts certain income of trusts operated to provide retirement benefits. The IRS has accepted that RRSPs and RRIFs qualify, so US dividends paid to them on US-listed shares are generally not withheld. TFSAs, RESPs and FHSAs aren't retirement trusts under the treaty, so 15% is withheld and can't be recovered, because there's no Canadian tax inside those accounts to credit it against.

In a taxable account the 15% is usually recoverable as a foreign tax credit; the full account-by-account comparison is in the [US dividend withholding post](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/).

The exemption applies to US-listed shares and US-listed ETFs held directly. A Canadian-listed ETF that holds US stocks pays withholding inside the fund; see [US withholding inside Canadian-listed vs US-listed ETFs](/investing/indexes-etfs/us-withholding-tax-canadian-vs-us-listed-etfs/).

## US estate tax: a larger-portfolio issue

US estate tax can apply to US-situs assets, including US-listed shares, owned by non-US persons at death. The treaty's Article XXIX B gives Canadian residents a share of the US unified credit, prorated by the portion of their worldwide estate that is in the US. For 2026 the IRS basic exclusion amount for US citizens is US$15 million, so for most Canadian DIY investors the prorated credit eliminates the tax. Very large US holdings are where advice is worth paying for.

| Hypothetical Canadian resident | Worldwide estate | US-listed shares | US share of estate |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Investor A | US$2M | US$400K | 20% |
| Investor B | US$25M | US$10M | 40% |

Investor A's worldwide estate is well below the exclusion amount, so the prorated credit generally covers any US estate tax. Investor B is the kind of case where planning matters. Exact calculations depend on IRS rules and filing a US estate tax return.

## What the treaty doesn't do

| Common assumption | Reality |
|:--|:--|
| "The treaty makes TFSAs exempt too" | It doesn't; only retirement trusts such as RRSPs and RRIFs |
| "The treaty rate applies automatically" | Only with a valid W-8BEN on file |
| "It removes Canadian tax on US dividends" | No; US dividends are fully taxable in Canada in a taxable account, with a credit for US tax |
| "US dividends get the dividend tax credit" | No; the credit is only for taxable Canadian corporations' dividends |
| "Interlisted Canadian companies are US dividends" | No; a Canadian company's dividend is Canadian even if you buy it on the NYSE |

## A treaty checklist for DIY holders

| Question | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| Is a W-8BEN on file at every broker? | 15% instead of 30% |
| Are high-yield US payers in my RRSP where possible? | The Article XXI exemption |
| Am I claiming the foreign tax credit in my taxable account? | Recovers the 15% |
| Is my US-listed portfolio large relative to my estate? | US estate tax exposure |
| Am I a US person (citizen or green-card holder)? | Different rules; get cross-border advice |
