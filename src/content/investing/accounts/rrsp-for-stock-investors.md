For a stock picker, an RRSP is not just a retirement account. It changes how every type of stock return is taxed. Dividends, capital gains and losses all lose their separate character inside the plan, and everything that comes out later is taxed as ordinary income. That matters when you decide which stocks to hold there.

Educational only — not tax advice. Rules below are from CRA guidance as of November 2026; your situation, province and residency can change the answer. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

An RRSP gives you a **deduction when you contribute**, **no tax while gains and dividends compound inside**, and **full income tax when you withdraw**. For stock investors that has five consequences: Canadian dividends lose the **dividend tax credit**, capital gains lose the **50% inclusion rate**, **capital losses can't be claimed**, US-listed dividends are generally **exempt from US withholding**, and withdrawals have tax **withheld at 10%, 20% or 30%** (lower in Quebec) depending on the amount. The 2026 RRSP dollar limit is **$33,810**, subject to 18% of your prior-year earned income.

| Return type | In a taxable account | Inside an RRSP |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Eligible Canadian dividends | Grossed up, dividend tax credit applies | No annual tax; taxed as income on withdrawal, no credit |
| Capital gains | Half included in income when sold | No tax on sale; full amount taxed as income on withdrawal |
| Capital losses | Offset gains; carry back 3 years or forward | Not claimable |
| US dividends (US-listed shares) | 15% withheld, usually creditable | Generally exempt under the treaty |
| Interest | Fully taxed each year | No annual tax; taxed on withdrawal |

The trade is simple to state: you get a deduction at your current marginal rate and pay tax at your rate when you withdraw. Whether that's a good trade depends on those two rates, not on which stock you hold. But the stock you hold changes what you give up.

## What you give up for stocks in particular

The RRSP is least generous to the returns that are already taxed lightly outside it.

| Holding (hypothetical) | Taxable-account advantage you lose | RRSP advantage you gain |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Canadian dividend payer | Dividend tax credit | Deferral |
| Non-dividend growth stock | 50% inclusion rate and loss claims | Deferral |
| US dividend payer | Little (15% is usually creditable outside) | Withholding exemption plus deferral |
| Speculative small cap | Ability to claim a loss | Deferral on any gain |

For a deeper comparison of where each type belongs, see [account location for US dividend stocks](/investing/accounts/account-location-us-dividend-stocks/) and [account location for Canadian dividend stocks](/investing/accounts/account-location-canadian-dividend-stocks/). The US withholding mechanics are in [withholding tax on US dividends](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/).

## Losses inside an RRSP are gone

If a stock falls 60% inside your RRSP, there is no capital loss to claim. The contribution room you used doesn't come back either. That makes the RRSP a poor home for positions you think have a real chance of permanent loss.

## Contributing shares in kind

You can move shares from a taxable account into an RRSP "in kind." The transfer is treated as a sale at fair market value:

| Hypothetical in-kind contribution | Share with a gain | Share with a loss |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Adjusted cost base | $6,000 | $10,000 |
| Fair market value at transfer | $10,000 | $6,000 |
| Deemed gain or loss | +$4,000 | −$4,000 |
| Tax result | Gain is taxable (half included) | Loss is denied |
| RRSP contribution | $10,000 | $6,000 |

If a holding is underwater, selling it in the taxable account first, then contributing cash, can preserve the loss. Watch the [superficial loss rule](/investing/accounts/superficial-loss-rule-stock-swaps/) if you buy it back inside the RRSP within 30 days.

## Withdrawal framing

When you withdraw, your broker withholds tax at a rate set by the size of the withdrawal. CRA's rates for residents of Canada outside Quebec are 10% up to $5,000, 20% over $5,000 up to $15,000, and 30% over $15,000. In Quebec the federal rates are 5%, 10% and 15%, plus provincial withholding.

Withholding is a prepayment, not the final tax. The full withdrawal is added to your income for the year and taxed at your marginal rate, so you may owe more (or get some back) when you file. Withdrawing shares in kind works the same way: the market value on the day of withdrawal counts as income.

## A stock investor's RRSP checklist

| Question | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| Do I expect a lower tax rate at withdrawal? | That is where most of the RRSP benefit comes from |
| Which holdings lose the most by being here? | Canadian dividend payers lose the credit; losers lose their loss |
| Are my US-listed dividend payers here? | The treaty exemption mostly applies in an RRSP or RRIF |
| Am I contributing a losing position in kind? | The loss is denied |
| Do I know my withdrawal withholding? | 10%, 20% or 30% is a prepayment, not the final bill |
| Do I know the conversion deadline? | An RRSP must be collapsed or converted (for example to a RRIF) by the end of the year you turn 71 |
