"Canadian dividend stocks go in the taxable account" is one of the most repeated rules in Canadian DIY investing. It's often right — but for reasons that are more specific than most people realize, and with exceptions that matter for stock pickers who hold REITs, high-yield names or small-cap payers.

Educational only — not tax advice. Rates and credits change and vary by province; check current CRA guidance or a qualified professional. Examples are **hypothetical**. This post is dividend-specific; for the full account-by-account placement framework see [core-satellite portfolio design for Canadian accounts](/investing/accounts/core-satellite-canadian-accounts/).

## Direct answer

Canadian **eligible dividends** are among the most tax-efficient kinds of investment income in a taxable account because of the gross-up and **dividend tax credit**. Since that credit is only usable outside registered plans, Canadian eligible-dividend payers are usually the best candidates to sit in a taxable account once TFSA and RRSP room is used for less tax-efficient holdings (interest, US and foreign dividends, REIT "other income"). Exceptions: payers whose distributions aren't eligible dividends (most REITs and income trusts, many small private-style corporations), very high-yield positions if the grossed-up income affects your benefits, and long-term growth stocks you'd rather shelter in a TFSA.

## Step 1: know what kind of "dividend" you're getting

| Payer type | Usually reported on | Tax character in a taxable account | Placement implication |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Large TSX corporation (banks, utilities, telecoms, pipelines) | T5 | **Eligible** dividend: 38% gross-up, larger credit | Strong taxable-account candidate |
| Smaller Canadian corporation paying from income taxed at the small-business rate | T5 | **Non-eligible** dividend: 15% gross-up, smaller credit | Less efficient; still better than interest |
| REIT or income trust | T3 | Mix of other income, capital gains, return of capital | Often better in a TFSA or RRSP |
| Canadian company listed on the NYSE as well | T5 (Canadian source) | Still a Canadian dividend; no US withholding | Same as its TSX listing |

For the background on the two dividend types, see [eligible vs ordinary dividends](/investing/dividends/eligible-vs-ordinary-dividends/); for trusts, see [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/).

The gross-up is how the system credits you for tax the corporation already paid. It also has a side effect: the **grossed-up** amount — not the cash — goes into your net income. That matters if you're near income-tested thresholds such as the OAS recovery tax, or other benefits based on net income.

| Holding (hypothetical) | Where it usually fits | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Canadian bank or utility paying eligible dividends | Taxable (after registered room is used for less efficient income) | Dividend tax credit is only usable in taxable |
| Canadian REIT paying mostly other income | TFSA or RRSP | Distributions taxed like ordinary income in taxable; ROC complicates ACB |
| High-yield Canadian payer, near benefit thresholds | TFSA | Keeps grossed-up income off your return |
| Low-yield Canadian dividend grower with big expected price gains | TFSA or taxable | Most of the return is capital gain, which is deferrable in taxable and tax-free in a TFSA |
| Canadian dividend payer when you have no other RRSP holdings | RRSP is fine | Sheltering beats not investing; you just don't get the credit |

The RRSP isn't "wrong" for Canadian dividends — it simply wastes the dividend tax credit, and RRSP withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income whatever the source. When RRSP room is limited, US dividend payers and interest-bearing holdings usually benefit more from that shelter. US payers have their own rules — see [account location for US dividend stocks](/investing/accounts/account-location-us-dividend-stocks/).

## Step 4: stock-picker wrinkles

| Situation | What changes |
|:--|:--|
| You DRIP in a taxable account | Every reinvested dividend is a new purchase that adds to [adjusted cost base](/investing/accounts/adjusted-cost-base-tracking-diy-portfolios/) — track it |
| You hold the same stock in TFSA and taxable | Fine, but ACB is tracked only for the taxable shares; selling at a loss in taxable and buying in the TFSA can trigger the [superficial loss rule](/investing/accounts/superficial-loss-rule-stock-swaps/) |
| A payer cuts its dividend | Taxable: you can at least realize a capital loss if you sell. TFSA: the loss is permanent |
| Special dividends or capital gains dividends | Check the slip; character can differ from regular dividends |
| Your income is very low | Eligible dividends can be taxed at very low (in some provinces negative) marginal rates; check your province's rates |

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Treating every Canadian "yield" as an eligible dividend | REITs and trusts pay different income types | Check the payer's tax info page or last year's slip |
| Filling the TFSA with bank stocks while US payers sit in the TFSA losing withholding | Each account does the wrong job | Use the TFSA for growth or REITs; US payers usually in RRSP |
| Ignoring the grossed-up income | Can affect benefits and clawbacks | Model net income, not just cash dividends |
| Choosing stocks by tax efficiency | Tax is a tie-breaker, not a thesis | Start from the investment case; see [why yield alone is a terrible screen](/investing/dividends/why-yield-alone-is-a-terrible-screen/) |
