A TFSA is supposed to shelter investment growth from tax. But the Income Tax Act has a carve-out that many active stock pickers don't know about: if the TFSA itself is **carrying on a business** — and frequent, trader-like buying and selling of securities can be a business — the income from that business is **taxable**, even though it was earned inside a TFSA.

Educational only — not tax or legal advice. The rules below are summarized from CRA guidance and court decisions; your facts matter, so speak to a qualified tax professional if your TFSA activity looks anything like Investor C or D below. All examples are **hypothetical**. If you're new to holding individual stocks in a TFSA, start with [TFSA for stock investors](/investing/accounts/tfsa-for-stock-investors/).

## Direct answer

A TFSA trust is taxable on income from carrying on a business (Income Tax Act s. 146.2(6)). Whether trading counts as a business is a **question of fact**: CRA looks at factors such as how often you trade, how long you hold, your knowledge of markets, the time you spend, and whether you buy speculative securities for quick gains. If CRA concludes your TFSA ran a trading business, gains can be reassessed as fully taxable business income rather than tax-free growth, and you as the holder are **jointly and severally liable** with the TFSA trust for that tax. The safest posture for a stock picker is to run the TFSA like an **investment account, not a trading desk**: long holding periods, documented theses, and modest turnover.

Two facts surprise people:

- **Holding only qualified investments doesn't protect you.** In *Canadian Western Trust Company v. The King* (2024 FCA 108), the Federal Court of Appeal agreed that a TFSA trading qualified investments can still be carrying on a business taxable under s. 146.2(6).
- **The liability lands on you, not just the trustee.** CRA's Folio S3-F10-C1 (¶1.94.1) notes that the TFSA holder is jointly and severally liable with the TFSA trust for tax on business income, while the trustee's (your broker's) liability is limited.

## The factors CRA looks at

CRA's folio points to the long-standing court factors for whether securities transactions are a business (originally set out in Interpretation Bulletin IT-479R). None decides the question alone; the combination does.

| Factor | Looks like investing | Looks like a business |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Frequency of transactions | A handful of trades a year | Dozens or hundreds of trades a month |
| Period of ownership | Months to years | Minutes, days, or a few weeks |
| Knowledge of markets | General DIY knowledge | Professional background used in the trading |
| Time spent | Periodic reviews, earnings season | Hours a day watching screens |
| Nature of securities | Established, dividend-paying or long-thesis holdings | Speculative names bought for quick price moves |
| Intention at purchase | Hold for return over time | Buy to flip at a profit |
| Pattern over years | Stable, low turnover | Turnover rising as the account grows |

Folio S3-F10-C1 also notes that certain strategies — some **option writing**, **foreign exchange trading**, or **short selling** — can point toward a business, and that TFSAs effectively can't short sell because of the borrowing restriction.

## What's at stake if CRA reassesses

| Item | If the TFSA is investing | If the TFSA is found to be carrying on a business |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Gains and income | Tax-free | Business income taxable (the TFSA trust files a T3 return) |
| Who pays | No one | TFSA trust and you, jointly and severally |
| Contribution room | Unchanged by gains | Unchanged, but the tax bill is real money |
| Losses | Not deductible either way | Business losses stay inside the trust, not on your personal return |

The bigger the account has grown through active trading, the larger the potential bill. That asymmetry is the core risk: years of apparently tax-free compounding can be revisited.

## A lower-risk TFSA routine for stock pickers

| Habit | Why it helps |
|:--|:--|
| Written thesis before each buy | Shows investment intent; see the [bull / bear / watch template](/investing/analyze/stock-brief-template-bull-bear-watch/) |
| [Sell criteria documented in advance](/investing/process/document-sell-criteria-before-you-buy/) | Exits follow a plan, not price twitches |
| Turnover budget (e.g., a few position changes per quarter, hypothetical) | Keeps frequency and holding period in investing territory |
| Short-term or tactical ideas outside the TFSA | Gains are then taxed normally, and losses are usable |
| Trade log with dates and reasons | Useful for your process, and useful if CRA ever asks |

This is also where [behavioural biases](/investing/process/behavioural-biases-stock-pickers/) show up: overconfidence and recency push active stock pickers toward more trading, and the TFSA is the account where that habit carries extra tax risk. If you run a core-satellite portfolio, keep the TFSA for long-horizon satellites, as described in [core-satellite design for Canadian accounts](/investing/accounts/core-satellite-canadian-accounts/).

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Better approach |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "It's all qualified investments, so it's fine" | Qualified-investment status and the business test are separate questions | Watch frequency and holding period, not just what you own |
| Using the TFSA as the "fun money" trading account | Concentrates business-like activity in the account where it's riskiest | Keep speculation small and in a taxable account |
| Copying high-turnover strategies from social media | Pattern can look like a trading business | Match turnover to a long-term thesis |
| No records | Hard to show investment intent later | Keep a simple decision journal |
