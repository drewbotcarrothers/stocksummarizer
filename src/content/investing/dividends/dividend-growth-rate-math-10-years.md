Dividend growth sounds modest year to year. A 6% raise on a $1.00 dividend is six cents. Compounded over a decade it changes what a starting yield is really worth. This post walks through the math: compound annual growth rate (CAGR), the rule of 72, yield on cost and cumulative income, so you can compare a lower-yield grower with a higher-yield, slower payer on the same terms.

It's educational. All figures are hypothetical and assume growth is sustained, which is never guaranteed.

## Direct answer

Dividend per share after *n* years = **starting DPS × (1 + g)^n**. At **7%** a year, a dividend roughly **doubles in 10 years**; at **5%** it grows about **1.63 times**; at **3%** about **1.34 times**. To find a company's historical growth rate, use **CAGR = (ending DPS ÷ starting DPS)^(1/years) − 1**. Then compare total income over the holding period, not just today's yield, and check that earnings or free cash flow per share grew at a similar pace. Growth that outruns the business ends in a slowdown or a cut.

## The core formulas

| Calculation | Formula | Example (hypothetical) |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Future DPS | DPS × (1 + g)^n | $1.00 × 1.07^10 = $1.97 |
| Historical CAGR | (End ÷ Start)^(1/n) − 1 | ($1.90 ÷ $1.00)^(1/11) − 1 ≈ 6.0% |
| Rule of 72 (doubling time) | 72 ÷ growth rate (%) | 72 ÷ 8 = ~9 years |
| Yield on cost | Current DPS ÷ your purchase price | $1.97 ÷ $28.57 = 6.9% |

Yield on cost is useful for seeing how growth changes your income relative to what you paid. It's less useful for decisions: what matters for a hold-or-sell decision is today's yield and prospects, not your original price.

## Cumulative income over 10 years

Starting with a C$10,000 investment, with dividends taken as cash and the share count unchanged:

| Hypothetical | Start yield | Growth | Year 1 income | Year 10 income | 10-year total income |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| High yield, flat | 5.0% | 0% | $500 | $500 | $5,000 |
| Middle | 3.5% | 5% | $350 | $543 | $4,402 |
| Grower | 2.5% | 9% | $250 | $543 | $3,798 |

Over 10 years the flat 5% payer still wins on total cash collected, even though the growers pass it on annual income around years 9 to 10. Growth wins over longer horizons, and only if it's sustained. This is why the comparison depends on your time frame and on how confident you are in each growth rate; see [dividend growth vs high yield](/investing/dividends/dividend-growth-vs-high-yield/).

## Where the math misleads

| Trap | What happens | Check |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Extrapolating a peak growth rate | A 10% grower slows to 4% as payout ratio rises | Compare DPS growth with FCF per share growth |
| Using a start year after a cut | CAGR from a depressed base looks strong | Measure from a pre-cut year too |
| Ignoring splits | Fake negative growth year | Use split-adjusted DPS; see [splits and dividend per share](/investing/dividends/stock-splits-consolidations-dividend-per-share/) |
| Ignoring inflation | 3% growth with 3% inflation is zero real growth | See [inflation and a stable dividend](/investing/dividends/inflation-real-value-stable-dividend/) |
| Ignoring price | Yield on cost ignores what the shares are worth today | Track total return, not just income |

## A sustainability cross-check

Dividend growth over long periods can't exceed per-share earnings or cash flow growth unless the payout ratio rises. A quick rule: **sustainable DPS growth ≈ FCF per share growth + any room to raise the payout ratio**. If a company already pays 90% of FCF, future dividend growth depends almost entirely on growing the business.
