A dividend streak is a single number: how many years in a row a company has raised its payout. It's a useful filter, but it hides almost everything that matters. Two companies can both show "11 consecutive years of increases" while one doubled its dividend and the other added a penny a year to keep the streak alive.

This post shows how to read a dividend history chart in layers so you see the shape of the record, not just its length. It's educational; the companies and figures are hypothetical. For how streak rules work in index methodology, see [Canadian dividend aristocrats explained](/investing/dividends/canadian-dividend-aristocrats-explained/); this post doesn't repeat those rules.

## Direct answer

Look at five things on a long dividend chart: the **level** of dividends per share (DPS), the **slope** (growth rate, broken into periods), any **gaps** (cuts, freezes or skipped raises), the **context** behind the payout (earnings and free cash flow per share on the same chart) and **adjustments** (splits, consolidations and special dividends) that can make the line misleading. A long streak with a falling slope and a rising payout ratio tells a very different story from a long streak with steady growth that tracks earnings.

| Layer | What to look for | What it can tell you |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Level | DPS on a per-share basis over at least 10 years | Total progress since a reference year |
| Slope | Growth rate in 3- to 5-year windows, not one 10-year figure | Whether growth is speeding up or fading |
| Gaps | Flat years, cuts, suspensions, skipped raise dates | How the board behaved under stress |
| Context | EPS and free cash flow per share plotted on the same axis | Whether the dividend is outrunning the business |
| Adjustments | Splits, consolidations, specials, currency changes | Whether the line is distorted by one-off items |

## Put earnings and cash flow on the same chart

The most useful single addition is a second line for earnings or free cash flow per share. If DPS rises faster than either for several years, the payout ratio is climbing, and that can't continue indefinitely. See [payout ratio explained](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/) and [FCF coverage of dividends](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/) for how to calculate both.

| Hypothetical pattern | DPS growth | FCF per share growth | Read |
|:--|--:|--:|:--|
| Growth funded by the business | 6% | 6–7% | Payout ratio stable |
| Growth borrowed from the future | 6% | 2% | Payout ratio rising; raises may slow |
| Catch-up after a reset | 10% | 4% | Check whether the board is restoring a prior level |

## Adjustments that distort the line

- **Splits and consolidations.** A 2-for-1 split halves DPS. Most data providers adjust history, but some charts and older filings don't. See [how stock splits and consolidations affect dividend per share](/investing/dividends/stock-splits-consolidations-dividend-per-share/).
- **Special dividends.** A one-time payment creates a spike that a trailing chart may count as ordinary. See [special dividends: how to treat them in your analysis](/investing/dividends/special-dividends-how-to-treat-in-analysis/).
- **Currency.** Some TSX-listed companies declare dividends in US dollars, so the Canadian-dollar line moves with the exchange rate even when the declared dividend is flat.
- **Payment timing.** A company that moves from semi-annual to quarterly payments, or shifts a raise from Q4 to Q1, can show a fake flat or double year on a calendar-year chart. Use declared rates where you can.
