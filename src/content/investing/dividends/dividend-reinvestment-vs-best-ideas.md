A dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) is the easiest way to compound: dividends buy more shares of the same stock automatically. But every dividend is also a capital allocation decision. Reinvesting into the stock that paid it is one choice; adding to your most attractive idea or an underweight position is another. The right answer depends on your portfolio, not on the DRIP button.

This post gives an opportunity-cost framework for that choice. It is educational; figures are hypothetical. For DRIP mechanics, see [DRIP strategies inside registered accounts](/investing/dividends/drip-strategies-registered-accounts/) and [DRIP strategies in taxable accounts](/investing/dividends/drip-strategies-taxable-accounts-acb/); this post doesn't repeat them.

## Direct answer

Automatic reinvestment is a sensible default **when the paying stock is at or below its target weight, still within your fair value range and still your preferred use of new money**. When it isn't (the holding is already large, looks expensive versus your estimate, or you have a better-ranked idea), **pooling dividends as cash and deploying them deliberately** usually fits a stock picker's process better. The cost of pooling is behavioural: cash can sit idle. The cost of a blanket DRIP is drift: your biggest payers grow largest by default.

| Question | If yes | If no |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Is the position at or below its target weight? | DRIP is fine | Pool; DRIP adds to an overweight |
| Is the price within your fair value range? | DRIP is fine | Pool; you're buying above your own estimate |
| Is the thesis intact and recently reviewed? | DRIP is fine | Pool until you've reviewed it |
| Is there a clearly better-ranked idea or gap? | Redirect | DRIP is fine |
| Will you actually deploy pooled cash promptly? | Pooling works | DRIP protects you from idle cash |

The last question is the honest one. Pooling only beats a DRIP if the cash gets invested.

## The opportunity cost, in numbers

Suppose your portfolio throws off C$2,000 a year in dividends. Where those dollars go compounds over time. The expected returns below are hypothetical estimates, not forecasts.

| Hypothetical: C$2,000 a year for 10 years | Expected return | Value after 10 years |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Reinvest in a mature payer you rate as fully valued | 4% | $24,012 |
| Reinvest in a fairly valued holding | 6% | $26,362 |
| Redirect to an idea you rate as undervalued | 9% | $30,386 |

The difference looks meaningful, but it rests on your return estimates being right. If you're no better than average at ranking ideas, the gap may not exist. That's why this is a framework for people with a written process, such as a [fair value range](/investing/valuation/fair-value-ranges-instead-of-price-targets/) and target weights, not a reason to trade more.

## Drift: what a blanket DRIP does to weights

High-yield holdings reinvest more dollars than low-yield ones, so with every DRIP switched on, your highest payers become your biggest positions over time even if you never chose that.

| Hypothetical | Weight today | Yield | Effect of DRIP over years |
|:--|--:|--:|:--|
| Utility | 15% | 5% | Grows its share fastest |
| Bank | 15% | 4% | Grows its share |
| Industrial | 15% | 1.5% | Lags |
| Growth stock, no dividend | 15% | 0% | Shrinks as a share unless price outperforms |

Drift isn't always bad, but it should be a decision. See [concentration risk in Canadian portfolios](/investing/process/concentration-risk-canadian-portfolios/) and [position sizing for DIY stock pickers](/investing/process/position-sizing-diy-stock-pickers/).

## Three workable approaches

| Approach | How it works | Best for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| DRIP everything | All dividends reinvest automatically | Hands-off investors with balanced weights |
| Pool and deploy | Dividends land as cash; you invest monthly or quarterly into the best-ranked idea or underweight | Stock pickers with a written ranking and target weights |
| Selective DRIP | DRIP only holdings below target weight and within fair value; others pay cash | Most DIY investors who want both |

A practical routine for pooling: pick a set day each month or quarter, list positions by distance below target weight, check valuation against your range and buy the top one or two. Write the reason down alongside your [thesis tracking](/investing/analyze/track-stock-thesis-over-quarters/) notes.

## Small frictions that tip the decision

- Broker DRIPs in Canada usually buy **whole shares only**, so small dividends on high-priced stocks may sit as cash anyway.
- Some company plans (through a transfer agent) offer a **discount** on reinvested shares; check the plan terms.
- With commission-free trades at many brokers, the cost of deploying pooled cash is often low, but check your broker's fees and FX costs on US holdings.
- In taxable accounts, every DRIP purchase adds an ACB entry. Pooling doesn't remove the record keeping, but it can mean fewer lots.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving DRIPs on for every holding by default, then wondering why one stock is 25% of the portfolio.
- Pooling dividends and letting the cash sit for a year.
- Redirecting cash into a "better idea" that's really just recent price momentum.
- Treating a DRIP discount as free money when the stock itself is expensive.
