Canadian dividends come in two tax flavours: **eligible** and **non-eligible** (often called "ordinary" or "other than eligible"). For a stock investor holding TSX companies in a **taxable** account, the distinction changes your after-tax income. In a TFSA or RRSP it doesn't matter for tax at all. This guide explains the mechanics at the level an investor needs, without turning into a tax return walkthrough.

Educational only — not tax advice. Rates below are the federal rules in effect at the time of writing; provincial credits vary and rules change. Confirm with the CRA or a tax professional. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

**Eligible dividends** are generally paid by public Canadian companies (and some private ones) out of income taxed at the general corporate rate. **Non-eligible dividends** generally come from income taxed at the lower small-business rate, so they're mostly paid by private Canadian-controlled companies. Both are "grossed up" and then reduced by a **dividend tax credit**, which credits you for tax the corporation already paid. Eligible dividends get the bigger gross-up and the bigger credit, so they're usually taxed less. Most large TSX-listed companies designate their dividends as eligible.

## Investor framing: what matters for TSX stocks

| Question | Practical answer |
|:--|:--|
| Are my TSX dividends eligible? | Usually yes for large public Canadian companies — check the "eligible dividend" designation on the company's dividend or investor page, or your T5 slip |
| Does it matter in a TFSA or RRSP? | No — registered accounts aren't taxed on dividends as they're received |
| What about US dividends? | Not eligible for the Canadian dividend tax credit; taxed as foreign income — see [US withholding tax](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/) |
| REIT distributions? | Mostly not dividends at all — other income, capital gains, return of capital — see [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/) |
| Preferred shares of public companies? | Typically eligible dividends too |

The gross-up is the part people miss. A $10,000 eligible dividend shows up as **$13,800 of income** on your return before the credit is applied. That can matter for income-tested items calculated on net income — the Old Age Security recovery tax, for example, or some provincial and federal benefits.

## After-tax framing (hypothetical)

Exact after-tax results depend on province and bracket, so here's a direction-only comparison for a hypothetical investor in a middle bracket:

| $1,000 received as… | Relative after-tax result in a taxable account |
|:--|:--|
| Eligible Canadian dividend | Usually among the most tax-efficient forms of investment income at low-to-middle incomes |
| Capital gain | Often similar or better at high incomes (only half is included) |
| Non-eligible dividend | Taxed more than eligible |
| US / foreign dividend | Taxed like interest (full rate), less any foreign tax credit |
| Interest | Taxed at your full marginal rate |

*Use your province's current combined rates (published by the CRA and many tax-software makers) for real numbers.*

## When the distinction should change a decision

1. **Account location.** In a taxable account, eligible Canadian dividends are generally more tax-efficient than US dividends or interest. That's one reason many DIY investors keep Canadian dividend payers in taxable accounts and higher-yield US payers in RRSPs — a portfolio-level choice, not a rule.
2. **Income-tested benefits.** The gross-up raises net income. Retirees near the OAS recovery threshold should model it.
3. **Comparing yields.** A 5% REIT distribution and a 5% bank dividend don't leave the same after-tax amount in a taxable account. Neither does a US dividend.

It should **not** override the business analysis. A tax-efficient dividend that gets cut is still a cut — check [FCF coverage](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/) and [payout ratio](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/) first.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking eligible dividends are "tax-free" — they're taxed, just at a lower effective rate for many investors.
- Forgetting the gross-up when estimating net income.
- Assuming a TSX-listed REIT or income trust pays eligible dividends.
- Comparing pre-tax yields across Canadian, US and REIT income.
