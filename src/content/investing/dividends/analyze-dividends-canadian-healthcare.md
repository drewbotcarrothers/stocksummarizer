The TSX has few large healthcare companies that pay dividends. Most Canadian healthcare income comes from a narrow group: long-term care and retirement home operators, healthcare real estate owners, and a handful of service and product companies. Their dividends depend less on drug pipelines or device sales and more on government funding, occupancy and wages.

Educational only; worked numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named. Healthcare REITs and seniors housing trusts use REIT metrics; for those, also see [how to analyze dividends in Canadian REITs](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-reits/).

## Direct answer

Analyze a Canadian healthcare dividend by first identifying **which business you own** (government-funded care, private-pay retirement living, healthcare property, or products and services), then testing the drivers that matter for it: **funding rates**, **occupancy**, **labour costs**, **capital spending on redevelopment** and **debt costs**. Measure payout against AFFO or free cash flow per unit, and test it at lower occupancy and higher wages.

## Step 1: identify the business model

| Type | Main revenue | Main dividend risk |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Long-term care operator | Provincial per-diem funding | Wage costs outrunning funding |
| Retirement residences | Private resident fees | Occupancy and rate growth |
| Healthcare real estate | Rent from clinics, hospitals, medical offices | Interest costs, tenant quality |
| Services and products | Contracts, product sales | Contract renewals, pricing |

Many issuers mix these. Read segment reporting to see which part produces the cash.

Occupancy fell across the sector during the pandemic and took several years to recover. If you're buying during a recovery, test the distribution at the current occupancy, not the target.

## Step 4: labour and funding

Wages are the largest cost in care homes. In long-term care, much of the revenue comes from provincial funding set by government, so the question is whether funding increases keep up with wage increases.

| Hypothetical operator, C$M | Base | Wages +4%, funding +2% |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Funded revenue | 1,000 | 1,020 |
| Labour costs | 700 | 728 |
| Other costs | 200 | 204 |
| Cash flow before debt | 100 | 88 |

A gap of two percentage points between wage and funding growth cut cash flow by 12% in this example.

## Step 5: debt and interest rates

Healthcare property owners and seniors housing operators carry mortgage and construction debt. Read the debt maturity table, the share of floating-rate debt and any interest rate hedges, and when they expire. Rising interest costs reduce AFFO per unit directly.

## Step 6: capital spending and redevelopment

Older long-term care homes in some provinces must be rebuilt to newer standards. Redevelopment ties up capital for years and may be funded with debt, equity or government support. Check how the company plans to fund it before assuming the distribution is safe.

## A healthcare dividend checklist

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| Business model | Clear, with cash by segment |
| Payout of AFFO or FCF per unit | Below 90% for property-heavy issuers |
| Occupancy | Stable or rising; distribution covered at current level |
| Wage vs funding growth | Funding keeping pace |
| Floating-rate debt and hedges | Small, or hedges with long remaining terms |
