In a TFSA or RRSP, a dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) is set-and-forget. In a taxable (non-registered) account it is set-and-record. Every reinvested dividend is **taxable income in the year it is paid**, even though the cash never reached your account, and every reinvestment is a **new purchase** that raises your adjusted cost base (ACB). Miss those purchases and you can end up paying tax on the same dividends twice: once when they were paid and again as an inflated capital gain when you sell.

This post covers DRIPs in taxable accounts from the ACB angle. For registered accounts, see [DRIP strategies inside registered accounts](/investing/dividends/drip-strategies-registered-accounts/). ACB basics for all events are in [adjusted cost base tracking for DIY portfolios](/investing/accounts/adjusted-cost-base-tracking-diy-portfolios/). It is educational, not tax advice; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

In a taxable account, a reinvested dividend is **reported on your T5 and taxed like a cash dividend** (with the gross-up and dividend tax credit for eligible Canadian dividends). The amount reinvested **adds to your total ACB**, and the new shares are averaged into your cost per share. Keep a record of **every DRIP purchase: date, shares received, price and amount**. Watch two traps: the **superficial loss rule** (a DRIP purchase within 30 days of selling at a loss can deny the loss) and **DRIP discounts**, whose tax treatment is technical enough to check against the plan's tax notes or a tax professional.

## Traps specific to taxable DRIPs

| Trap | What happens | How to handle it |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Superficial loss rule | Selling at a loss while a DRIP buys shares within 30 days before or after can deny the loss (added to ACB of the new shares instead) | Turn off the DRIP before tax-loss selling; see [the superficial loss rule](/investing/accounts/superficial-loss-rule-stock-swaps/) |
| Tax owed without cash | Dividends are taxable even though they were reinvested | Keep some cash elsewhere for the tax bill |
| Treasury DRIP discounts | Shares bought below market; tax treatment is technical | Read the plan's tax section; record price paid and market price; ask a tax professional if material |
| Return of capital on REITs and trusts | ROC reduces ACB, partly offsetting DRIP additions | Use the T3 breakdown each year; see [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/) |
| Shares at more than one broker | ACB is averaged across all your non-registered holdings of the same security | Keep one combined ledger |
| US-dollar stocks | Each DRIP purchase converted at that day's exchange rate | Record the CAD amount per reinvestment |

## Is a DRIP still worth it in a taxable account?

Often yes for core long-term holdings, because compounding and discipline still work and the record-keeping is manageable with a spreadsheet. It is less attractive when:

- the position is already large (cash lets you rebalance instead; see [position sizing](/investing/process/position-sizing-diy-stock-pickers/)),
- you plan to harvest losses on the name, or
- the record-keeping across many small DRIP purchases is something you won't actually keep up.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking reinvested dividends aren't taxable because no cash arrived.
- Using the original purchase price as ACB years later.
- Leaving a DRIP on during tax-loss selling.
- Relying on broker book value after a transfer between brokers.
