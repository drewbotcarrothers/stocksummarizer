Base metals miners rarely appear on dividend screens, but when copper or zinc prices are high their payouts and specials can look generous. That's usually the most dangerous time to buy for income.

This list goes with [how to analyze dividends in Canadian base metals](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-base-metals/), which explains the cash chain and price tests. Educational only; numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named.

## Direct answer

The common mistakes are setting expectations from a **peak-year payout**, counting **special dividends** as regular income, ignoring **single-mine and single-country concentration**, relying on **by-product credits** that can fall, ignoring **debt maturities** and **growth projects**, forgetting **smelter terms and currency**, and assuming metals that are "needed for the energy transition" can't fall in price. Nickel prices fell sharply in 2023 and 2024 as Indonesian supply grew, a reminder that long-term demand stories don't protect a dividend in the short term.

## Mistake 1: the peak-year payout

A dividend set at 30% of free cash flow in a strong year can become unaffordable when prices return to normal.

## Mistake 2: one mine, one country

Large base metals mines can produce most of a company's cash. A legal ruling, a strike, a flood or a change in government can stop that cash quickly.

First Quantum is the recent Canadian example. After Panama's Supreme Court ruled the Cobre Panama contract unconstitutional in late 2023 and production was halted, the company suspended its dividend in January 2024 and moved to sell assets and reduce spending.

## The full mistakes table

| # | Mistake | Why it happens | Better habit |
|--:|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | Expecting peak-year payouts to last | Recent results anchor expectations | Test the dividend at mid-cycle and trough |
| 2 | Counting specials as income | They show up in trailing yield | Strip specials; plan on the regular dividend |
| 3 | Ignoring single-mine concentration | Company looks diversified | Check the largest mine's share of EBITDA |
| 4 | Ignoring jurisdiction | Mines are far away | Read country risks and contract terms |
| 5 | Relying on by-product credits | Low reported costs look like efficiency | Check costs before by-product credits |
| 6 | Ignoring debt maturities | Debt sits in the notes | Map maturities against expected FCF |
| 7 | Ignoring growth project risk | Projects promise more production | Check budget, schedule and funding |
| 8 | Forgetting smelter terms | Concentrate sales are technical | Watch treatment and refining charges |
| 9 | Forgetting currency | Costs partly in local currency | Check FX sensitivities in the MD&A |
| 10 | Trusting long-term demand stories | Electrification needs metals | Short-term supply can still crash prices |

## Mistake 5: by-product credits

Copper miners often report costs "net of by-product credits," subtracting the value of gold, silver or molybdenum they also produce. That can make costs look very low. If by-product prices fall, reported copper costs rise even though the mine is unchanged. Compare costs before and after credits.

## Mistake 7: growth projects

Base metals companies frequently build or expand mines. Projects that run over budget or late can absorb cash that would otherwise support the dividend, and can push debt up just as prices weaken. Read project updates each quarter and compare the latest cost estimate with the original.

## Mistake 10: the demand story

Copper, nickel and other metals are expected to benefit from electrification over the long term. That doesn't stop prices from falling when supply grows faster than demand. Nickel's sharp price fall in 2023 and 2024, driven largely by expanding Indonesian supply, hurt producers around the world. A long-term thesis and a short-term dividend are separate questions.

## A base metals warning-sign list

| Sign | What to check next |
|:--|:--|
| Dividend raised after a record year | Payout at mid-cycle prices |
| Largest mine facing legal or permit issues | Share of EBITDA and alternatives |
| Net debt rising during a project | Funding plan and budget changes |
| By-product credits growing as a share of costs | Costs before credits |
| Large debt maturity within two years | Refinancing plan |
