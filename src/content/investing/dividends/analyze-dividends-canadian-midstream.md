"Midstream" and "pipelines" are often used as if they mean the same thing. For dividend analysis they don't. Large long-haul pipelines earn most of their cash from take-or-pay contracts and regulated tolls. Midstream companies built around **gas processing, NGL fractionation, storage and marketing** earn a larger share from volume-based fees and commodity margins. That changes how you should judge the dividend.

This post covers that processor and NGL side of the sector. For long-haul pipeline payout policy, see [how pipelines think about distributions vs dividends](/investing/dividends/pipelines-distributions-vs-dividends-policy/). Educational only; worked numbers are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Split the company's EBITDA into **take-or-pay or cost-of-service**, **fee-for-service tied to volumes**, and **marketing or commodity margin**. Then ask whether the dividend is covered by the **fee-based cash flow alone**, after interest, maintenance capital and cash taxes. If it is, marketing income is a bonus. If the dividend needs a good marketing year to be covered, it is more cyclical than the yield suggests. Then check **customer concentration and credit**, **contract terms**, **frac spreads**, and how **growth projects** are funded.

## Step 1: split EBITDA by contract type

Most Canadian midstream companies publish a segment table and state what share of EBITDA is fee-based. Turn that into three buckets.

| Bucket | What it is | How it behaves |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Take-or-pay / cost-of-service | Customer pays for capacity whether used or not | Stable through the cycle |
| Fee-for-service (volume-based) | Fee per unit processed, stored or moved | Moves with producer activity |
| Marketing / commodity margin | Buying and selling NGLs, crude, gas or products | Swings with prices and spreads |

## Step 2: test the dividend against fee-based cash flow

Worked example, C$ millions:

| Hypothetical | Strong marketing year | Weak marketing year |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Take-or-pay EBITDA | 700 | 700 |
| Fee-for-service EBITDA | 350 | 320 |
| Marketing EBITDA | 350 | 100 |
| Interest | −200 | −200 |
| Maintenance capex and cash taxes | −150 | −150 |
| Distributable cash flow | 1,050 | 770 |
| Of which fee-based | 700 | 670 |
| Dividend | 600 | 600 |
| Payout of total cash flow | 57% | 78% |
| Payout of fee-based cash flow | 86% | 90% |

A company in this position can say its dividend is funded by contracted and fee cash, with marketing paying for growth or buybacks. If fee-based payout were above 100%, the dividend would depend on commodity margins.

## Step 3: understand the frac spread

Gas processors and fractionators separate natural gas liquids (ethane, propane, butane, condensate) from raw gas. Marketing businesses earn a **frac spread**: roughly, the value of the NGLs sold minus the cost of the gas and fees used to get them. When propane and butane prices are high relative to natural gas, marketing margins expand. When they fall, so do margins. Many companies disclose a sensitivity to propane prices or frac spreads; note it.

## Step 4: look at who the customers are

Fee-for-service revenue depends on producers drilling and sending volumes. Check:

| Question | Where to look |
|:--|:--|
| Share of revenue from top customers | MD&A, AIF |
| Customer credit quality (investment grade share) | Investor presentation |
| Minimum volume commitments | Contract descriptions |
| Average remaining contract life | MD&A or presentation |

A plant with take-or-pay contracts from investment-grade producers is very different from one depending on volumes from a few small, levered producers.

## Step 5: export exposure and new markets

Some Canadian midstream companies export propane or butane to Asian buyers from terminals on the B.C. coast. Export margins add growth but also add shipping, tariff and price risks. Check how much EBITDA depends on exports and whether the terminal capacity is contracted.

## Step 6: funding growth

Processing plants, fractionators and export terminals cost billions. As with pipelines, look at how the equity share of projects is being funded and where leverage sits against the company's target. The [capex super-cycle post](/investing/dividends/capex-super-cycles-threaten-dividend-capacity/) covers the pattern.

## A midstream dividend checklist

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| Fee-based share of EBITDA | High and stable or rising |
| Dividend ÷ fee-based distributable cash | Below 100% in a weak marketing year |
| Top customer concentration | Low; mostly investment-grade |
| Contract tenor | Long, with minimum volume commitments |
| Marketing / export sensitivity | Disclosed and understood |
| Leverage vs target | Inside the range |
