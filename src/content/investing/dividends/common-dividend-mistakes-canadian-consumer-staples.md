Staples are the sector people buy when they want to stop worrying about dividends. That comfort causes most of the mistakes: assuming steady sales mean steady profit, ignoring the cash that goes to rent, dismissing low yields, or forgetting that food processors and fuel retailers are not grocers.

This list goes with [how to analyze dividends in Canadian consumer staples](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-consumer-staples/), which covers the cash chain, lease-adjusted free cash flow and buybacks. Educational only; numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named.

## Direct answer

The common mistakes are assuming **stable sales mean stable profits** when margins are thin, measuring payout **before lease payments**, **dismissing a low dividend yield** when buybacks do most of the work, treating **processors and convenience retailers like grocers**, ignoring **regulatory and pharmacy pricing risk**, chasing a **long growth streak** without checking EPS, and **overpaying for defensiveness**.

## Mistake 1: stable sales, stable profit

Grocery sales rarely fall much. Margins are another matter. A grocer earning 5 cents of operating income on each dollar of sales loses a tenth of its profit if that drops to 4.5 cents.

Promotions, shrink (theft and spoilage), wage increases and energy costs all move margins. Read the gross margin and operating margin discussion in each MD&A.

## Mistake 2: payout before rent

Most store leases are on the balance sheet under IFRS 16, and lease principal payments sit in financing cash flow. If you calculate free cash flow as operating cash flow minus capex, rent disappears.

| Hypothetical, C$M | Before leases | After leases |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Free cash flow | 2,100 | 1,300 |
| Dividends | 590 | 590 |
| Payout | 28% | 45% |

Use the after-lease number when you compare a grocer with a company that owns its stores.

Canadian grocers often yield 1% to 2%. Buybacks reduce the share count, which lifts future dividends per share. If you only compare dividend yields, you'll underrate staples. If you only compare total shareholder yields, you'll overrate them in years when buybacks are debt-funded. Look at both.

## Mistake 4: processors are not grocers

Dairy, meat and packaged food companies buy commodities (milk, hogs, grain) and sell to grocers with prices that adjust with a lag. When input costs jump, margins can stay squeezed for several quarters. Stress-test a processor's dividend with a wider margin range than a grocer's.

| Hypothetical processor | Normal year | Cost-squeeze year |
|:--|--:|--:|
| EBITDA (C$M) | 1,500 | 1,100 |
| Free cash flow (C$M) | 700 | 350 |
| Dividend (C$M) | 300 | 300 |
| Payout of FCF | 43% | 86% |

## Mistake 5: fuel margins inside convenience retailers

Convenience operators earn a large share of gross profit from fuel. Fuel margins per litre can swing widely from year to year, and long-term demand for gasoline is uncertain. Separate fuel from in-store profit when you assess dividend cover.

## Mistake 6: pharmacy and regulatory risk

Grocers with pharmacy operations are exposed to provincial generic drug pricing and professional fee changes. These are policy decisions, not market moves, and can change profit in a single budget. Read the risk section of the annual information form; see [how to analyze regulatory risk disclosures](/investing/analyze/analyze-regulatory-risk-disclosures-aif/).

## Mistake 7: the streak is not the test

Metro has raised its dividend every year for more than three decades. A long streak tells you about board priorities. It doesn't tell you the next raise is affordable. If dividend growth has run ahead of EPS growth for several years, the payout ratio is rising.

## Mistake 8: overpaying for safety

Defensive stocks can trade at high multiples when investors are nervous. A safe dividend bought at a high price can still deliver weak total returns for years. See [income vs total return: choosing a scoreboard](/investing/dividends/income-vs-total-return-choosing-scoreboard/).

## A staples warning-sign list

| Sign | What to check next |
|:--|:--|
| Operating margin down two quarters running | Promotions, shrink, wages |
| Dividends + buybacks above FCF after leases | Debt and leverage trend |
| Dividend growth above EPS growth for 3+ years | Payout ratio trend |
| Processor margin squeeze | Input cost trend and pricing actions |
| Policy change on drug pricing | Pharmacy share of profit |
