Most Canadian REIT investors look at the yield and the AFFO payout ratio and stop there. Those two numbers matter, but they describe the REIT as a whole. Your income depends on what happens **per unit**, and REITs issue units more often than almost any other kind of TSX company.

This post is about that per-unit lens: AFFO per unit, how equity issuance and DRIPs change it, and how refinancing feeds through to distribution capacity. For structure and tax character, see [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/); for valuation, see [valuing REITs with NAV, FFO and AFFO](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/). Educational only; worked numbers are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Analyze a REIT distribution by tracking **AFFO per unit** over several years, comparing it with **distribution per unit**, and asking what is changing the unit count. Units issued when the REIT's **AFFO yield is higher than the yield on what it buys** shrink AFFO per unit. DRIPs and unit-based compensation add slow dilution. **Refinancing** debt at higher rates cuts AFFO per unit directly. A REIT whose AFFO per unit is flat while its total AFFO grows is not growing your income.

## Step 1: build a per-unit table

Pull five years of AFFO, distributions and weighted average units from the MD&A. Divide each by the unit count.

| Hypothetical | Year 1 | Year 3 | Year 5 |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Total AFFO (C$M) | 120 | 127 | 135 |
| Weighted average units (M) | 100 | 104 | 108 |
| AFFO per unit | $1.20 | $1.22 | $1.25 |
| Distribution per unit | $0.96 | $0.96 | $0.99 |
| AFFO payout per unit | 80% | 79% | 79% |

If the per-unit line is flat or falling while the REIT reports "record AFFO," growth is coming from issuing units, not from the properties.

## Step 2: test whether equity issuance is accretive

REITs often buy properties with money raised by selling new units. Whether that helps existing unitholders depends on one comparison: the **AFFO yield on the units sold** (AFFO per unit ÷ unit price) vs the **AFFO yield on the property bought**.

Worked example: a REIT with 100 million units and AFFO of C$120 million (C$1.20 per unit) buys a C$150 million property that adds C$9 million of AFFO, a 6.0% yield. It funds the purchase entirely with new units.

| Issue price | AFFO yield on units | New units | AFFO per unit after |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| $13 | 9.2% | 11.5M | $1.157 (−3.6%) |
| $20 | 6.0% | 7.5M | $1.200 (flat) |
| $24 | 5.0% | 6.25M | $1.214 (+1.2%) |

This is why REITs trading well below NAV often stop issuing equity, and why a REIT that keeps issuing at low prices should worry you. Real deals usually mix debt and equity, so repeat the test with the actual funding mix in the deal release.

## Step 3: count the slow dilution

| Source of new units | Where to find it | What to check |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Public offerings | Prospectus supplements, news releases | Issue price vs NAV and AFFO yield |
| DRIP (often with a bonus) | Distribution release, MD&A | Participation rate and bonus percentage |
| Unit-based compensation | Notes to the financial statements | Units granted each year |
| Exchangeable units / LP units | Unit continuity note | Counted in AFFO per unit? |

A 3% to 4% DRIP bonus with modest participation can add around 1% a year to the unit count without any announcement. Over five years that's a few cents of AFFO per unit.

## Step 4: run the refinancing test

Most Canadian REITs have debt maturing every year. When old mortgages at low rates are refinanced at higher rates, interest expense rises and AFFO per unit falls, even if the properties perform well.

| Hypothetical | Amount |
|:--|--:|
| Debt maturing over two years | C$500M |
| Old rate | 3.0% |
| New rate | 5.0% |
| Extra annual interest | C$10M |
| On 110M units | −$0.09 AFFO per unit |

Find the debt maturity table in the MD&A, note the weighted average rate on maturing debt, and compare it with current borrowing costs.

## Step 5: read the payout per unit, not just the ratio

An 80% AFFO payout is reasonable for many Canadian REITs, but a falling AFFO per unit can push it above 100% within a couple of years. Look at the direction, and look at how management defines AFFO. Some REITs deduct a "normalized" maintenance capex reserve that may be lower than actual spending.

## Sector differences

| REIT type | Per-unit pressure to watch |
|:--|:--|
| Residential | Rent control limits, high development capex |
| Industrial | Low acquisition yields make issuance easy to over-do |
| Retail | Tenant failures, re-leasing costs |
| Office | Occupancy, leasing incentives, refinancing |
| Diversified | Mix changes from asset sales |

In 2020 several Canadian REITs cut distributions, including H&R REIT (halved in May 2020) and RioCan (down one-third, announced December 2020). In both cases AFFO per unit and payout pressure built up before the announcement.

## A REIT distribution checklist

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| AFFO per unit, five-year trend | Rising |
| Distribution per unit vs AFFO per unit | Distribution growing no faster |
| Recent equity issued at | An AFFO yield below the acquisition yield |
| Unit count growth from DRIP and compensation | Under about 1% a year |
| Refinancing over next two years | Impact on AFFO per unit estimated and manageable |
| AFFO definition | Maintenance capex close to actual spending |
