Real estate developers are not REITs. A REIT collects rent and pays most of it out. A developer buys land, gets approvals, builds, and sells or leases the finished product, so its profit arrives in lumps when projects close. Developers that pay dividends usually pay modest ones, and the analysis is about whether a steady dividend can sit on top of uneven income.

Educational only; worked numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named. For the rent-collecting side, see [how to analyze dividends in Canadian REITs](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-reits/); this post does not repeat the per-unit AFFO method.

## Direct answer

Analyze a developer's dividend by separating **recurring income** (rent, management fees) from **development profits** (closings and sales), testing the dividend against **recurring income alone** and against **multi-year average** profit, checking **land inventory and presales** that set future closings, and reading **construction debt and guarantees**. Development profit is often recognized when units close, so one year can be several times another.

| Income type | Timing | Dividend lens |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Recurring rental income | Monthly | Can support a steady dividend |
| Asset management and fees | Quarterly | Fairly steady |
| Development closings | When projects complete | Lumpy; better for specials or buybacks |
| Land sales | Occasional | One-off |

Read the segment note to see how much operating income each part contributes. A dividend that recurring income covers on its own is sturdier than one that depends on next year's closings.

## Step 3: land inventory and presales

Future closings come from today's land and presales. Read:

| Item | What it tells you |
|:--|:--|
| Land held (acres, lots, or density) | Years of future projects |
| Presold units and deposits | Closings already contracted |
| Projects under construction | Near-term closings and funding needs |
| Approval status | Timing risk |

Presales usually have to reach a threshold before construction lenders will fund a project. When presales slow, starts slow, and closings fall a few years later.

## Step 4: construction debt and guarantees

Developers fund projects with construction loans, often through joint ventures. The company may guarantee part of a partner's debt. Read the commitments and contingencies note and the joint venture note, not just the consolidated balance sheet. See [how to analyze off-balance-sheet commitments](/investing/analyze/analyze-off-balance-sheet-commitments/).

| Debt check | Where |
|:--|:--|
| Construction loans and maturities | Debt note |
| Guarantees of joint venture debt | Contingencies note |
| Share of projects held in joint ventures | Equity-accounted investments note |

## Step 5: value vs dividend

Developers are usually valued on net asset value, not yield. The dividend is often a small part of the return. If a developer trades at a large discount to NAV, buybacks may add more value per share than a higher dividend. See [what price to NAV means](/investing/valuation/what-price-nav-means-canadian-diy/).

## A developer dividend checklist

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| Dividend ÷ recurring income | Covered, or close |
| Dividend ÷ multi-year average profit | Comfortably below 100% |
| Presales and land pipeline | Several years of closings |
| Construction debt and guarantees | Disclosed and manageable |
| Use of windfall years | Specials or buybacks, not a higher base |
