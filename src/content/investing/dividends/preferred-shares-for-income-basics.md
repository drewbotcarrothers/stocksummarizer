Preferred shares sit between bonds and common stock. They pay a set dividend, rank ahead of common shareholders, and in Canada usually pay **eligible dividends**, which makes them a familiar tool for income investors in taxable accounts. But preferred dividends don't grow, the income on many issues resets every five years, and prices can move sharply with interest rates.

This post covers preferred shares from an income perspective for people who mainly own stocks. It is educational; figures are hypothetical. For credit analysis, ranking and non-viability features, see [how to analyze preferred shares and hybrid capital](/investing/analyze/analyze-preferred-shares-hybrid-capital/).

## Direct answer

A preferred share pays a **dividend set by its terms**, usually as a percentage of a **C$25 par value**. Income is **predictable but doesn't grow** the way a good common dividend can. On **rate reset** preferreds, the dividend is recalculated every five years as the 5-year Government of Canada yield plus a fixed spread, so your income can rise or fall at each reset. Prefs suit investors who want **stable, tax-efficient income** and accept **limited upside, interest-rate sensitivity, call risk and thinner trading**. They're a complement to dividend stocks, not a substitute for dividend growth.

## How preferred income compares

| | Common dividend stock | Preferred share | Corporate bond |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Income set by | Board, can grow or be cut | Share terms; can be suspended (non-cumulative) or accrued (cumulative) | Contract; missing a coupon is a default |
| Income growth | Possible | None (fixed) or reset-based | None |
| Ranking in liquidation | Last | Ahead of common, behind debt | Ahead of preferred |
| Tax in a taxable account | Eligible dividends (Canadian issuers) | Eligible dividends (Canadian issuers) | Interest, fully taxed |
| Price upside | Unlimited | Limited; issuers can redeem at par | Limited |

## Income on a rate reset preferred

Rate resets are the most common type in Canada. At each reset date, the new rate = **5-year Government of Canada yield + the issue's fixed spread**.

|:--|--:|--:|
| 5-year GoC yield 1% | 3.5% | $0.875 |
| 5-year GoC yield 2% | 4.5% | $1.125 |

| 5-year GoC yield 4% | 6.5% | $1.625 |

That's the income risk most first-time buyers miss: if government yields are low on the reset date, your dividend can drop for five years. Many rate resets also let holders convert into a floating-rate series at each reset, which pays a rate tied to short-term treasury bill yields. Check each prospectus for the exact terms.

## Yield on what you pay, not on par

Preferreds trade away from par. Yield to you is the dividend divided by **your price**.

| Hypothetical | Dividend | Price | Current yield |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Trading at par | $1.375 | $25.00 | 5.5% |
| Trading below par | $1.375 | $20.00 | 6.9% |
| Trading above par | $1.375 | $26.00 | 5.3% |

A pref trading above par can be **redeemed at par** by the issuer, often on a reset date, which would hand you a capital loss. Below-par prices often reflect a low reset spread, credit concerns or falling rate expectations.

## Account placement

| Account | How preferred income fits |
|:--|:--|
| Taxable | Eligible dividends get the gross-up and dividend tax credit, often the main reason Canadians hold prefs; see [the dividend tax credit and after-tax yield](/investing/dividends/dividend-tax-credit-after-tax-yield/) |
| TFSA | Tax-free, but you give up the dividend tax credit's advantage |
| RRSP / RRIF | Withdrawals taxed as income, so the eligible-dividend benefit is lost |

## Income-focused checklist

| Check | Why |
|:--|:--|
| Type (perpetual, rate reset, floating, minimum reset) | Determines how income changes |
| Reset spread and next reset date | Your income for the next five years |
| Cumulative or non-cumulative | Whether missed dividends must be made up; many bank-issued prefs are non-cumulative |
| Credit rating of the preferred (for example the Pfd scale) | Lower ratings mean higher suspension risk |
| Price vs par and redemption terms | Call risk and capital loss risk |
| Trading volume and bid-ask spread | Many prefs trade thinly |

Many investors use a preferred share ETF instead of single issues for diversification. That swaps issue-level risk for fund costs and the fund's own rules; read the [ETF factsheet](/investing/indexes-etfs/how-to-read-etf-factsheet/) closely.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a rate reset dividend as fixed forever.
- Buying above par without checking the redemption date.
- Assuming prefs are as safe as bonds; dividends can be suspended.
- Holding prefs in an RRSP for the dividend tax credit, which doesn't apply there.
