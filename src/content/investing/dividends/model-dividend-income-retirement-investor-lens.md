Many dividend investors want to know one thing: roughly how much income could this portfolio produce in five, ten or fifteen years? You can build a sensible range with a spreadsheet and four inputs. The danger is turning that range into a single number and treating it as a promise.

This is an investor-lens worksheet for estimating dividend income from a portfolio of stocks. It is **not** a retirement plan: it doesn't cover withdrawal strategies, RRIF minimums, government benefits, taxes or how much you need to retire. A fee-only financial planner is the right person for those questions. Everything here is educational, and all numbers are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Model dividend income as a **range**, not a point. Start from **forward annual income** (current dividends per share × shares held), apply a **conservative growth rate** (haircut the recent history), subtract a **cut scenario** for your weakest payers, and convert the result to **today's dollars** with an inflation assumption. Re-run it once a year with actual dividends. If the cautious case doesn't work for you, the plan depends on luck.

| Step | Input | Where it comes from | Common shortcut to avoid |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1. Forward income | Current annual dividend per share × shares | Latest declaration releases | Using trailing yield after a special dividend |
| 2. Growth | Weighted dividend growth rate | Five- and ten-year DPS history, haircut | Projecting the best decade forward |
| 3. Cut scenario | Income lost if the weakest payers cut | Payout and coverage review | Assuming no holding ever cuts |
| 4. Inflation | Annual inflation assumption | A long-run assumption you choose | Reading nominal income as spending power |
| 5. Range | Cautious, base and optimistic cases | The four steps above | Reporting only the base case |

Two lessons. First, flat dividends lose about a third of their spending power over 15 years at 2.5% inflation; see [how inflation changes what a stable dividend is worth](/investing/dividends/inflation-real-value-stable-dividend/). Second, small changes in the growth assumption compound into large differences, which is why the growth input deserves the most scepticism. [Dividend growth rate math over 10 years](/investing/dividends/dividend-growth-rate-math-10-years/) shows how to calculate and sanity-check it.

## Building the cut scenario

Assume something goes wrong. Rank holdings by dividend risk (payout ratio, FCF coverage, leverage, cyclicality) and apply a cut to the weakest.

| Hypothetical cut scenario | Income weight affected | Cut applied | Income lost |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Two weakest of 20 equal-weight payers cut by half | 10% | 50% | 5% |
| One sector at 30% of income cuts by a third | 30% | 33% | 10% |
| Severe recession: weakest quarter of income halved | 25% | 50% | 12.5% |

The second row is why concentration matters as much as individual safety. If a third of your income comes from one sector, one regulatory change or commodity slump can move the whole forecast. See [concentration risk in Canadian portfolios](/investing/process/concentration-risk-canadian-portfolios/) and [how to stress-test dividend safety in a recession](/investing/dividends/stress-test-dividend-safety-recession/).

## Putting the range together

| Case | Growth | Cut haircut | Inflation | Year 10 income, today's dollars (hypothetical) |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Cautious | 1% | 12.5% | 3.0% | about $14,400 |
| Base | 3% | 5% | 2.5% | about $19,900 |
| Optimistic | 5% | 0% | 2.0% | about $26,700 |

These are illustrations of the method using the C$20,000 starting income. The useful output is the spread: if your plans only work in the optimistic case, you're relying on everything going right.

## Common mistakes

- Starting from trailing yield that includes a special dividend or a dividend already cut.
- Using the portfolio's best decade of dividend growth as the base case.
- Forgetting currency swings on US dividends; see [how currency affects US dividend income](/investing/dividends/currency-us-dividend-income-canadians/).
- Treating the model as a plan. It estimates income; it doesn't decide how much you can safely spend.
