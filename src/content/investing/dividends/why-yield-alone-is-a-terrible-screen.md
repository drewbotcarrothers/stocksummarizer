Open any stock screener, sort the TSX by dividend yield, and the top of the list will be full of names whose prices have fallen hard. That isn't a coincidence; it's arithmetic. A screen that ranks only on yield mostly ranks on **how much the market doubts the dividend**. This guide explains why, and what to screen on instead.

Educational only — not financial advice. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

Dividend yield = annual dividend ÷ share price. When a price falls because investors expect a cut, the yield **rises** until the cut actually happens. Sorting by yield therefore pushes the riskiest dividends to the top. Use yield only as the *last* filter, after **free-cash-flow coverage, payout ratio, balance sheet, dividend history and business quality** — and compare yields within a sector, not across the whole market.

*The yield is backward-looking: it uses the last declared dividend. The market price is forward-looking. When they disagree sharply, the price is usually telling you something.*

## What a very high yield usually signals

| Signal | What may be going on | How to check |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Yield far above sector peers | Market expects a cut or a business problem | Compare with 3–5 peers; read the latest MD&A |
| Yield up because price fell 30%+ | Earnings or cash-flow downgrade, rising leverage | Recent quarterly results and guidance |
| Payout ratio above 100% of FCF | Dividend funded by debt or equity | [FCF coverage check](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/) |
| Recent large special dividend | Trailing yield inflated by a one-off | Use only the regular dividend |
| Variable/commodity-linked dividend | Payout floats with prices | Separate the fixed base from the variable part |

None of these proves a cut is coming. Some high yields are genuinely mispriced. But the burden of proof flips: a high yield needs an explanation **before** it gets your money. The [dividend trap guide](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/) covers the warning signs in more detail.

## Yield ignores total return

A 7% yield on a stock whose price drifts down 5% a year is a 2% total return. A 2% yield on a business compounding dividends and value at 8% a year is a 10% total return (hypothetical numbers). For Canadian DIY investors — especially in a TFSA, where there's no tax difference between dividends and capital gains — total return is what builds wealth. See [how dividend investing works](/investing/dividends/how-dividend-investing-works-canadian-diy/).

| Hypothetical stock | Yield | Annual price change | Total return |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| A — high yield, shrinking | 7% | −5% | 2% |
| B — moderate yield, steady | 4% | +2% | 6% |
| C — low yield, compounding | 2% | +8% | 10% |

## Cross-sector yield comparisons are apples to oranges

Different TSX sectors have different "normal" yields because of capital needs, growth runway and structure. A yield that is high for a software company is low for a pipeline. Compare within the group using our sector deep dives: [banks](/sectors/banks/), [utilities](/sectors/utilities/), [midstream & pipelines](/sectors/midstream-pipelines/), [telecom](/sectors/telecom/), [REITs](/sectors/real-estate-reits/).

REIT distributions and corporate dividends also differ in tax character, so yields aren't directly comparable in a taxable account — see [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/).

| Filter | Example rule of thumb (adapt to sector) |
|:--|:--|
| FCF coverage | Dividends ≤ free cash flow on a multi-year average |
| Payout ratio | Earnings, FCF, DCF or AFFO payout at a level the business model supports — see [payout ratio explained](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/) |
| Balance sheet | Leverage within the company's stated targets and in line with peers |
| History | No cut in the last downturn, or a cut followed by a disciplined reset |
| Yield | Ranked against sector peers, not the whole TSX |

Most screeners let you build at least some of these filters (payout ratio, debt-to-equity, dividend growth streak). Where they can't — FCF coverage is often missing — export the shortlist and check it yourself from the cash-flow statement. ## Common mistakes

- Sorting the whole market by yield and buying the top five.
- Using trailing yields that include special dividends.
- Treating an index of dividend payers as automatically "safe" — see [Canadian Dividend Aristocrats explained](/investing/dividends/canadian-dividend-aristocrats-explained/).
- Ignoring the after-tax yield by account type, especially for US payers — see [US dividend withholding tax](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/).
