Pipelines are some of the most popular dividend stocks in Canada. They own long-lived assets, most of their cash flow comes from contracts or regulated tolls, and several have raised their dividends for decades. That record is real, but it makes it easy to stop checking the things that can still go wrong.

This is a mistakes list. For how pipeline payout policy works (distributions vs dividends, DCF per share and guidance language), start with [how pipelines think about distributions vs dividends](/investing/dividends/pipelines-distributions-vs-dividends-policy/). Educational only; worked numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named.

## Direct answer

The common mistakes are judging safety on **yield and streak length**, using **EPS payout** instead of distributable cash flow, ignoring **leverage** (debt to EBITDA) and the **funding plan for growth projects**, treating every dollar of EBITDA as contracted, overlooking **DRIP and hybrid dilution**, reading a **spinoff reset** as a cut (or missing a real cut inside one), and forgetting **permit, legal and currency** risks on cross-border assets. Pipelines are steady businesses with heavy balance sheets, and most dividend problems start on the balance sheet.

## Mistake 1: letting the dividend outgrow cash flow per share

A pipeline can raise its dividend faster than its cash flow per share for years before anything looks wrong. The payout ratio just creeps up.

## Mistake 2: using earnings payout instead of cash flow

Pipelines carry large depreciation charges, so net income and EPS are usually well below cash flow. A dividend that is "150% of earnings" can still be well covered by distributable cash flow. The reverse mistake also happens: a comfortable EPS payout can hide weak cash flow if earnings include non-cash gains. Use the company's own DCF or AFFO-style measure, read its definition in the MD&A, and stay consistent across years. The [payout ratio explainer](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/) shows why the denominator matters.

## Mistake 3: ignoring leverage

Canadian pipelines usually publish a leverage target expressed as debt to EBITDA, often in the 4x to 5x range. That target matters more to the dividend than the yield does. A company sitting at the top of its range has less room to keep raising the dividend if a project runs over budget or rates rise.

| Leverage check | What to look for |
|:--|:--|
| Debt to EBITDA vs target | Inside the range, at the top, or above it? |
| Trend over three years | Rising leverage with rising dividends is a warning combination |
| Credit rating outlook | A negative outlook can force asset sales or slower dividend growth |
| Hybrid securities | Count their interest as a cost ahead of common dividends |

## Mistake 4: forgetting that growth projects need funding

Big projects take years of spending before they earn anything. If cash flow after the dividend can't cover the equity share of that spending, the money comes from new shares, asset sales, hybrids or a smaller dividend.

That is what happened at Inter Pipeline in 2020. Facing an oil price collapse and the remaining cost of its Heartland Petrochemical Complex, it cut its monthly dividend 72% and suspended its DRIP, saying retained cash was the most effective equity financing for the project.

| # | Mistake | Why it happens | Better habit |
|--:|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | Dividend growth outpacing DCF per share | Each raise looks small | Compare five-year growth rates of dividend vs DCF per share |
| 2 | Using EPS payout | EPS is the default on quote pages | Use the company's DCF measure and read its definition |
| 3 | Ignoring leverage | Yield gets the attention | Track debt to EBITDA against the stated target |
| 4 | Not asking how growth is funded | Projects are announced, funding isn't | Read the funding plan: retained cash, hybrids, asset sales, equity |
| 5 | Treating all EBITDA as contracted | "Pipeline" sounds fully fee-based | Check the share from marketing, processing margins or commodity exposure |
| 6 | Ignoring DRIP and hybrid dilution | Shares issued quietly through plans | Watch share count growth and any discounted DRIP |
| 7 | Misreading spinoff resets | Dividend history shows a drop | Add the spun-off company's dividend back before calling it a cut |
| 8 | Ignoring permit and legal risk | Assets have run for decades | Read the legal proceedings note for major lines |
| 9 | Overlooking currency | Listed in Toronto, paid in C$ | Check how much cash flow is earned in US dollars |
| 10 | Owning several pipelines as "diversification" | Different names, same drivers | Treat pipelines as one rate- and leverage-sensitive group |

## Mistake 5: assuming all of EBITDA is contracted

Most large pipelines say the bulk of their EBITDA is take-or-pay or cost-of-service. The rest can come from marketing, processing margins or volume-based fees that move with commodity prices and production. Read the segment table and the company's stated contracted share, and check whether it has been falling. For companies where that slice is larger, see [how to analyze dividends in Canadian midstream](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-midstream/).

## Mistake 7: spinoffs that look like cuts

When a pipeline spins off part of its business, the parent's dividend per share is often reset lower and the new company pays its own dividend. A screen that only reads the parent's history will flag a "cut." The fair test is whether a shareholder who kept both companies' shares received roughly the same total dividend. TC Energy's 2024 spinoff of South Bow is a Canadian example worth reading through in both companies' filings. The [splits and consolidations post](/investing/dividends/stock-splits-consolidations-dividend-per-share/) explains how per-share history gets distorted.

## Mistake 8: permit and legal risk doesn't go away

Long-lived assets can still face legal challenges. Enbridge's Line 5, which crosses the Straits of Mackinac in Michigan, has faced years of legal action by the State of Michigan over its easement. Single lines rarely threaten a large company's whole dividend, but they can tie up capital for years. Read the legal proceedings and contingencies notes in the annual report.

## A quick pipeline dividend check

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| Dividend growth vs DCF per share growth (5 yrs) | Similar, or dividend slower |
| Debt to EBITDA vs stated target | Inside the range |
| Share of EBITDA contracted or regulated | High and stable |
| Growth capex funded by | Retained cash and debt capacity, not discounted equity |
| Share count growth | Low, explained |
