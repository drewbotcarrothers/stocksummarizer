Most Canadian dividend stocks pay quarterly, so a portfolio of a few names can dump most of its cash into four months and leave the others empty. "Laddering" by payout month means mixing payers whose payment months don't all coincide, so cash arrives in a smoother monthly stream. It's a cash-flow calendar exercise, not a reason to pick a weaker company just to fill February.

This post is about building that calendar. For the behavioural and fundamental differences between monthly and quarterly payers, see [monthly vs quarterly dividends](/investing/dividends/monthly-vs-quarterly-dividends/); this post doesn't repeat that comparison. It's educational; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Map each holding's **typical payment months** and **expected cash** onto a 12-month worksheet. If four quarterly payers all follow a March-June-September-December cycle, your income arrives in four lumps. Mixing in payers on a February-May-August-November cycle, or a monthly payer, **smooths the cash** without changing the annual total. Use the calendar to plan spending and reinvestment dates, not as a stock-picking scorecard. Payment dates can shift by a week or two, and special dividends don't belong on the regular calendar.

The concentrated portfolio isn't "worse" as an investment. It's just lumpier. If you spend the income or reinvest it on a schedule, the lumps matter. If you don't, they mostly don't.

## Common Canadian payment-month cycles

| Typical payment months | Common among | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Mar, Jun, Sep, Dec | Many banks, some utilities | The most crowded cycle |
| Feb, May, Aug, Nov | Some utilities, telecoms, pipelines | Useful complement to the Mar cycle |
| Jan, Apr, Jul, Oct | A smaller set of payers | Fills the remaining quarter-ends |
| Monthly (every month) | Some REITs, utilities, ETFs | Smoothest; confirm whether the yield is funded |

These are typical patterns, not a promise. Always confirm the latest declared dates on the company's investor relations page or a TSX dividend notice.

## Rules that keep the calendar from running the portfolio

- **Don't buy a weaker company to fill a month.** If February is empty, holding a bit more cash that month is usually better than stretching for a name that fails your coverage tests.
- **Don't overweight a sector just because its payers land in a gap.** Sector risk matters more than calendar smoothness.
- **DRIPs ignore your calendar.** If dividends are reinvested automatically, the cash never hits your spending account; see [dividend reinvestment vs buying more of your best ideas](/investing/dividends/dividend-reinvestment-vs-best-ideas/).
- **Ex-dates aren't payment dates.** You need to own the shares before the ex-date, but cash arrives on the payment date, often weeks later; see [ex-dividend date mechanics](/investing/dividends/ex-dividend-date-mechanics-tsx/).

## When lumpiness is a real problem

| Situation | Why the calendar helps | What not to do |
|:--|:--|:--|
| You use dividends for living expenses | Avoid months with a cash shortfall | Don't reach for yield to fill a gap |
| You reinvest in lumps on a schedule | Know when cash will be available | Don't let idle cash sit for months unplanned |
| You hold a few large positions | One delayed payment is a big share of income | Diversify for quality reasons, not only timing |
