Canadian bank dividends have a long record, which is exactly why investors get careless with them. The mistakes below are the ones that show up again and again when DIY investors buy bank stocks for income: misreading yield spikes, ignoring capital, skipping the credit cycle and building more bank exposure than they realize.

This is a mistakes list only. For how the dividend decision works, see [how banks set and grow dividends in Canada](/investing/dividends/how-canadian-banks-set-grow-dividends/); for a full analysis walkthrough, the [first Canadian bank analysis playbook](/investing/pathways/first-canadian-bank-analysis-playbook/). Educational only; numbers are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

The most common mistakes are treating a **higher yield after a price drop** as a bargain without asking why the price fell, comparing **payout ratios built on different earnings measures**, ignoring **CET1 capital** and **provisions for credit losses**, missing what a **DRIP discount** says about capital needs, and stacking **individual bank stocks on top of index funds** that already hold the same banks. Each has a simple fix, listed below.

| Hypothetical, C$4.00 annual dividend | Price | Yield |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Normal market | $100 | 4.0% |
| Sector sell-off | $90 | 4.4% |
| Credit worries | $80 | 5.0% |
| Severe stress | $70 | 5.7% |

Bank yields rise when the market worries about credit losses, funding or capital. Sometimes that worry is overdone; sometimes it isn't. **Fix:** before buying on yield, read the latest provisions for credit losses, CET1 ratio and management commentary. See [why yield alone is a terrible screen](/investing/dividends/why-yield-alone-is-a-terrible-screen/).

## The full mistakes table

| # | Mistake | Why it happens | Better habit |
|--:|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | Treating a yield spike as a bargain | Yield looks high versus history | Ask why the price fell; check credit and capital first |
| 2 | Comparing payout ratios on different bases | Banks report reported and adjusted EPS | Use the same basis (usually adjusted) for every bank you compare |
| 3 | Ignoring CET1 | Capital ratios feel technical | Track CET1 against the bank's target and OSFI's requirements each quarter |
| 4 | Skipping provisions for credit losses | Focus on revenue and EPS | Watch PCLs as a share of loans; rising PCLs pressure dividend growth |
| 5 | Expecting a raise every quarter | Habits from past years | Learn each bank's usual raise timing; a skipped raise is a signal, not a disaster |
| 6 | Treating the 2020–21 freeze as a cut-proof guarantee | "Banks never cut" | The OSFI freeze stopped increases, not dividends; a future rule could differ |
| 7 | Missing the DRIP signal | DRIP terms are in the fine print | A new DRIP discount with treasury shares often means the bank wants capital |
| 8 | Ignoring US and international exposure | Thinking of banks as domestic | Check segment earnings and currency; foreign units can drive results |
| 9 | Over-concentrating in banks | Big Six plus index funds plus bank ETFs | Add up look-through exposure before buying another bank |
| 10 | Projecting the past decade's dividend growth forward | Strong history | Use a range; growth depends on EPS growth and the payout range |

## Mistakes 3 and 4: capital and credit come first

A bank's dividend comes after it meets regulatory capital needs. If CET1 is near the bottom of the bank's target range, extra profit is likely to go to rebuilding capital, not raising the dividend. When credit losses rise, provisions eat into earnings before the payout ratio is calculated.

| What to read each quarter | Where | Watch for |
|:--|:--|:--|
| CET1 ratio | Quarterly report, capital section | Falling toward the bottom of the target range |
| Provisions for credit losses | Quarterly report, credit section | Rising as a share of average loans |
| Adjusted EPS and payout | Results release | Payout drifting above the bank's stated range |
| DRIP terms | Dividend declaration release | Discount added or treasury issuance switched on |

[Bank operating metrics to track](/investing/sectors/bank-operating-metrics-to-track/) covers each of these in more detail.

## Mistake 7: missing the DRIP signal

When a bank switches its DRIP from buying shares on the market to issuing new shares, sometimes at a discount, it is raising a little equity every quarter. In 2023 several of the large Canadian banks did this to build capital. It isn't a dividend cut, but it tells you management wanted more capital cushion. For the shareholder mechanics, see [DRIP strategies in registered accounts](/investing/dividends/drip-strategies-registered-accounts/).

## Mistake 9: hidden concentration

A hypothetical investor holds a broad Canadian index ETF (with banks as its largest group), two bank stocks and a Canadian dividend ETF. Banks could easily make up a quarter or more of the total portfolio without any single position looking large. Add up the look-through weights before buying another bank. See [concentration risk in Canadian portfolios](/investing/process/concentration-risk-canadian-portfolios/).
