A dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) turns each dividend into more shares instead of cash. Inside a TFSA or RRSP, DRIPs are about as simple as investing gets: no tax slips, no adjusted cost base to track, and compounding runs quietly in the background. Simple isn't the same as automatic, though. Whether to DRIP, which kind of DRIP to use and when to switch it off are still decisions.

This post covers DRIP strategies **inside registered accounts**. For taxable accounts, where every reinvested dividend changes your tax records, see [DRIP strategies in taxable accounts](/investing/dividends/drip-strategies-taxable-accounts-acb/). It is educational; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

In a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA, reinvested dividends don't create taxable income or ACB records, so a DRIP is mainly a **compounding and discipline tool**. Broker ("synthetic") DRIPs buy **whole shares at market price**, usually free; some companies run **treasury DRIPs** that issue new shares, sometimes at a **1–5% discount**. DRIPs work best for holdings you would happily buy more of at today's price. Switch them off when a position is already large, when the valuation has run ahead of the business, or when you would rather direct the cash to a better idea. Note that **US dividends in a TFSA still lose 15% to withholding** before reinvestment.

| Option | How it works | Best for | Watch out for |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Broker (synthetic) DRIP | Broker buys whole shares with each dividend; leftover cash stays in the account | Most DIY investors | Small positions may not generate enough for one share |
| Company (treasury) DRIP | Company issues new shares, sometimes at a discount | Long-term holders of plan companies | Not all brokers pass through the discount; check eligibility |
| Manual reinvestment | You collect cash and choose what to buy | Investors who rebalance or add to best ideas | Commissions and the discipline to actually invest |
| Take the cash | Dividends accumulate as cash | Large or fully valued positions; building cash for new ideas | Cash drag if left uninvested |

## Registered-account specifics

| Account | DRIP tax effect | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| TFSA | None; growth is tax-free | US dividends lose 15% withholding first; see [the US dividends in a TFSA mistake](/investing/frameworks/mistake-us-dividend-payers-in-tfsa/) |
| RRSP / RRIF | None until withdrawal | US dividends generally exempt from withholding under the Canada–US tax treaty |
| FHSA | None if used for a qualifying home purchase | Shorter time horizon; reinvesting adds risk to a near-term goal |
| RESP | None until withdrawal | Consider de-risking as the child nears post-secondary age |

## When to turn the DRIP off

| Situation | Why |
|:--|:--|
| Position exceeds your target weight | DRIP keeps adding to your biggest risk; see [position sizing for DIY stock pickers](/investing/process/position-sizing-diy-stock-pickers/) |
| Valuation looks stretched | You'd be buying more at prices you wouldn't choose manually |
| Dividend safety is in doubt | Reinvesting into a possible cut compounds the problem; see [how to avoid dividend traps](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/) |
| A treasury DRIP is diluting other holders heavily | Large DRIP issuance can mask weak cash coverage |
| You want to rebalance | Collecting cash lets you top up underweight holdings instead |

## Treasury DRIPs and dilution

When a company issues new shares through its DRIP, especially at a discount, it keeps cash in the business but increases the share count. If participation is high, the "dividend" is partly paid in new shares rather than cash. That isn't bad on its own, but it means cash coverage of the dividend is weaker than the headline payout suggests. Check the shares issued under the DRIP in the cash-flow statement or equity note; see [how dilution changes per-share valuation math](/investing/valuation/dilution-per-share-valuation-math/).

## Common mistakes

- Leaving DRIPs on forever, so winners become oversized.
- Assuming a broker DRIP gets the company plan's discount.
- Forgetting that DRIPs in a TFSA don't recover US withholding tax.
- Treating reinvested dividends as "free" compounding when the business is weakening.
