"Dividend Aristocrat" sounds like a seal of quality. In Canada it's a specific index-eligibility test run by S&P Dow Jones Indices for the **S&P/TSX Canadian Dividend Aristocrats Index** — and the Canadian rules are much looser than many investors assume, especially compared with the US version. Knowing the actual rules lets you use the list as a starting point instead of a shortcut.

Educational only — not financial advice. Rules below are summarized from the S&P Dow Jones Indices methodology document; always check the [current methodology](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/indices/dividends-factors/sp-tsx-canadian-dividend-aristocrats-index/) because index rules change. Dividend histories in the examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

To be eligible, a stock (or income trust) must be listed on the TSX, sit in the S&P Canada Broad Market Index, have **increased its regular cash dividend in each of at least the past five years** — though it may hold the dividend **flat for up to two consecutive years** inside that window — and pass minimum size and liquidity tests. The index is then **weighted by indicated dividend yield**, with a cap on any single name. That's a test of *dividend policy history*, not of coverage, balance-sheet strength or valuation.

| Rule (summarized) | What it means for you |
|:--|:--|
| Increased regular cash dividends for at least five years | A *short* track record — five years may not include a recession |
| May hold the dividend flat for up to two consecutive years in that period | A company can qualify with only a few actual increases in the five-year window |
| Non-constituents must have raised in the first year of the review window | A new entrant can't start the streak with a flat year |
| Special dividends excluded; only the fixed part of fixed-plus-variable policies counts | Good — headline payouts are cleaned up |
| Initiations and re-initiations don't count as increases | A company that restarts a dividend can't count the restart as growth |
| Minimum float-adjusted market cap of C$300M plus liquidity tests | Screens out very small or illiquid names, not weak ones |
| Weighted by indicated annual dividend yield, single-stock weight capped (8% in the methodology at time of writing) | Higher-yielding names get more weight — see [why yield alone is a terrible screen](/investing/dividends/why-yield-alone-is-a-terrible-screen/) |
| At least 40 constituents; reviewed annually (January) with dividend data on a calendar-year basis | If too few names qualify, the methodology allows names with four years of growth to fill the gap |

## What the label doesn't test

| Not tested | Why it matters | Where to check |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Free-cash-flow coverage | A dividend can grow while being funded by debt | [FCF coverage of dividends](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/) |
| Payout ratio | High payouts leave no room for a bad year | [Payout ratio explained](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/) |
| Leverage | Rising debt can force a future cut | Balance sheet and MD&A liquidity section |
| Valuation | A great dividend payer can still be expensive | [Triangulating valuation](/investing/valuation/triangulating-multiples-dcf-peer-checks/) |
| Sector concentration | Yield weighting tilts toward high-yield sectors | Compare weights with the broad TSX |
| Size of increases | A token one-cent raise counts the same as a 10% raise | Dividend history on the company's investor site |

## How to use the list well

1. **As a universe, not a buy list.** Start from the constituents, then apply your own coverage, payout and balance-sheet checks.
2. **Check the gaps.** Some strong long-term dividend growers may be absent because of size, liquidity or a reset years ago. Some members may be weaker than the label suggests.
3. **Look at sector weights.** Yield weighting tends to concentrate in financials, energy infrastructure, utilities and real estate — the same areas Canadian investors often already overweight. See [banks](/sectors/banks/), [utilities](/sectors/utilities/), [midstream](/sectors/midstream-pipelines/).
4. **Track increases, not just streaks.** A slowing growth rate is often an earlier warning than a missed increase.
5. **Know when a stock drops out.** Removal after a cut confirms bad news; it doesn't warn you ahead of time. The [dividend trap guide](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/) covers earlier signals.

## Aristocrat ETFs vs picking your own

Some Canadian ETFs track this index. Owning the ETF gives you the rules above, applied mechanically, for a management fee. Picking individual names lets you add the coverage and valuation tests the index doesn't apply — at the cost of time and concentration risk. If you're deciding between the two approaches, the [ETF investor to stock picker pathway](/investing/pathways/etf-investor-to-stock-picker/) walks through the trade-offs.
