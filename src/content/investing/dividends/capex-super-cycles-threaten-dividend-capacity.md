A capex super-cycle is a period when a company commits to years of unusually heavy capital spending: a pipeline expansion, an LNG terminal, a fibre network, a new mine or a large grid build-out. During the build, cash flow goes into concrete and steel instead of dividends. If the dividend keeps growing at the same time, the gap has to be funded with debt or new shares, and cost overruns can turn a manageable plan into a dividend problem.

This post shows how to spot and size that risk. For how to read capex disclosures in the first place, see [how to read capex plans in MD&A](/investing/analyze/read-capex-plans-mdna/). It's educational; figures are hypothetical unless noted.

## Direct answer

Split capex into **maintenance** (keeping the existing business running) and **growth** (new capacity). During a super-cycle, growth capex can push **free cash flow below the dividend** for several years. That's sustainable only if the gap is funded within the company's **leverage target**, the project has **contracted or regulated returns**, and the timeline and budget hold. Watch for **cost overruns**, **delays** (cash out, no cash in), **rising leverage** and **equity issuance** that dilutes per-share cash. The dividend is most exposed when a super-cycle meets a downturn or higher interest rates.

## Where super-cycles show up in Canada

| Sector | Typical super-cycle | Main risks to dividends |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Pipelines and midstream | Major expansions, LNG-linked infrastructure | Cost overruns, regulatory delay |
| Utilities | Grid modernization, generation build-out | Rate-case lag, equity issuance |
| Telecoms | Fibre and 5G network build-outs, spectrum | Price competition during the build; see [telecom dividends](/investing/dividends/canadian-telecom-dividends-sustainability-framework/) |
| Miners | New mines and expansions | Commodity price drop mid-build; see [gold miner dividends](/investing/dividends/gold-miner-dividends-cyclical-income-reality-check/) |
| Energy producers | Oil sands or LNG supply projects | Commodity cycles; see [energy producer dividends](/investing/dividends/energy-producer-dividends-commodity-cycles/) |

Cost overruns are a real risk on large Canadian projects. TC Energy's Coastal GasLink pipeline, for example, ended up costing more than double its original estimate, and the company responded with asset sales and a plan to bring leverage down. The general lesson: build a margin of safety into any budget a company publishes.

## Warning signs during a build

| Signal | Where to find it | What it means |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Capex guidance raised | Quarterly MD&A, investor day | Larger funding gap |
| In-service date pushed back | Project updates | Cash out longer, cash in later |
| Leverage above target with no timeline | MD&A, rating agency reports | Balance sheet absorbing the overrun |
| Equity issuance or discounted DRIP | News releases | Per-share dilution |
| Asset sales "to fund growth" | News releases | Selling existing cash flow to finish the project |
| Dividend growth continues unchanged | Dividend declarations | Board prioritizing the record over the balance sheet |

## A super-cycle stress test

| Scenario | Question | Your estimate |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Base case | Is the FCF gap within the leverage target? | |
| 25% cost overrun | How much more debt or equity? | |
| One-year delay | How much cash flow is pushed out? | |
| Rates up 1 point | How much more interest on new debt? | |
| Overrun plus downturn | Is the dividend still payable without a large equity raise? | |
