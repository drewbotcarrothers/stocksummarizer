Trucking, logistics and air cargo stocks rarely pay big dividends, so the mistakes are less about dramatic cuts and more about misreading the cash behind a modest payout. Fuel surcharges inflate revenue, asset sales inflate cash flow, and fleet spending can be pushed into next year. Each can make a dividend look safer than it is.

This list goes with [how to analyze dividends in Canadian transportation](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-transportation/). Rail-specific mistakes are in [common dividend mistakes when buying Canadian rails](/investing/dividends/common-dividend-mistakes-canadian-rails/) and aren't repeated here. Educational only; numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named.

## Direct answer

The common mistakes are **reading revenue growth that is mostly fuel surcharge**, **counting sale-leaseback or equipment-sale cash as recurring**, missing **deferred fleet capex**, ignoring **lease payments** that sit below free cash flow, forgetting a **US-dollar dividend**, buying a **monthly yield after a rally**, and extrapolating a **freight-cycle peak**. Cargojet is a clean example of the second one: its Q3 2026 free cash flow was C$62.1M, down from C$152.4M a year earlier, when it had larger sale-and-leaseback proceeds.

## Mistake 1: confusing fuel surcharges with growth

Carriers pass diesel and jet fuel costs to customers through surcharges. When fuel rises, revenue rises with it, but profit doesn't.

Revenue grew 8% and margin fell. Many carriers report revenue "before fuel surcharge" for exactly this reason. Use that line for growth and for margin trends.

## Mistake 2: counting one-off cash as recurring

Sale-and-leaseback deals turn an owned aircraft or terminal into cash today and a lease payment tomorrow. Gains on selling used trucks also show up in cash flow. Neither is recurring.

| Cash source | Recurring? | Dividend lens |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Freight and logistics operating cash | Yes | The base for cover |
| Gains on used equipment sales | Cyclical | Shrink when used-truck prices fall |
| Sale-leaseback proceeds | No | Exclude from cover; add the new lease cost |
| Asset or division sales | No | Exclude |

## Mistake 3: missing deferred fleet capex

In a downturn, carriers often stop buying new trucks. Free cash flow jumps for a year or two, and the dividend looks extremely well covered. The bill comes later.

| Hypothetical trucking fleet | Normal | Deferral years | Catch-up year |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Operating cash flow (C$M) | 320 | 260 | 340 |
| Net capex (C$M) | −200 | −90 | −330 |
| Free cash flow (C$M) | 120 | 170 | 10 |
| Dividend (C$M) | 45 | 45 | 45 |
| Cover | 2.7x | 3.8x | 0.2x |

Compare capex with depreciation. If capex runs well below depreciation for several years, the fleet is ageing and a catch-up is coming.

## Mistake 4: ignoring lease payments

Under IFRS 16, lease principal payments sit in financing cash flow. "Free cash flow" in a press release may be calculated before them. Air cargo and courier operators often lease aircraft, hubs and vehicles, so the difference can be large. Subtract lease principal before judging cover.

## The full mistakes table

| # | Mistake | Better habit |
|--:|:--|:--|
| 1 | Treating fuel surcharge revenue as growth | Use revenue before fuel surcharge |
| 2 | Counting sale-leaseback or asset-sale cash | Use recurring operating cash only |
| 3 | Missing deferred fleet capex | Compare capex with depreciation over 3–5 years |
| 4 | Ignoring lease payments | Subtract lease principal from FCF |
| 5 | Forgetting a US-dollar dividend | Translate at a range of exchange rates |
| 6 | Buying a monthly yield after a rally | Recalculate yield at today's price |
| 7 | Extrapolating a freight peak | Use mid-cycle operating ratio |
| 8 | Ignoring contract concentration | Check the largest customers and contract end dates |
| 9 | Assuming airlines will pay dividends | Airline capital needs usually come first |
| 10 | Treating acquisitions as automatic growth | Check leverage vs covenant after each deal |

## Mistake 5 and 6: currency and stale yields

TFI International declares US$0.47 a quarter. A stronger Canadian dollar lowers your C$ income even when the dividend rises. Mullen Group pays C$0.07 a month; its indicated yield was about 3.1% at the early-October price and closer to 2.6% after the stock rallied on record Q3 results, per our [Mullen briefing](/summaries/2026-10-23-mtl-mullen-record-quarter/). Screens often show stale yields; recompute them yourself.

## Mistake 8: customer and contract concentration

A single large customer or partnership can make up a meaningful share of a carrier's volume. TFI said in October 2026 that a freight partnership with UPS would end in 2027 (see the [TFI briefing](/summaries/2026-11-01-tfii-q4-guide-ups-exit/)). Read the customer concentration and contract disclosures before assuming volume is stable.

## A transportation warning-sign list

| Sign | What to check next |
|:--|:--|
| Revenue up, revenue before fuel surcharge flat | Real volume and pricing |
| FCF jump in a year with asset sales | Recurring operating cash |
| Capex below depreciation for 3+ years | Fleet age and catch-up plans |
| "FCF" defined before lease payments | Lease principal in financing cash flow |
| Leverage rising after a deal | Covenant headroom |
| Largest customer contract ending | Replacement volume |
