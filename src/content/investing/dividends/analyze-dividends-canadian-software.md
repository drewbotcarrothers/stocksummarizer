Very few TSX software companies pay dividends, and the ones that do usually pay small ones. Shopify, Descartes and Kinaxis pay nothing. Constellation Software, Open Text and Enghouse pay something. For these companies the dividend is less about income and more about what it says on capital allocation: how much cash the business makes beyond what it can reinvest at high returns.

Educational only; worked numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named. For software business models, see [how to analyze software business models on the TSX](/investing/analyze/analyze-software-business-models-tsx/), and for acquirers, [how to analyze serial software acquirers](/investing/analyze/analyze-serial-software-acquirers-tsx/).

## Direct answer

Treat a software dividend as a **capital allocation signal** first and an income stream second. Measure payout against **free cash flow after stock-based compensation**, check how much free cash flow goes to **acquisitions** and at what returns, look for **special dividends** used to return excess cash, and test whether **recurring revenue** (maintenance and subscriptions) is large enough to carry the dividend if new licence sales stall. Constellation Software has paid a regular dividend of US$1.00 a share each quarter for more than a decade, plus a US$20 special dividend in 2019 when it said it had more capital than it needed.

## Step 1: what kind of software payer is it?

| Type | Example pattern | What the dividend signals |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Token dividend, acquisitions first | Small fixed dividend, most cash to deals | Discipline and confidence; not income |
| Mature cash generator | Growing dividend, modest growth | Fewer high-return reinvestment options |
| Leveraged after a big deal | Dividend held while debt is repaid | Debt comes first |
| No dividend | All cash reinvested | Growth still the priority |

Constellation's regular dividend has been flat at US$4 a year while the business has grown many times over, so its yield has fallen to a small fraction of a percent. That is consistent with a company whose main use of cash is buying other software businesses. A special dividend is a way to return cash without committing to a higher regular payment. See [how to treat special dividends in analysis](/investing/dividends/special-dividends-how-to-treat-in-analysis/).

| Use of cash | What to measure |
|:--|:--|
| Acquisitions | Price paid vs cash flow acquired; return on invested capital |
| Dividend | Share of free cash flow; any change in policy |
| Debt repayment | Leverage vs target after big deals |
| Buybacks | Price paid vs value; offset of share issuance |

If acquisitions are earning high returns, a small dividend is a sensible choice. If deal returns are falling, a higher dividend or buyback may be the better use of cash, and management commentary on that trade-off is worth reading.

## Step 4: free cash flow after stock-based compensation

Software companies often pay employees partly in shares. Stock-based compensation is a real cost: it adds shares or uses cash for buybacks to offset them. Subtract it from free cash flow when you calculate payout.

| Hypothetical, US$M | Amount |
|:--|--:|
| Reported free cash flow | 1,000 |
| Stock-based compensation | −250 |
| Free cash flow after stock comp | 750 |
| Dividend | 400 |
| Payout of reported FCF | 40% |
| Payout after stock comp | 53% |

## Step 5: recurring revenue as the dividend floor

Maintenance, support and subscription revenue usually renew at high rates, while licence and professional services revenue is lumpier. Compare the dividend with the cash generated by recurring revenue alone.

| Hypothetical revenue mix | Share | Dividend lens |
|:--|--:|:--|
| Maintenance and subscription | 75% | Carries the dividend in a weak year |
| Licence | 10% | Volatile |
| Services and hardware | 15% | Low margin |

## Step 6: leverage after a large deal

A large debt-funded acquisition can push leverage above target. Software companies that do this often hold the dividend flat while paying down debt. Check net debt to EBITDA against the company's own target and its timetable for getting back.

## A software dividend checklist

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| Dividends ÷ FCF after stock comp | Low, or clearly covered |
| Main use of free cash flow | Stated and consistent |
| Recurring revenue share | High enough to carry the dividend |
| Leverage vs target | At or below, or a clear path |
| Organic growth | Positive, separate from acquired growth |
