The payout ratio answers a simple question: **what share of what the company earns does it pay out as dividends?** It's the first number most investors check for dividend safety, and the one most often calculated on the wrong base. For Canadian operating companies — retailers, industrials, railways, telecoms, utilities, producers — the right base depends on how the business spends cash.

Educational only — not financial advice. Examples are **hypothetical**. (REITs use AFFO instead; see [valuing REITs](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/).)

## Direct answer

**Payout ratio = dividends ÷ earnings** (per share or in total). Calculate it three ways — on **reported EPS**, on **adjusted EPS**, and on **free cash flow per share** — and pay most attention to the FCF version for capital-intensive businesses. Compare the result with the company's **own stated payout target** and with sector peers. A ratio that's rising toward or above 100% of FCF for several years is the warning sign, not any single number.

## Three ways to compute it

| Version | Formula | Strength | Weakness |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Earnings payout | Dividends per share ÷ reported EPS | Simple; widely quoted | Distorted by one-offs, impairments, non-cash items |
| Adjusted earnings payout | Dividends per share ÷ adjusted EPS | Often matches management targets | "Adjusted" is management's definition — check the bridge |
| **FCF payout** | Dividends paid ÷ (cash from operations − capex − lease principal) | Closest to cash reality | Lumpy year to year; average over 3–5 years |

## Worked example (hypothetical)

| Item (per share) | Company A — asset-light retailer | Company B — capital-heavy telecom |
|:--|--:|--:|

| Adjusted EPS | $3.40 | $2.80 |

| **Adjusted payout** | 59% | 71% |
| **FCF payout** | **56%** | **95%** |

## Which payout ratio to use, by sector

| TSX business type | Lean on | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Asset-light (retail, staples, services, software) | Earnings or FCF payout — usually similar | Low capex keeps earnings close to cash |
| Capital-heavy (telecom, rails) | FCF payout | Large ongoing capex; D&A may not match spending |
| Pipelines / midstream | DCF payout (distributable cash flow) | Industry convention; check how growth capex is excluded — see [midstream](/sectors/midstream-pipelines/) |
| Regulated utilities | Adjusted earnings payout vs target, plus equity issuance | Growth capex is often funded externally by design — see [utilities](/sectors/utilities/) |
| Producers / miners | FCF payout at mid-cycle prices; base vs variable dividend | Commodity swings — see [oil & gas producers](/sectors/oil-gas-producers/) |
| Banks, insurers | Adjusted earnings payout vs target, plus capital ratios | FCF isn't meaningful for lenders — see [banks](/sectors/banks/) |

| Where the ratio sits | Typical reading |
|:--|:--|
| Inside the target range | Policy working as described |
| At the top of the range for 2+ years | Dividend growth likely to slow |
| Above the range | Ask how and when it gets back — earnings growth, slower raises, or a cut |
| Target quietly changed or redefined | Treat as a warning sign; see [reading non-GAAP metrics](/investing/analyze/read-non-gaap-metrics-critically/) |

## Payout ratio and dividend growth

A lower payout leaves room to grow the dividend faster than earnings for a while; a high payout means dividend growth can't outpace earnings growth for long.

| Hypothetical | Payout 50% | Payout 90% |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Earnings growth 5% a year | Dividend can grow 5%+ and still leave a cushion | Dividend growth capped near 5%, little cushion |
| Earnings flat for 2 years | Dividend can still rise modestly | Raises likely pause |
| Earnings fall 20% | Payout rises to ~63% — manageable | Payout rises to ~113% — a cut becomes likely |

## Common mistakes

- Using reported EPS in a year with a big impairment (payout looks scary) or a big gain (looks safe).
- Using data-site payout ratios without checking which earnings they use.
- Judging a telecom or railway on earnings payout alone.
- Forgetting preferred dividends and minority interests when computing the common payout.
