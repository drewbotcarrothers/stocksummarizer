Before 2006, income trusts were one of the most popular ways for Canadians to own businesses for income. Companies from pipelines to restaurant royalty owners converted to trusts that paid out most of their cash pre-tax. On October 31, 2006, the federal government announced a tax on specified investment flow-through entities (SIFTs), and the model ended for most businesses.

The history matters less than the lessons it teaches about structure. Today's REITs, limited partnerships, royalty companies and holding companies all raise similar questions. For the pipeline-specific angle on distributions vs dividends, see [how pipelines think about distributions vs dividends](/investing/dividends/pipelines-distributions-vs-dividends-policy/); this post focuses on lessons that carry across structures. It's educational, not tax advice.

## Direct answer

Income trusts paid distributions from pre-tax cash because the trust itself paid little tax, which made their yields look high relative to corporations. When Ottawa announced the SIFT tax in 2006 (fully applying from 2011 for existing trusts, with REITs that meet the REIT exception excluded), most trusts converted to corporations and many reset their payouts. The lasting lessons: **a payout reflects structure and tax treatment, not just business quality**; **tax-driven advantages can be removed by policy**; **paying out nearly all cash leaves little room for reinvestment or bad years**; and **when a structure changes, check distributable cash per unit before and after**.

Investors who held through conversions saw that the business hadn't changed, but the cash that could reach them had. Tax treatment also shifted at the investor level: trust distributions were often a mix of income and return of capital, while corporate dividends can qualify for the dividend tax credit. See [how the dividend tax credit changes after-tax yield](/investing/dividends/dividend-tax-credit-after-tax-yield/).

## Lessons that still apply

| Lesson from the trust era | Where it shows up today | What to check |
|:--|:--|:--|
| High payouts can be a structure artifact | REITs, LPs, some royalty vehicles | Payout vs distributable cash (AFFO, DCF per unit) |
| Policy risk is real | Tax rules for REITs, partnerships and foreign income | Whether the yield depends on a specific tax treatment |
| Paying out nearly all cash limits flexibility | High-payout REITs and income funds | How growth is funded: debt, equity issuance or retained cash |
| Return of capital can hide erosion | Funds and REITs with ROC in distributions | NAV or book value per unit over time; see [return of capital distributions](/investing/dividends/return-of-capital-distributions-what-to-track/) |
| Structure changes reset the baseline | Conversions, spin-offs, simplifications | Distributable cash per unit before and after |

## Structure questions for any income vehicle

| Question | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| Who pays tax: the entity, the investor or both? | Determines how much cash can reach you |
| What metric does management use to set the payout? | AFFO, distributable cash flow and EPS lead to different ratios |
| What share of cash is paid out? | Higher payouts leave less cushion and more reliance on capital markets |
| How is growth funded? | Unit issuance dilutes; debt adds risk |
| Could a rule change alter the model? | Some structures depend on specific tax exemptions |

## Common mistakes

- Comparing a REIT's or LP's distribution yield directly with a corporation's dividend yield without adjusting for structure and tax treatment. See [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/).
- Assuming a long payout record under one structure will continue after a conversion.
- Treating a tax advantage as permanent.
