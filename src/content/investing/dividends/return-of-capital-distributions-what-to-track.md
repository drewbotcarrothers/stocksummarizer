Some of the income you receive from REITs, income funds, covered call ETFs and certain corporations is not income at all for tax purposes. It is **return of capital (ROC)**: part of your own investment handed back to you. ROC isn't taxed when you receive it, which makes it look attractive, but it lowers your adjusted cost base (ACB) and can hide a payout that the investment isn't actually earning.

This post covers what ROC is, where it shows up, what to track and how to tell a harmless ROC from one that is slowly shrinking your investment. It is educational; figures are hypothetical and this is not tax advice.

## Direct answer

ROC is a distribution that tax rules treat as a **repayment of your capital** rather than income. In a taxable account you **don't pay tax when you receive it**, but you **must subtract it from your ACB**, which raises your capital gain (or shrinks your loss) when you sell. If ROC pushes your ACB below zero, the negative amount is treated as a capital gain in that year and your ACB resets to zero. Beyond tax, ask the economic question: **is the issuer earning the distribution, or paying out more than it earns?** The first is usually a tax-timing quirk; the second erodes value.

## Where return of capital shows up

| Investment | Why ROC appears | Where you see it |
|:--|:--|:--|
| REITs | Depreciation and other deductions shelter part of distributable cash, so some of the payout is classed as ROC | T3 slip; the REIT's annual tax allocation |
| Income funds and many ETFs | Distributions above the fund's taxable income for the year | T3 slip; the fund's annual tax breakdown |
| Covered call ETFs | Fixed monthly payouts can exceed income and realized gains | T3 slip; fund distribution history |
| Limited partnerships | Partnership allocations differ from cash paid | T5013 slip |
| Corporations (rare) | A formal reduction of paid-up capital, often after an asset sale | News release and the company's tax notice |

The tax character (income, capital gains, ROC) of a fund or REIT's distributions is usually only known **after year end**, when the issuer publishes its breakdown. The cash you received during the year doesn't tell you the split.

## What ROC does to your adjusted cost base

Take a hypothetical holding: 1,000 units bought at C$20.00, so ACB is C$20,000. The fund pays C$1.20 per unit a year, and its tax breakdown shows C$0.48 of that as ROC.

| Hypothetical | Per unit | 1,000 units |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Purchase price (starting ACB) | $20.00 | $20,000 |
| Annual distribution | $1.20 | $1,200 |
| of which return of capital | $0.48 | $480 |
| of which taxable income | $0.72 | $720 |
| ACB after one year | $19.52 | $19,520 |
| ACB after five years | $17.60 | $17,600 |

## Harmless ROC vs destructive ROC

Tax-classed ROC and economic ROC are different things. A REIT may class part of a well-covered distribution as ROC simply because depreciation reduced its taxable income; the property portfolio and its cash flow are intact. A fund that pays a fixed distribution it doesn't earn is literally handing your capital back, and the net asset value (NAV) per unit falls.

### Quick questions to tell them apart

- Is NAV or book value per unit **stable or rising** over several years, or drifting down?
- For a REIT, is the distribution covered by **AFFO**? See [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/).
- Is the payout a **fixed amount** that doesn't change with what the fund earns?
- Does management explain the source of distributions in the MD&A or fund reports?

## Account type changes the tax story, not the economics

| Account | Does ROC lower ACB? | Does destructive ROC still hurt? |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Taxable | Yes, you must track it | Yes |
| TFSA | No ACB tracking needed for tax | Yes |
| RRSP / RRIF | No ACB tracking needed for tax | Yes |
| FHSA | No ACB tracking needed for tax | Yes |

Inside registered accounts the tax label is irrelevant, but a fund that shrinks its NAV to fund a payout shrinks your registered balance just the same.

## Common mistakes

- Treating ROC as "tax-free income." It is tax-deferred in a taxable account, and in economic terms it may be your own money.
- Forgetting to reduce ACB, then under-reporting the gain when you sell.
- Missing the deemed gain when ACB goes below zero.
- Comparing a fund's distribution yield with a company's dividend yield without checking how much is ROC.
- Assuming all ROC is bad. For many REITs, it reflects tax deductions, not an uncovered payout.
