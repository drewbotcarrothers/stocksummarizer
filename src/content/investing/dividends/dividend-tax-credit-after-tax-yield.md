A 4% dividend yield and a 4% GIC rate look identical on a screen. In a taxable account they are not. Eligible Canadian dividends come with the **dividend tax credit**, which can leave you with noticeably more after tax than the same amount of interest or foreign dividend income. If you compare yields across stocks, bonds and US payers without adjusting for that, you are comparing the wrong numbers.

What "eligible" and "non-eligible" mean, and how the gross-up shows up on your return, is covered in [eligible dividends vs ordinary dividends](/investing/dividends/eligible-vs-ordinary-dividends/). This post shows how to turn a **pre-tax yield into an after-tax yield** and when that changes a decision. It is educational, not tax advice; rates below are illustrative.

## Direct answer

For eligible Canadian dividends in a **taxable** account, the cash dividend is **grossed up by 38%**, taxed at your marginal rate, then reduced by a **federal dividend tax credit of 15.0198% of the grossed-up amount** plus a provincial credit. The result is a lower effective tax rate on dividends than on interest at most income levels. **After-tax yield = pre-tax yield × (1 − your effective tax rate on that type of income).** To compare a dividend with interest, convert it to an **interest-equivalent yield**: dividend yield × (1 − dividend tax rate) ÷ (1 − interest tax rate). Inside a TFSA or RRSP none of this applies.

Non-eligible dividends (often from small private corporations, sometimes from income trusts or certain issuers) use a **15% gross-up** and a federal credit of **9.0301%** of the grossed-up amount, so their tax advantage is smaller.

## Interest-equivalent yield (illustrative)

What interest rate would you need to match a 4% eligible dividend after tax?

| Bracket | After-tax on 4% dividend | Interest-equivalent yield |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Middle | 3.72% | 5.31% |
| Higher | 3.00% | 5.26% |
| Top | 2.44% | 5.19% |

Formula: 4% × (1 − dividend rate) ÷ (1 − interest rate). For example, at the middle bracket: 4% × 0.93 ÷ 0.70 = 5.31%.

## How different income types compare

| Income type | Tax treatment in a taxable account | After-tax yield effect |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Eligible Canadian dividends | Gross-up and dividend tax credit | Usually the most tax-efficient income at low-to-middle incomes |
| Non-eligible dividends | Smaller gross-up and credit | Less efficient than eligible |
| Interest (GICs, bonds, HISAs) | Fully taxed at your marginal rate | Least efficient |
| US and other foreign dividends | Fully taxed like interest; foreign tax credit for withholding | No dividend tax credit; see [US dividend withholding tax](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/) |
| REIT and trust distributions | Mix of income, capital gains and return of capital | Depends on the breakdown on your T3; see [REIT distributions vs corporate dividends](/investing/dividends/reit-distributions-vs-corporate-dividends/) |
| Capital gains | Only part of the gain is taxable, when realised | Often competitive at high incomes |

## When it should change a decision

- **Account placement.** Eligible Canadian dividend payers are often the natural fit for a taxable account once registered room is used; see [account location for Canadian dividend stocks](/investing/accounts/account-location-canadian-dividend-stocks/).
- **Yield comparisons.** A 5% bond yield and a 4% eligible dividend yield may be roughly even after tax, before considering risk and growth.
- **Income-tested benefits.** The grossed-up amount (not the cash) counts toward net income, which can affect items like the OAS recovery tax. At higher incomes this can reduce the advantage.
- **Never as the only reason to buy.** A tax credit on a dividend that gets cut is worth nothing; see [why yield alone is a terrible stock screen](/investing/dividends/why-yield-alone-is-a-terrible-screen/).

## Common mistakes

- Comparing pre-tax yields across dividends, interest and US payers.
- Applying the dividend tax credit to dividends held in a TFSA or RRSP (it doesn't apply there).
- Assuming every Canadian dividend is eligible; check the company's dividend announcements.
- Forgetting the gross-up when estimating income for benefit thresholds.
