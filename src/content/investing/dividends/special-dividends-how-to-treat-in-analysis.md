Every so often a Canadian company announces a **special dividend**: a one-time payment on top of the regular dividend, often after an asset sale, a strong year or a build-up of excess cash. It's welcome cash for shareholders, but it can quietly wreck your analysis. Leave it in the trailing yield and the stock looks like a high-yield bargain. Treat it as recurring and you overestimate future income. Forget the share price drop on the ex-dividend date and you think the stock "fell" for no reason.

This post shows how to treat special dividends in yield, payout, valuation and total return. It is educational; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Treat a special dividend as a **one-time return of capital to shareholders in cash**, not as part of the ongoing dividend. **Exclude it from forward yield and payout ratio**, **include it once in total return**, and **find where the cash came from** (asset sale proceeds, accumulated excess cash, a tax-driven distribution or a windfall year). Expect the share price to drop by roughly the special dividend amount on the ex-dividend date. A special dividend is a signal about **capital allocation**, not about dividend growth.

## Where the cash came from matters

| Source | What it suggests | Follow-up question |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proceeds from selling a business | The company is smaller now | What earnings and cash flow left with the asset? Rebase your estimates |
| Accumulated excess cash | Management doesn't see better uses | Is this better than a buyback? See [buybacks per-share math](/investing/valuation/buybacks-per-share-valuation-math/) |
| A windfall year (commodity spike, one-off gain) | Earnings were unusually high | Will normal earnings support the regular dividend? See [spot one-time items](/investing/analyze/spot-one-time-items-canadian-statements/) |
| Variable or "base plus special" policy | Payout flexes with cash flow by design (common in energy) | What formula does the company use? |
| Funded with debt | Rare and worth scrutiny | Leverage after the payment; see [interest coverage](/investing/analyze/analyze-interest-coverage-levered-tsx/) |

## Base-plus-variable dividend policies

Some Canadian energy producers pay a modest base dividend plus variable or special payments tied to free cash flow above a set level. For these, the **base dividend** is what to use for safety analysis, and the variable portion should be modelled against your commodity price assumptions; see [how commodity price assumptions dominate resource valuations](/investing/valuation/commodity-price-assumptions-resource-valuations/) and [how oil and gas producers make money](/investing/sectors/how-oil-gas-producers-make-money/).

## Tax and account notes

- In a taxable account, a special dividend from a Canadian corporation is usually reported like any other dividend on your T5, often designated eligible; check the company's announcement.
- Some special distributions are designated as **capital gains dividends** or **return of capital**, which are treated differently; ROC reduces ACB. See [adjusted cost base tracking](/investing/accounts/adjusted-cost-base-tracking-diy-portfolios/).
- Inside a TFSA or RRSP, the tax label doesn't matter, though US special dividends in a TFSA still lose withholding tax.

## Common mistakes

- Screening on trailing yield that includes a special dividend.
- Treating a special as a sign the regular dividend will grow.
- Ignoring that a special funded by an asset sale shrinks future earnings.
- Thinking the ex-date price drop is a loss; you received the cash.
