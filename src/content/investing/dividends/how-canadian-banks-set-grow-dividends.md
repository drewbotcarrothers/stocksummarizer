Canada's large banks have long been core holdings for dividend investors. Their dividends look steady from the outside, but each increase comes from a set of decisions: how much the bank earned, how much capital it needs to keep, what payout range the board has set and what regulators expect. Knowing that chain helps you judge how much room a bank has to keep raising, and what could slow it down.

This post walks through how Canadian banks set and grow dividends. It is educational; the bank figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

A Canadian bank's dividend is a **board decision constrained by capital**. The bank earns profit, keeps enough to hold its **CET1 capital ratio** above its internal target (which sits above OSFI's minimum plus buffers), then pays a dividend within a **target payout range of adjusted earnings** that it publishes in its annual report. Increases usually follow **earnings growth**; when earnings fall, banks tend to hold the dividend and let the payout ratio rise rather than cut. Regulators can also step in: in March 2020, OSFI told federally regulated banks not to raise dividends or buy back shares, and lifted that restriction in November 2021.

## The payout range: the main dial

Large Canadian banks publish a **target dividend payout range** as a percentage of adjusted earnings, commonly stated around 40% to 50%. Check each bank's current annual report, as the wording and range can change.

A hypothetical bank earning C$8.00 of adjusted EPS with a 45% payout would pay about C$3.60 a year, or C$0.90 a quarter. If EPS grows 6% to C$8.48, the same payout supports about C$3.82, roughly a 6% increase.

A payout above the range doesn't automatically mean a cut. Banks can run above target for a period, especially when provisions for credit losses spike temporarily. What matters is whether **capital** stays healthy while they do it.

## Why capital comes before the dividend

Retained earnings build common equity, the "C" in CET1. Every dollar paid out is a dollar not added to capital. A simplified illustration:

| Hypothetical bank | Value |
|:--|--:|
| Risk-weighted assets (RWA), start | C$600B |
| CET1 capital at 13.0% | C$78.0B |
| Annual net income | C$16.0B |
| Dividends at 45% | C$7.2B |
| Retained earnings | C$8.8B |
| CET1 ratio next year if RWA is flat | 14.5% |
| CET1 ratio next year if RWA grows 5% to C$630B | 13.8% |

Loan growth raises RWA and absorbs capital. So does a large acquisition or a model change. When capital is tight, banks usually slow **buybacks** first and protect the dividend. OSFI sets minimum ratios plus buffers, including a Domestic Stability Buffer that it reviews twice a year, so the capital bar itself can move. For the metric details, see [bank operating metrics to track](/investing/sectors/bank-operating-metrics-to-track/).

## How increases usually happen

- **Timing:** increases are typically announced alongside quarterly results. Some banks have raised twice a year in normal periods; check each bank's dividend history rather than assuming a pattern.
- **Size:** increases tend to track earnings growth, not a fixed percentage.
- **Pauses:** a held dividend often reflects a payout near the top of the range, a capital build or regulatory guidance, as in 2020–2021.
- **Cuts:** the large Canadian banks have a long record of avoiding cuts, but past behaviour is not a promise. A deep credit loss cycle or a capital shortfall could change the calculus.

## What to watch as an investor

| Signal | Healthy | Worth a closer look |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Payout ratio vs stated range | Inside or below | Above the range for several quarters |
| CET1 vs management target | Comfortably above | Near target with RWA growing fast |
| Provisions for credit losses | Stable or normalizing | Rising sharply, or guidance raised repeatedly |
| Buybacks | Active, funded by surplus capital | Suspended to protect capital |
| Management language | "Consistent with our payout range" | "Prudent," "evaluating," talk of rebuilding capital |

## Common mistakes

- Projecting past dividend growth forward without checking earnings and capital.
- Treating a payout ratio above the range as a cut signal, without looking at CET1.
- Comparing bank payout ratios directly with utilities or pipelines. Banks retain capital for regulatory reasons, so their payout ratios are structurally lower.
- Ignoring that OSFI can restrict dividend increases sector-wide, as it did in 2020.
