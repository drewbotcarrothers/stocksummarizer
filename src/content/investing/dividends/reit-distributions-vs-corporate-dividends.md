A Canadian REIT paying a 5.5% distribution and a Canadian bank paying a 5.5% dividend look like the same kind of income on a screener. They aren't. They come from different legal structures, are judged by different safety metrics, carry different **tax character**, and can affect your **adjusted cost base** differently. For DIY investors who mix REITs and dividend stocks, these differences change both the analysis and the after-tax result.

Educational only — not tax or financial advice. Confirm tax treatment with the CRA, your slips or a tax professional. Examples are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

A **corporate dividend** is paid out of a company's after-tax profits and, for most large TSX companies, is an *eligible dividend* reported on a **T5** slip. A **REIT distribution** is a flow-through of the trust's cash, reported on a **T3** slip, and can be a mix of **other income, capital gains, return of capital (ROC)** and sometimes foreign income. ROC isn't taxed when received but **reduces your adjusted cost base**, raising the capital gain when you sell. Judge REIT safety on **AFFO payout**, not earnings. In a TFSA or RRSP, the tax-character differences don't matter.

| | Corporate dividend | REIT distribution |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Legal payer | Corporation | Trust (units, not shares) |
| Tax slip | T5 | T3 (or the broker's summary) |
| Typical tax character | Eligible dividend | Mix: other income, capital gains, return of capital, foreign income |
| Dividend tax credit | Yes (eligible or non-eligible) | Only on any portion designated as a dividend (often small or none) |
| Affects adjusted cost base | No | Yes, if any portion is return of capital |
| Usual safety metric | Earnings or FCF payout | AFFO payout, leverage |
| Usual frequency | Quarterly | Often monthly |

## Tax character in a taxable account (hypothetical)

Suppose a REIT pays $1,000 of distributions in a year, and the T3 breaks it down like this:

| Component | Amount | How it's taxed (summary) |
|:--|--:|:--|
| Other income | $550 | Fully taxable at your marginal rate |
| Capital gains | $150 | Half included in income at the current inclusion rate |
| Return of capital | $300 | Not taxed now; reduces your ACB |
| **Total** | **$1,000** | |

The breakdown changes year to year and is only known after year-end, when the REIT publishes its tax allocation. Compare that with $1,000 of eligible dividends from a TSX bank: grossed up and then credited, as explained in [eligible vs ordinary dividends](/investing/dividends/eligible-vs-ordinary-dividends/).

## Return of capital and your adjusted cost base

ROC is often misunderstood. It isn't "free" income and it isn't automatically bad. It's a deferral: you pay less tax now and more capital gains tax when you sell.

Key points:

- **Track ACB yourself** in a taxable account. Brokers' book values aren't always adjusted for ROC; the REIT's annual tax-allocation release gives you the numbers.
- If cumulative ROC pushes ACB **below zero**, the negative amount is generally treated as a capital gain in that year.
- ROC in the *tax* sense (from the T3) isn't the same as "paying out more than it earns" in the *business* sense. Check the AFFO payout for that.

## Judging safety: AFFO payout, not earnings

| Metric | Corporate dividend payer | REIT |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Primary payout metric | Earnings or FCF payout — see [payout ratio explained](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/) | AFFO payout — see [valuing REITs](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/) |
| Cash check | [FCF coverage](/investing/dividends/fcf-coverage-of-dividends/) | AFFO after maintenance capex and leasing costs |
| Balance-sheet check | Debt-to-EBITDA, maturities | Debt-to-gross-book-value, debt-to-EBITDA, maturity ladder |
| Why IFRS net income fails | One-offs | Fair-value swings on properties |

REITs pay out a large share of cash by design, so their payout ratios naturally run higher than an operating company's. That's normal — but it leaves less cushion when refinancing costs rise or occupancy dips. Our [REIT sector deep dive](/sectors/real-estate-reits/) compares payout and leverage across TSX property types.

## Which account suits which?

| Account | REIT distributions | Eligible Canadian dividends |
|:--|:--|:--|
| TFSA | Tax character irrelevant; no ACB tracking for tax | Tax character irrelevant |
| RRSP | Tax character irrelevant until withdrawal (then all taxed as income) | Dividend tax credit is lost inside the RRSP |
| Taxable | Other income taxed fully; ROC requires ACB tracking | Gross-up and credit usually make these tax-efficient |

That's why many DIY investors prefer to hold REITs inside registered accounts and keep eligible Canadian dividend payers in taxable accounts when they have to choose — a whole-portfolio decision that also depends on your [TFSA strategy](/investing/accounts/tfsa-for-stock-investors/) and any US holdings ([US withholding tax](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/)).

## Common mistakes

- Comparing a REIT's distribution yield with a bank's dividend yield without adjusting for tax character in a taxable account.
- Forgetting to reduce ACB for return of capital.
- Judging REIT safety on IFRS earnings or FFO instead of AFFO.
- Assuming monthly payments mean more stable income.
