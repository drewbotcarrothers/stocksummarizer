Canadian telecoms have long been core holdings for dividend investors: big subscriber bases, recurring monthly bills and a market with few national players. But the business is also capital-hungry. Networks need constant upgrading, spectrum costs billions, and price competition can erode revenue per user. In 2025, BCE cut its dividend by more than half, a reminder that a long record isn't a guarantee.

This post is a framework for judging whether a telecom's dividend is sustainable. For how telecoms make money and the operating metrics (ARPU, churn, capex intensity), see [how telecoms make money](/investing/sectors/how-telecoms-make-money/) and [how to analyze telecoms](/investing/analyze/analyze-telecoms-arpu-churn-capex/). It's educational; figures are hypothetical unless noted.

## Direct answer

Judge a telecom dividend on five tests. **Coverage**: is the dividend paid from free cash flow after capex *and* lease payments, not only from "adjusted" cash measures? **Leverage**: is net debt to EBITDA stable or falling, or is debt rising to fund the payout? **Capex**: are network builds, fibre rollouts or spectrum purchases about to absorb more cash? **Competition**: are pricing and churn stable? **Funding**: is any gap filled by discounted DRIP shares, asset sales or new debt? A dividend that passes only with help from DRIP issuance and asset sales is under pressure, even if it hasn't been cut.

| Test | Healthier reading | Warning signs |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Coverage | Dividend below FCF after leases, with a cushion | Payout near or above 100% of FCF for several years |
| Leverage | Net debt / EBITDA steady or falling toward management's target | Leverage rising while the dividend grows |
| Capex | Peak build behind; guidance shows capex easing | New fibre or 5G build-out, or a spectrum auction ahead |
| Competition | Stable pricing, low churn, ARPU flat to rising | Price wars, rising churn, promotional intensity |
| Funding | Dividend paid in cash from FCF | Discounted DRIP issuing many new shares; asset sales to fund the payout |

## Spectrum and "lumpy" costs

Spectrum licences are bought at government auctions and can cost billions at once. Companies often exclude spectrum from their free cash flow definitions because it's irregular. But it's a recurring need over time, so a fair coverage test should average spectrum spending over several years.

| Approach | FCF definition | Effect on payout ratio |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Company "adjusted" FCF | Excludes spectrum, sometimes leases | Lowest payout ratio |
| FCF after leases | Includes lease principal | Higher |
| FCF after leases and averaged spectrum | Adds a multi-year spectrum average | Most conservative |

## Discounted DRIPs: a hidden cost

Some Canadian telecoms have offered DRIPs that issue new shares at a discount to the market price. That keeps cash in the company, but it increases the share count, so per-share growth is diluted. If a large share of the dividend is effectively paid in new stock, the cash payout ratio understates what the dividend really costs existing holders; see [how dilution changes per-share valuation math](/investing/valuation/dilution-per-share-valuation-math/).

## A real reminder: BCE in 2025

On May 8, 2025, BCE cut its quarterly common dividend from C$0.9975 to C$0.4375 a share (C$3.99 to C$1.75 annualized), about 56%. At the same time it set a new payout policy of 40% to 55% of free cash flow, ended discounted treasury issuance under its DRIP, and said it would disclose free cash flow both before and after lease payments.

| BCE, May 2025 | Before | After |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Annualized dividend per share | C$3.99 | C$1.75 |
| Payout policy | Based on FCF; payout had run above FCF | 40%–55% of FCF |
| DRIP | Discounted treasury shares | Discount ended |
| FCF disclosure | FCF | FCF and FCF after lease payments |

Before the cut, the signals in this framework were visible in public filings: dividends above free cash flow, heavy debt and capex, and a discounted DRIP. The point isn't hindsight; the same tests apply to every telecom.
