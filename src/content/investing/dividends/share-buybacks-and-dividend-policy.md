Companies return cash to shareholders in two main ways: dividends and share buybacks. They're often discussed separately, but they're two dials on the same machine. How a company sets one tells you a lot about the other, and about how much of its dividend growth is coming from a shrinking share count rather than a growing business.

This post explains how buybacks interact with dividend policy. It is educational; figures are hypothetical. For buyback value math and disclosures, see [how buybacks change per-share valuation math](/investing/valuation/buybacks-per-share-valuation-math/) and [read share buyback disclosures in Canada](/investing/analyze/read-share-buyback-disclosures-canada/).

## Direct answer

Think of **total shareholder payout = dividends + net buybacks**. Boards treat dividends as **sticky** (cutting them is costly to reputation), so they usually set the dividend at a level they can sustain and use **buybacks as the flexible layer**, larger in strong years, paused in weak ones. Buybacks also **raise dividends per share** even when total dividend cash is flat, because fewer shares split the same pool. When you assess dividend growth, separate growth from the **business** from growth from the **shrinking share count**, and check that buybacks aren't being funded with debt at the dividend's expense.

## Buybacks lift dividends per share

A hypothetical company has 200 million shares, pays C$500M in dividends (C$2.50 per share) and buys back 3% of its shares each year.

|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Shares outstanding (millions) | 200.0 | 194.0 | 188.2 | 182.5 | 177.1 |
| Total dividend cash (C$M) | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 |

The dividend per share grows about 3% a year while the company pays no more cash in total. That's real value for continuing shareholders, but it isn't the same as a business growing its cash flow. If you're projecting dividend growth, split it:

| Source of dividend-per-share growth | Hypothetical |
|:--|--:|
| Growth in total dividend cash (from business growth) | 4% |
| Share count reduction from buybacks | 3% |
| **Approximate dividend-per-share growth** | **~7%** |

If buybacks stop, dividend-per-share growth drops to the business rate.

## Total shareholder yield

Dividend yield alone understates what some companies return. Add the net buyback yield.

| Hypothetical, C$10B market cap | Company A | Company B |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Dividends | C$400M | C$200M |
| Gross buybacks | C$0 | C$500M |
| Shares issued (options, DRIP, acquisitions) | C$0 | C$200M |
| Dividend yield | 4.0% | 2.0% |
| Net buyback yield | 0.0% | 3.0% |
| **Total shareholder yield** | **4.0%** | **5.0%** |

Use **net** buybacks. A company that buys back shares while issuing as many through stock compensation or a treasury DRIP isn't shrinking its share count. See [what dilution is](/investing/glossary/what-is-dilution/).

## When buybacks threaten the dividend

| Warning sign | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|
| Buybacks funded with new debt | Leverage rises; in a downturn, both payouts compete for less cash |
| Total payout above free cash flow for years | Not sustainable without asset sales or borrowing |
| Buybacks heavy at high prices, paused at low prices | Destroys value; less cash left for dividends later |
| Payout ratio rising while buybacks continue | The dividend's cushion is shrinking |

Healthy companies generally fund dividends and buybacks from free cash flow and suspend buybacks first when cash tightens. That order is a feature: it means buybacks act as a shock absorber for the dividend. The [stress test](/investing/dividends/stress-test-dividend-safety-recession/) post shows this lever in a worked example.

## Reading the policy in filings

| What to find | Where |
|:--|:--|
| Stated capital allocation priorities | Annual report, investor day deck |
| NCIB size, approved maximum and purchases to date | NCIB news release and quarterly MD&A |
| Total dividends and buybacks vs free cash flow | Cash flow statement, financing activities |
| Share count trend, basic and diluted | Notes to the financial statements |

## Common mistakes

- Comparing dividend yields without adding net buybacks.
- Attributing dividend-per-share growth to the business when it's mostly share count reduction.
- Ignoring share issuance that offsets buybacks.
- Praising buybacks regardless of price paid.
