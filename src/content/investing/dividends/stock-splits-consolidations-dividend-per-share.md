A stock split or consolidation changes how many shares exist and what each one costs. It doesn't change the business, and on its own it doesn't change the dividend you receive in total. But it does change **dividend per share (DPS)**, and that trips up charts, screeners and spreadsheets that don't adjust for it.

This post explains how splits and consolidations affect DPS, yield and dividend histories. It's educational; figures are hypothetical unless noted.

## Direct answer

In a **split** (say 2-for-1), each share becomes two and the dividend per share is divided by the same ratio. In a **consolidation** or reverse split (say 1-for-4), four shares become one and DPS is multiplied by four. Your **total dividend income and yield are unchanged by the event itself**. What changes is the per-share history: a chart or spreadsheet that isn't **split-adjusted** will show a fake cut after a split or a fake jump after a consolidation. Watch for boards that pair a split or consolidation with a real dividend change, and always compare DPS on an adjusted basis.

## What changes and what doesn't

| Hypothetical: 1,000 shares at $80, $2.40 annual DPS | Before | After 2-for-1 split | After 1-for-4 consolidation |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Shares held | 1,000 | 2,000 | 250 |
| Share price | $80.00 | $40.00 | $320.00 |
| Annual DPS | $2.40 | $1.20 | $9.60 |
| Your annual dividend income | $2,400 | $2,400 | $2,400 |
| Dividend yield | 3.0% | 3.0% | 3.0% |
| Value of your position | $80,000 | $80,000 | $80,000 |

## The fake-cut problem in dividend histories

Canadian Pacific's 5-for-1 split in 2021 is a real example of the kind of event that needs adjusting: on an unadjusted basis, quarterly DPS after the split was about one-fifth of the pre-split figure. Nothing about the dividend got worse; the per-share unit changed.

| Where it goes wrong | Symptom | Fix |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Your own spreadsheet | DPS growth rate shows a large negative year | Divide pre-split DPS by the split ratio |
| Older annual reports | Five-year tables use pre-split figures | Check the footnote; later reports often restate |
| Free charting sites | Mixed adjusted and unadjusted series | Confirm the data provider's methodology |
| Payout ratio from mixed data | Pre-split EPS vs post-split DPS | Use EPS and DPS on the same share basis |

## Consolidations: a different signal

Splits usually follow a rising share price. Consolidations more often follow a falling one, when a company wants to lift its per-share price for listing requirements or investor perception. A consolidation by itself doesn't hurt dividend holders, but it often happens alongside a recapitalization, a dividend reset or new share issuance. Read the circular and news release to see what else is changing.

| Event | Common reasons | What to check for dividends |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Forward split | Price has risen; improve trading liquidity | Whether the board raised DPS at the same time |
| Consolidation | Price has fallen; listing standards; reduce share count optics | Whether a dividend cut, restructuring or equity raise is bundled |
| Stock dividend | Paying shares instead of cash | Whether it's effectively a split, and its tax treatment |

## When a split arrives with a raise

Boards sometimes announce a split and a dividend increase together. To measure the raise, put both figures on the same share basis:

| Hypothetical | Per old share | Per new share (3-for-1) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Old quarterly DPS | $0.90 | $0.30 |
| New quarterly DPS | — | $0.33 |
| Equivalent per old share | $0.99 | — |
| Actual raise | 10% | 10% |

## Tax and record-keeping notes

A straightforward split or consolidation isn't a taxable event in Canada; your total adjusted cost base stays the same and is spread across the new number of shares. Update your ACB per share in your records; see [adjusted cost base tracking for DIY portfolios](/investing/accounts/adjusted-cost-base-tracking-diy-portfolios/). Consolidations can leave fractional shares that are paid out in cash, which may create a small disposition.
