Dividends are paid in cash, not earnings. A company can report solid net income and still struggle to fund its dividend if capital spending eats the cash, working capital ties it up, or the "earnings" are mostly non-cash. That's why **free-cash-flow (FCF) coverage** is the most useful single safety check for a dividend stock.

Educational only — not financial advice. All figures are **hypothetical**.

## Direct answer

**FCF dividend coverage = free cash flow ÷ dividends paid**, where FCF = cash from operations − capital expenditures (and, under IFRS 16, minus lease principal payments). Above 1.0× means the dividend was funded from internally generated cash; below 1.0× means debt, asset sales or new shares filled the gap. Average it over **three to five years** to smooth timing, and use the sector's own version where FCF doesn't fit (DCF for pipelines, AFFO for REITs).

## Why FCF beats earnings-based payout

| Issue | Earnings payout ratio | FCF coverage |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Heavy capex needs | Hidden — depreciation lags spending | Captured directly |
| Working-capital swings | Ignored | Captured (smooth by averaging) |
| Non-cash gains/losses | Distort the ratio | Excluded |
| Dividends paid in shares (DRIP) | Not visible | Visible: compare cash dividends vs declared |

Earnings-based payout ratios are still useful, especially for banks where FCF isn't meaningful — see [payout ratio explained](/investing/dividends/payout-ratio-explained-canadian-companies/).

| Coverage (multi-year average) | Typical reading |
|:--|:--|
| Above 1.5× | Comfortable; room for growth or buybacks |
| 1.1×–1.5× | Covered; watch capex plans and leverage |
| 0.9×–1.1× | Tight; any downturn means borrowing |
| Below 0.9× | Funded externally; needs a credible plan |

*These are rough reading bands, not rules — a regulated utility can run tighter than a miner.*

## Sector versions of coverage

Some TSX sectors use different cash measures because of how they spend:

| Sector | Measure | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Pipelines / midstream | Distributable cash flow (DCF) per share vs dividend | Separates maintenance from growth capex; see [midstream](/sectors/midstream-pipelines/) |
| Utilities | Earnings payout + FFO-to-debt | Growth capex is often funded with debt and equity by design; see [utilities](/sectors/utilities/) |
| REITs | AFFO payout ratio | See [REIT valuation](/investing/valuation/valuing-reits-nav-ffo-affo/) |
| Banks, insurers | Earnings payout + capital ratios | FCF isn't meaningful for lenders; see [banks](/sectors/banks/) |
| Producers / miners | FCF at mid-cycle prices; base vs variable dividend | Commodity swings; see [oil & gas producers](/sectors/oil-gas-producers/) |

Be careful with "growth capex" exclusions: if a company always calls most of its spending "growth," a DCF-based coverage ratio may flatter.

## Red flags

1. Coverage below 1.0× for two or more years with no stated plan.
2. Rising net debt every year while the dividend grows.
3. A large share of the dividend paid in shares via a DRIP discount (cash coverage looks better than the real dilution).
4. FCF propped up by cutting capex below depreciation for several years.
5. Management redefining the coverage metric when it gets worse.

These overlap with the [dividend trap checklist](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/).

## A 15-minute coverage check

1. Download five years of cash-flow statements from SEDAR+ ([how-to](/investing/analyze/use-sedar-plus-for-stock-research/)).
2. Compute FCF and cash dividends paid for each year.
3. Compute coverage per year and the five-year average.
4. Compare with net debt over the same period.
5. Record the result in the dividend line of your [stock brief](/investing/analyze/stock-brief-template-bull-bear-watch/).
