Dividend capture sounds like a free lunch: buy a stock just before it goes ex-dividend, collect the dividend, then sell. Repeat across many stocks and you'd earn dividends without holding anything for long. In practice, the share price adjusts for the dividend, trading costs and taxes take a slice, and the risk you carry for a few days isn't paid for.

This post explains how dividend capture works and why most DIY investors should skip it. It is educational; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

On the **ex-dividend date**, a stock typically opens lower by **roughly the dividend amount**, because new buyers no longer get that payment. So capturing the dividend usually means **taking an offsetting price drop**. After **bid-ask spreads, commissions, FX costs** on US stocks and **tax friction** (dividends and capital losses aren't taxed symmetrically in a taxable account), the expected result of a simple capture strategy is close to zero or negative, with extra short-term price risk. Holding good dividend payers for the long term and ignoring the ex-date is simpler and cheaper.

| Date | What it means |
|:--|:--|
| Declaration date | Board announces the dividend amount, record date and payment date |
| Ex-dividend date | First day the stock trades without the right to the upcoming dividend |
| Record date | Shareholders on the company's books get the dividend; same day as the ex-date under T+1 settlement |
| Payment date | Cash arrives, often weeks later |

Canada moved to T+1 settlement in May 2024, which put the ex-dividend date and the record date on the same business day. To receive the dividend you must buy **before** the ex-date, meaning by the close of the prior trading day.

## Why the math rarely works

A hypothetical trade: buy 1,000 shares at C$50.00 the day before the ex-date, collect a C$0.50 dividend, sell at C$49.50 on the ex-date.

| Hypothetical | Amount |
|:--|--:|
| Dividend received (1,000 × $0.50) | +$500 |
| Price drop on the ex-date (1,000 × $0.50) | −$500 |
| Bid-ask spread, buy and sell (1,000 × $0.02 × 2) | −$40 |
| Commissions ($10 each way) | −$20 |
| **Net before tax** | **−$60** |

The price doesn't always drop by exactly the dividend; on any given day it can fall more or less as markets move. That randomness is risk, not a reliable edge. Academic research on ex-dividend days has long found the average drop is close to, though often slightly less than, the dividend, and any gap is generally too small to cover an individual investor's costs and taxes.

## The tax mismatch in a taxable account

| Item | Treatment (taxable account) |
|:--|:--|
| Eligible dividend | Grossed up and taxed, with the dividend tax credit; see [how the dividend tax credit works](/investing/dividends/dividend-tax-credit-after-tax-yield/) |
| Capital loss on the price drop | Only partly deductible, and only against capital gains |
| US dividend | 15% withholding typically applies (with a W-8BEN on file); foreign tax credit rules then apply |
| Frequent trading | CRA can treat a pattern of short-term trading as business income rather than capital gains |

The dividend is taxed as income while the matching price drop becomes a capital loss you may not be able to use right away, so even a break-even trade before tax can lose money after tax. Holding for only a day or two can also bump into the [superficial loss rule](/investing/accounts/superficial-loss-rule-stock-swaps/) if you rebuy the same stock within 30 days.

## What about doing it in a TFSA?

Taxes on gains and dividends disappear inside a TFSA, but the price drop, spreads and commissions don't. US dividends in a TFSA still lose the 15% withholding with no way to recover it. And CRA has said that carrying on a trading business inside a TFSA can make the income taxable. A capture strategy is exactly the kind of high-frequency pattern that invites that question.

## Capture vs simply holding

| | Dividend capture | Buy and hold dividend payers |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Trades per year | Dozens or hundreds | A few |
| Costs | Spreads and commissions on every round trip | Minimal |
| Tax | Mismatch between income and capital losses; possible business income | Dividends and long-term gains, often in favourable accounts |
| Risk | Short-term price moves on every trade | Company and market risk over time |
| Time required | Heavy | Light |
| Expected edge | Close to zero before costs | Long-term business returns |

## Common mistakes

- Assuming the stock will recover the dividend amount within days.
- Ignoring spreads on thinly traded TSX names, which can be wider than the dividend.
- Forgetting T+1: buying on the ex-date means you don't get the dividend.
- Running capture in a TFSA without thinking about the business-income question.
