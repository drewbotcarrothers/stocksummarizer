How do you know whether your dividend investing is working? One investor looks at the dividends deposited this year and sees success. Another compares the portfolio's total return with an index fund and sees disappointment. Both are keeping score, but they're answering different questions.

This post explains the two scoreboards, when each one misleads, and how to keep both. It's educational, not advice; the examples are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

**Income** answers "how much cash does this produce, and is that cash growing?" **Total return** (price change plus dividends) answers "was owning this better than the alternatives?" An income-only scoreboard can hide a falling share price; a total-return-only scoreboard can ignore that you actually need cash flow. Track **annual income and its growth rate** alongside **total return compared with a total-return benchmark**, and review both once a year.

## Same money, two stories

Compare two hypothetical stocks over 10 years, each bought for C$100, dividends not reinvested.

- **Stock A** yields 6% with no dividend growth; its price drifts down 1% a year.
- **Stock B** yields 2% and grows its dividend 8% a year; its price rises 7% a year.

Stock A produced twice the income. Stock B produced 50% more wealth, and by year 11 its dividend on the original C$100 would be about C$4.32, still below A's C$6.00, but on a holding now worth about twice as much. Neither outcome is guaranteed; the example shows why one scoreboard isn't enough. ## Pick the right benchmark

Index providers publish both a **price index** and a **total return index**. The S&P/TSX Composite price index excludes dividends, so comparing your dividend-inclusive returns against it flatters you. Use the total return version, or a broad Canadian equity ETF's total return. [What the S&P/TSX Composite measures](/investing/indexes-etfs/what-sp-tsx-composite-measures/) explains the difference.

| Comparison | Fair? |
|:--|:--|
| Your total return vs S&P/TSX Composite total return | Yes |
| Your total return vs S&P/TSX Composite price index | No: flatters you by the index's dividend yield each year |
| Your dividend income vs last year's income | Yes, for the income scoreboard |
| Your portfolio yield vs a high-yield ETF's distribution yield | Often no; see [ETF distribution yield vs stock dividend yield](/investing/dividends/etf-distribution-yield-vs-stock-dividend-yield/) |

"Income growth, same holdings" strips out new money, so you see whether the dividends themselves grew. If income grows while total return trails the benchmark for years, you may be paying for income with capital. If total return is strong but income is volatile and you need the cash, an [income sleeve](/investing/dividends/income-sleeve-without-abandoning-total-return/) may fit better.

## Common mistakes

- Celebrating income growth that came entirely from adding new money.
- Measuring total return against a price-only index.
- Ignoring the year a holding cut its dividend because "income is still up overall".
- Judging a strategy on fewer than five years of results.
