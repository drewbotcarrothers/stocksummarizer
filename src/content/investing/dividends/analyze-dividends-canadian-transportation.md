Canadian transportation dividends come from more than the two big railways. The TSX also lists truckload and less-than-truckload carriers, logistics and courier operators, an overnight air cargo airline, and diversified aviation-services companies. Their dividends are usually smaller than their stock buybacks, and the cash behind them depends on fleet spending, freight rates and fuel.

Educational only; worked numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named. The railways have their own guides, which this post doesn't repeat: [how to analyze dividends in Canadian rails](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-rails/) and [common dividend mistakes when buying Canadian rails](/investing/dividends/common-dividend-mistakes-canadian-rails/).

## Direct answer

Analyze a non-rail transportation dividend in five steps: measure **free cash flow after fleet capex** (trucks, trailers, aircraft) and after **lease payments**; read the **operating ratio** and how fast it moves when freight volumes fall; check **leverage against covenants and targets**, especially after acquisitions; note the **dividend currency and frequency** (TFI declares in US dollars; Mullen pays monthly); and stress the dividend through a **freight recession**. TFI International raised its quarterly dividend 4% to US$0.47 in 2026 while keeping funded debt near 2.1x EBITDA, according to its Q3 results.

|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Operating cash flow | 9% | 18% | 30% |

| Lease principal payments | −2% | −2% | −3% |
| Free cash flow | 6% | 6% | 7% |

Similar free cash flow margins can hide very different risks. The air cargo operator needs a large, lumpy aircraft budget; the logistics broker needs almost none but has thinner margins. Under IFRS 16, lease payments sit in financing cash flow, so subtract them yourself before calling cash "free."

## Step 2: the operating ratio

Truckers, like railways, quote an operating ratio (operating expenses ÷ revenue). Lower is better. What matters for dividends is how much the ratio rises when volume drops.

| Hypothetical carrier | Normal year | Freight slowdown |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Revenue (C$M) | 2,000 | 1,800 |
| Operating ratio | 88% | 93% |
| Operating income (C$M) | 240 | 126 |
| Change in operating income | — | −48% |

A 10% revenue drop roughly halved operating income in this example, because drivers, terminals and equipment don't shrink as fast as freight.

## Step 3: leverage and acquisitions

TSX transport companies grow by buying other carriers and logistics firms. Check:

| Check | Where to find it |
|:--|:--|
| Net debt ÷ EBITDA (or the covenant measure) vs the limit | MD&A liquidity section, debt notes |
| Fixed vs floating rate debt | Debt notes |
| Acquisition pipeline and stated priorities | Investor presentation |
| Sale-leaseback proceeds inside cash flow | Investing and financing sections |

Mullen Group, for example, reported net debt of 2.16 times operating cash flow under its private placement definition at September 30, 2026, against a 3.5 times covenant. Covenant headroom is what lets a board keep a dividend through a soft year.

## Step 4: currency and frequency

| Company (example) | Dividend style | What it means for you |
|:--|:--|:--|
| TFI International | Quarterly, declared in US$ (US$0.47) | C$ income moves with the exchange rate |
| Mullen Group | Monthly, C$0.07 (C$0.84 a year) | Smooth cash flow; frequency says nothing about safety |
| Cargojet | Quarterly, C$1.40 trailing annual | Small yield; buybacks and fleet decisions matter more |
| Air Canada | No dividend | Airlines' capital needs usually crowd out dividends |

See [monthly vs quarterly dividends](/investing/dividends/monthly-vs-quarterly-dividends/) for why frequency is a cash-flow preference, not a quality signal.

## Step 5: the freight-recession test

Rebuild a down year: volumes down 10–15%, rates down, fuel surcharges falling with diesel, and used-equipment prices weaker (which reduces gains on sale). Then compare free cash flow with the dividend.

| Hypothetical freight recession, C$M | Normal | Recession |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Operating cash flow | 360 | 250 |
| Net capex | −200 | −120 (fleet purchases deferred) |
| Lease principal | −40 | −40 |
| Free cash flow | 120 | 90 |
| Dividend | −45 | −45 |
| Cover | 2.7x | 2.0x |

Carriers can defer new trucks in a downturn, which protects cash for a year or two but ages the fleet. If cover only holds because capex was cut, note that it will need to be caught up later.

## A transportation dividend checklist

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| FCF after capex and lease payments covers the dividend | Over a full cycle, not just this year |
| Operating ratio sensitivity | Understood from past slowdowns |
| Leverage vs covenant or target | Comfortable headroom |
| Dividend currency | Known (US$ or C$) |
| Sale-leaseback or equipment-sale gains | Excluded from recurring cash |
| Dividend vs buybacks | Dividend is the smaller, protected part |
