Utilities are among the most dependable dividend payers on the TSX, but their dividends are funded in a way that surprises many investors: most utilities spend more on new assets than they generate in operating cash, so part of the growth is financed with new debt and equity. The dividend is sustainable when the regulatory model, the balance sheet and the funding plan all line up.

This post explains how regulated utilities fund and sustain dividends and what to check. It is educational; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

A regulated utility earns an **allowed return on equity** on the equity-financed portion of its **rate base** (the assets regulators approve). Earnings therefore grow roughly with the rate base, and the dividend is paid from those earnings, typically at a **higher payout ratio** than most industrial companies. Because capital spending usually exceeds operating cash flow after dividends, utilities close a **funding gap** with debt, hybrids, asset sales and new equity (including DRIP shares). The dividend is safe when **rate-base growth, allowed returns and credit metrics** support it without heavy, dilutive equity issuance.

Change any input and earnings move. A regulator that trims the allowed ROE by half a percentage point or the equity thickness by a few points cuts earnings directly, which is why rate case decisions matter so much to utility investors. ## Rate base growth drives dividend growth

If the utility's capital plan grows rate base 6% a year and the regulatory settings hold, earnings grow about 6% too. A dividend growing slightly slower than earnings lets the payout ratio ease over time.

Some utilities publish multi-year dividend growth guidance and tie it explicitly to rate base growth. When you see guidance like that, check that the capital plan in the investor presentation actually supports it.

## The funding gap

Here is the part that headlines skip. Growing the rate base costs money up front.

| Hypothetical, one year | C$B |
|:--|--:|
| Operating cash flow | 2.40 |
| Capital expenditures | (3.50) |
| Dividends paid in cash | (0.75) |
| **Funding gap** | **(1.85)** |
| New long-term debt | 1.20 |
| Hybrid or preferred securities | 0.30 |
| New common equity (DRIP and share issuance) | 0.35 |

A funding gap is normal for a growing utility. The question is how it's filled. Debt has to stay within what credit rating agencies tolerate, often judged with metrics such as funds from operations (FFO) to debt. Equity issuance dilutes existing holders, so heavy reliance on new shares can mean earnings per share grow more slowly than total earnings. A DRIP that issues shares from treasury is one form of equity funding; it's quieter than a public offering but still adds shares. See [how dilution affects per-share value](/investing/glossary/what-is-dilution/).

## What can strain a utility dividend

| Risk | How it hits | Where to look |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Lower allowed ROE or equity thickness | Earnings fall at the same rate base | Rate case decisions, regulator releases |
| Regulatory lag | Costs rise before rates are reset | MD&A discussion of rate filings |
| Higher interest rates | Higher financing costs and pressure on the share price | Debt maturity schedule; [how rates change justified multiples](/investing/valuation/interest-rates-change-justified-multiples/) |
| Capex overruns | Bigger funding gap | Project updates in quarterly reports |
| Credit downgrade risk | Costlier debt, pressure to issue equity | Rating agency reports, FFO/debt targets in the MD&A |
| Unregulated or merchant exposure | Earnings less predictable | Segment reporting |
| Wildfire or storm liabilities | Large unplanned costs | Risk factors, contingencies note |

## A utility dividend checklist

| Check | Healthier sign |
|:--|:--|
| Share of earnings from regulated operations | Large and stable |
| Rate base growth in the capital plan | Supports the dividend growth guidance |
| Payout ratio of EPS | Stable or easing, within management's target |
| FFO-to-debt or similar credit metric | Above the level management says it targets |
| Equity issuance | Modest and planned, not emergency |
| Recent rate case outcomes | Allowed ROE and equity thickness steady |

## Common mistakes

- Judging a utility dividend with FCF after all capex. Growth capex makes FCF negative by design; look at the funding plan instead.
- Ignoring dilution from DRIPs and share issuance when projecting dividends per share.
- Treating all utilities alike. Regulated wires and pipes behave differently from merchant power generation.
- Assuming rate cases are a formality.
