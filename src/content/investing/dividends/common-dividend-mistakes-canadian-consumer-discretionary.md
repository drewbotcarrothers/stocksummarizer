Discretionary dividends fail in a particular way: they look covered right up until consumers stop spending, and then they can go from comfortable to unaffordable in a quarter. Most of the mistakes come from judging them in good times.

This list goes with [how to analyze dividends in Canadian consumer discretionary stocks](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-consumer-discretionary/), which covers operating leverage, recession-year cash flow and credit arms. Educational only; numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named.

## Direct answer

The common mistakes are treating a **moderate payout ratio as proof of safety**, setting expectations from a **boom year**, ignoring **operating leverage**, forgetting **lease payments**, missing a **credit card arm's loan losses**, chasing **high yields at mature chains** with shrinking traffic, and ignoring **deal and refinancing risk**. Cineplex is the cautionary example: it reported a dividend payout of 68.7% of adjusted free cash flow for the 12 months to March 2020, and its dividend stopped that same spring when theatres closed.

Cineplex's monthly dividend was C$0.15 a share in 2019. Its payout of adjusted free cash flow was around two-thirds, which looked reasonable. When theatres closed in March 2020, revenue collapsed and the company continued its suspension of dividends through 2020. The lesson isn't that Cineplex was badly run. It's that a discretionary payout ratio only means something next to a stress case.

Pandemic-era spending on home goods, recreational vehicles and renovation pushed some companies to record free cash flow in 2020–2021. Dividends set from that base can look stretched when demand normalizes.

## Mistake 3: ignoring operating leverage

Profit falls faster than sales when costs are fixed.

|:--|--:|--:|
| Operating income change (35% contribution margin, 8% operating margin) | −22% | −44% |
| Dividend payout of operating income, from 40% | 51% | 71% |

## The full mistakes table

| # | Mistake | Better habit |
|--:|:--|:--|
| 1 | Trusting a moderate payout ratio | Test the payout in a stress year |
| 2 | Using a boom year as the base | Use a normal year's free cash flow |
| 3 | Ignoring operating leverage | Measure profit change vs sales change |
| 4 | Forgetting lease payments | Calculate FCF after lease principal |
| 5 | Missing credit card loan losses | Read the financial services segment |
| 6 | Chasing yield at mature chains | Check traffic and same-store sales trends |
| 7 | Ignoring deal and refinancing risk | Read debt maturities and deal terms |
| 8 | Assuming the brand protects cash flow | Check what happened in the last downturn |
| 9 | Overlooking US dollar inventory costs | Check how much product is bought in US dollars |
| 10 | Counting buybacks as income | Buybacks are cut first |

## Mistake 4: forgetting the rent

Retailers and restaurants lease most of their locations. Under IFRS 16 the lease principal is in financing cash flow, so free cash flow before leases can overstate what's available for dividends by a large amount. Use the after-lease number.

## Mistake 5: credit losses inside a retailer

A retailer with its own credit card business has two cycles: sales and credit. In a recession, credit losses rise as sales fall. Check the allowance for credit losses and the segment's share of operating income.

| Hypothetical card book | Normal year | Recession year |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Receivables (C$M) | 7,000 | 7,000 |
| Net write-off rate | 5% | 8% |
| Credit losses (C$M) | 350 | 560 |

## Mistake 6: high yields at mature chains

A high yield at a retailer can reflect a market that expects falling traffic. Check same-store sales over several years, store closures and the share of sales moving online before treating the yield as income. See [how to avoid dividend traps on the TSX](/investing/dividends/avoid-dividend-traps-tsx/).

## Mistake 7: deal and refinancing risk

Cineplex had agreed to be acquired by Cineworld in December 2019 and said it did not expect to pay further dividends after February 2020 if the deal closed. Pending deals, refinancing deadlines and covenants can all affect a dividend independently of trading. Read the debt maturity table and any merger agreement terms.

## A discretionary warning-sign list

| Sign | What to check next |
|:--|:--|
| Same-store sales negative for several quarters | Traffic vs price |
| Dividend set during a record year | Normal-year free cash flow |
| FCF after leases below dividend | Debt and liquidity |
| Credit losses rising | Card book quality |
| Major debt maturity within 18 months | Refinancing options |
