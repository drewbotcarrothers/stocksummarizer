A dividend that never gets cut can still lose purchasing power. If a company pays the same $1.00 a share for a decade while prices rise 2% a year, that dollar buys about 18% less at the end. "Stable" and "unchanged" are not the same thing once you adjust for inflation.

This post shows how to convert a dividend into real (inflation-adjusted) terms and what that means for growth targets. It's educational; inflation rates and figures are hypothetical illustrations, not forecasts.

## Direct answer

**Real dividend growth ≈ nominal DPS growth − inflation.** A frozen dividend shrinks in purchasing power every year prices rise. To hold purchasing power, DPS needs to grow at least in line with inflation; to grow in real terms, it needs to grow faster. Over 10 years at **2% inflation**, a frozen dividend is worth about **82 cents** in today's money; at **3%**, about **74 cents**. When you judge a "stable" payer, ask whether the raise history has kept up with prices, not only whether the cash amount was maintained.

| After 10 years | 2% inflation | 3% inflation |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Frozen $1.00 dividend, real | $0.82 | $0.74 |
| Purchasing power lost | 18% | 26% |
| Raise needed to hold purchasing power | 2% a year | 3% a year |
| Raise needed to grow 3% in real terms | ~5% a year | ~6% a year |

The 10-year factor is **1 / (1 + inflation)^10**. At 2%: 1 / 1.02^10 ≈ 0.82. At 3%: 1 / 1.03^10 ≈ 0.74.

## A simple real-income worksheet

Take a hypothetical holding of 1,000 shares, starting DPS of C$2.40 (C$2,400 a year), and 2% inflation:

| Year | Nominal income if frozen | Real income (year-0 dollars) | Nominal income if raised 5% | Real income if raised 5% |
|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 0 | $2,400 | $2,400 | $2,400 | $2,400 |
| 5 | $2,400 | $2,174 | $3,063 | $2,774 |
| 10 | $2,400 | $1,969 | $3,909 | $3,207 |

## What this means for screens and scorecards

| Habit | Problem | Better question |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Screening for "no cuts in 10 years" | Includes frozen and token-raise payers | Did DPS grow at least with inflation? |
| Treating yield as a constant | A 4% yield on a frozen payer is shrinking in real terms | What's the real income path if raises stay at the historical rate? |
| Using a dividend discount model with no inflation | The discount rate already includes inflation, but the growth rate must too | Is g a nominal rate that can exceed inflation? |

For the compounding math behind different raise rates, see [dividend growth rate math over 10 years](/investing/dividends/dividend-growth-rate-math-10-years/).

## Inflation, rates and dividend payers

Higher inflation often comes with higher interest rates, which can pressure share prices of utilities, telecoms and other rate-sensitive payers even if their dividends keep rising. That's a valuation issue (see [how interest rates change what multiples can justify](/investing/valuation/interest-rates-change-justified-multiples/)), separate from whether the cash payment itself is keeping up with prices. A company that can raise prices with inflation is better placed to grow dividends in real terms than one whose rates are set for years ahead.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a 10-year freeze "stable income."
- Comparing a 6% grower in a 2% inflation decade with a 6% grower in a 6% inflation decade as if they were equal.
- Forgetting that your spending (groceries, housing, travel) is what the dividend needs to keep up with, not a single official index in isolation.
