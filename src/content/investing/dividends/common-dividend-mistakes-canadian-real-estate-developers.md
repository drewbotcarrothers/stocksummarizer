A developer's dividend can look tiny in a record year and enormous in a quiet one, without anything changing in the business. Most mistakes come from reading one year in isolation or treating a developer like a REIT.

This list goes with [how to analyze dividends in Canadian real estate developers](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-real-estate-developers/), which covers recurring vs development income, presales and construction debt. Educational only; numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named.

## Direct answer

The common mistakes are judging payout from a **record closing year**, treating a developer like a **REIT**, ignoring the **presale pipeline** that sets future closings, missing **joint venture guarantees**, assuming **land always holds value**, raising expectations after a **one-off land sale**, and ignoring **interest rates** on both buyers and construction loans. Urbanation reported that new condo sales in the Greater Toronto Hamilton Area fell 60% in 2025 to 1,599 units, the lowest since 1991, with a record 28 active projects cancelled. That shows how quickly a pipeline can shrink.

A low payout in a big closing year is not spare capacity. Average profit over three to five years, and compare the dividend with recurring income on its own.

## Mistake 2: treating a developer like a REIT

| Feature | REIT | Developer |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Main income | Rent | Sales and closings |
| Income pattern | Steady | Lumpy |
| Typical payout | Most of AFFO | Small share of average profit |
| Main valuation | AFFO multiple, NAV | NAV, pipeline value |
| Key risk | Occupancy, refinancing | Sales pace, costs, approvals |

Applying REIT payout rules to a developer makes the dividend look either far too safe or far too risky, depending on the year.

Closings over the next two to three years are mostly set by projects already presold and under construction. Closings after that depend on today's presales. A falling presale number is an early warning for future profit and dividend capacity.

| # | Mistake | Better habit |
|--:|:--|:--|
| 1 | Judging payout from a record year | Use a 3–5 year average |
| 2 | Treating a developer like a REIT | Split recurring and development income |
| 3 | Ignoring the presale pipeline | Track presales, deposits and starts |
| 4 | Missing joint venture guarantees | Read the contingencies note |
| 5 | Assuming land always holds value | Check impairments and carrying costs |
| 6 | Treating a land sale as recurring | Exclude one-off gains |
| 7 | Ignoring interest rates | Rates hit both buyers and construction loans |
| 8 | Expecting the dividend to grow every year | Expect specials or buybacks in good years |
| 9 | Ignoring cost overruns | Read project budget updates |
| 10 | Overlooking buyer closing risk | Check deposit levels and closing defaults |

## Mistake 4: joint venture guarantees

Many projects are built in joint ventures. A developer may guarantee construction debt beyond its ownership share. That debt may not appear on the consolidated balance sheet, but it can call on the developer's cash if a partner or project struggles. See [how to read contingent liability disclosures](/investing/analyze/read-contingent-liability-disclosures/).

## Mistake 5: land is not always worth book value

Land costs money to carry (interest, taxes) and earns nothing until it's developed. If demand falls or approvals stall, land values can drop and impairments follow.

| Hypothetical land parcel | Value |
|:--|--:|
| Carrying value | C$100M |
| Annual carrying cost (interest and taxes, 6%) | C$6M |
| Five-year delay | C$30M of carrying costs |

## Mistake 6: counting a land sale as recurring

A large land sale can lift one year's earnings sharply. If the board raises the regular dividend on the back of it, the next year's payout will look stretched. Strip out one-off gains; see [how to spot one-time items](/investing/analyze/spot-one-time-items-canadian-statements/).

## Mistake 7: rates on both sides

Higher interest rates make it harder for buyers to qualify and close, and they raise the cost of construction loans. A developer can see sales slow and costs rise at the same time.

## Mistake 8: buyer closing risk

Presold condo buyers pay deposits years before completion. If values fall below the purchase price, some buyers may struggle to close. Urbanation's January 2026 report flagged rising buyer defaults alongside record project cancellations. Read disclosures on deposit levels and any closing defaults.

## A developer warning-sign list

| Sign | What to check next |
|:--|:--|
| Presales down sharply year over year | Closings two to four years out |
| Dividend above recurring income | Cash and credit for thin years |
| New joint venture guarantees | Size vs equity |
| Land impairments | Which markets and why |
| Rising closing defaults | Deposit coverage |
