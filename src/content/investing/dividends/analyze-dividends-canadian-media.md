"Media" on the TSX covers businesses that have little in common. At one end are information services companies that sell subscriptions to professionals. At the other are broadcasters that depend on TV and radio advertising. In between are publishers, music and audio services, and the large media divisions owned by telecom companies. Their dividends have gone in opposite directions over the past decade, and the reasons are mostly visible in the revenue mix.

Educational only; worked numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named. Media owned by BCE, Rogers or Quebecor is part of a telecom dividend, set by the parent, so it is covered by the [telecom dividend sustainability framework](/investing/dividends/canadian-telecom-dividends-sustainability-framework/) rather than here.

## Direct answer

Analyze a media dividend in five steps: identify **where the revenue comes from** (subscriptions vs advertising); measure how **fixed costs** such as programming and content amortize against that revenue; check **leverage against covenants**, because lenders can restrict dividends; note **dual-class control** and **currency**; and decide whether the company is a **media stock or a telecom stock with a media division**. The contrast is stark: Thomson Reuters raised its 2026 dividend 10% to US$2.62 a share, its 33rd consecutive annual increase, while Corus Entertainment suspended its dividend in October 2023 to redirect cash to debt repayment.

## Step 3: debt, covenants and lender restrictions

Media companies often carry debt from past acquisitions of channels and stations. When profit falls, leverage rises even without new borrowing, and credit agreements can limit dividends.

Corus is the documented case. Its October 2023 results showed pro forma net debt of 3.62 times segment profit. The same month it suspended its dividend and amended its credit facility, which, according to its Annual Information Form, raised the maximum debt-to-cash-flow ratio allowed and introduced additional restrictions on distributions to shareholders. Earlier in 2023 it had already cut its annual dividend to C$0.12 per Class B share.

| Check | Where to find it |
|:--|:--|
| Net debt ÷ segment profit or EBITDA | MD&A, results release |
| Covenant maximum and amendments | AIF, credit agreement on SEDAR+ |
| Restrictions on distributions | Credit agreement and AIF |
| Debt maturities | Debt note |

## Step 4: control, share classes and currency

Many Canadian media companies have dual-class shares, with voting control held by a family or founder. That doesn't make a dividend unsafe, but it means the controlling holder's priorities shape payout decisions. See [how to value dual-class share structures](/investing/valuation/value-dual-class-share-structures/).

Information services companies may also declare in US dollars. Thomson Reuters' dividend is set in US dollars, so a Canadian holder's income moves with the exchange rate.

| Feature | Question to ask |
|:--|:--|
| Dual-class shares | Who decides the dividend, and do they rely on it? |
| Different dividends per class | Which class do you own? (Corus set different rates for Class A and Class B) |
| US-dollar declaration | What is your C$ income at different exchange rates? |

## Step 5: media stock or telecom stock?

If a media business sits inside BCE, Rogers or Quebecor, its results affect the parent's free cash flow, but the dividend is a telecom dividend. Analyze it with telecom tools (wireless and internet cash, spectrum, capex) and treat media as one segment. Don't buy a telecom expecting it to behave like a media pure play, or the reverse.

## A media dividend checklist

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| Share of revenue that is recurring subscription | High, with stable renewals |
| Segment profit sensitivity to a 15% ad drop | Known and modest |
| Leverage vs covenant | Comfortable headroom; no recent amendments |
| Credit agreement limits on distributions | None that bind |
| Dividend history through the last ad recession | Held or grew |
| Share class and currency | Understood |
