Canadian oil and gas producers have become serious dividend payers. Many now pay a fixed base dividend and return extra cash through buybacks or variable dividends when prices are high. That structure is more honest than the old fixed-only model, but it creates new ways to misread the yield.

For how base-plus-variable frameworks work across the cycle, see [how energy producers' dividends move with commodity cycles](/investing/dividends/energy-producer-dividends-commodity-cycles/). This post is the mistakes list. Educational only; numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named.

## Direct answer

The costly mistakes are **annualizing a variable dividend**, judging coverage at **peak prices**, ignoring each producer's **breakeven oil price** for the base dividend, forgetting **sustaining capital and decline rates**, treating **buybacks as income**, ignoring **hedges, differentials and the Canadian dollar**, and owning several producers that all depend on the same commodity. Suncor's 55% dividend cut in May 2020 showed that even a long record can end when prices collapse.

## Mistake 1: annualizing the wrong number

Quote pages calculate yield in different ways. With variable dividends, the differences are large.

## Mistake 2: ignoring the breakeven price

Most producers disclose the oil price at which cash flow covers sustaining capital and the base dividend. That number varies widely across companies and is far more useful than the yield.

## The full mistakes table

| # | Mistake | Why it happens | Better habit |
|--:|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | Annualizing variable dividends | Quote pages do it | Plan on the base dividend only |
| 2 | Ignoring breakeven oil price | Yield is easier to find | Find the company's stated breakeven for base dividend |
| 3 | Coverage at peak prices | Recent results look strong | Re-run coverage at a mid-cycle price deck |
| 4 | Forgetting sustaining capital | "Free cash flow" is headlined | Check sustaining capex and decline rate |
| 5 | Counting buybacks as income | "Total shareholder return" framing | Separate cash you receive from shares retired |
| 6 | Ignoring differentials | WTI is the quoted price | Check WCS and Canadian gas price exposure |
| 7 | Ignoring the Canadian dollar | Oil is quoted in US$ | Understand how a stronger C$ cuts C$ revenue |
| 8 | Ignoring hedging | Hedges seem technical | Read the risk management note for hedged volumes |
| 9 | Ignoring leverage targets | Debt is lower than in 2014 | Check net debt targets that trigger more payouts |
| 10 | Owning several producers as diversification | Different companies, same commodity | Count total oil and gas exposure across holdings |

## Mistake 3: coverage at peak prices

When oil is high, payout ratios look tiny. Re-run the math at a mid-cycle price. In early 2020, oil prices collapsed with the pandemic and an OPEC+ price war. On May 5, 2020, Suncor cut its quarterly dividend to C$0.21 from C$0.465, about 55%, after years of increases. The lesson isn't about one company; it's that a dividend set in good years has to survive bad ones.

## Mistake 4: sustaining capital and decline rates

Oil and gas wells decline. A producer has to spend every year just to hold production flat. Oil sands mines decline slowly; shale and tight wells decline fast. A dividend covered by cash flow before sustaining capital is not covered at all. Find sustaining capex in the MD&A or guidance and subtract it first.

| Asset type | Typical decline profile | Dividend implication |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Oil sands mining / thermal | Low | Steadier free cash flow |
| Conventional oil | Moderate | Moderate reinvestment needs |
| Tight oil and gas wells | High in early years | Heavy spending just to stay flat |

## Mistake 5: buybacks are not income

Many producers return a fixed share of excess free cash flow, split between buybacks and variable dividends. Buybacks can add value, but they don't put cash in your account. If you need income, judge the producer on base plus expected variable dividends, not total shareholder returns.

## Mistakes 6 to 8: the price you actually get

A producer's revenue depends on Western Canadian Select differentials, Canadian gas prices, the exchange rate and hedges, not just WTI. A stronger Canadian dollar lowers C$ revenue for the same US$ oil price. Read the sensitivity table most producers publish (for example, cash flow change per US$1 WTI and per one-cent move in the exchange rate). The [commodity price sensitivity guide](/investing/analyze/read-commodity-price-sensitivity-resource-issuers/) shows how to read it.

## A quick energy dividend check

| Question | Healthy answer |
|:--|:--|
| Base dividend breakeven WTI | Well below a cautious price deck |
| Sustaining capex counted | Yes, before the dividend |
| Net debt | At or below the company's target |
| Variable dividend | Treated as a bonus, not income |
| Total energy weight in portfolio | Known and limited |
