Software dividends are small enough that few investors look at them closely, which is how mistakes happen. People ignore the dividend because the yield is tiny, or buy a mature software company for its yield without noticing that growth is coming from acquisitions and shares are being issued to employees every year.

This list goes with [how to analyze dividends in Canadian software](/investing/dividends/analyze-dividends-canadian-software/), which covers capital allocation, stock-based compensation and recurring revenue. Educational only; numbers are hypothetical unless a source is named.

## Direct answer

The common mistakes are **ignoring stock-based compensation** in the payout ratio, **confusing acquired growth with organic growth**, **dismissing a token dividend** as meaningless, **buying a mature software company for yield** without checking the reinvestment trade-off, missing **leverage after a large deal**, **annualizing a special dividend**, and treating **licence revenue as recurring**.

Reported free cash flow usually adds back stock-based compensation, because it's non-cash. But the shares are real: either the share count rises or the company spends cash on buybacks to offset dilution. Either way, it's a cost to existing shareholders.

Software consolidators report total revenue growth and, usually, organic growth separately. A dividend supported by a business with flat organic revenue depends on the company continuing to buy growth. Read both numbers every quarter.

## Mistake 3: dismissing the token dividend

Constellation Software's dividend has been US$1.00 a quarter for more than a decade, now a tiny yield. Ignoring it misses the information it carries: the board pays a fixed amount and returns excess capital in other ways, such as the US$20 special dividend in 2019. A token dividend can tell you a lot about how management thinks about capital.

| 2 | Treating acquired growth as organic | Track organic growth separately |
| 3 | Dismissing a token dividend | Read what it says about capital allocation |

| 6 | Annualizing a special dividend | Separate regular and special payments |
| 7 | Treating licence revenue as recurring | Use maintenance and subscription revenue |

| 9 | Assuming customer retention is permanent | Track retention and churn disclosures |
| 10 | Counting buybacks that only offset dilution | Check net change in share count |

## Mistake 4: buying mature software for income

A software company paying a meaningful dividend is often one with slower growth and fewer high-return places to reinvest. That can be fine, but the dividend then depends on keeping existing customers. Check maintenance renewal rates, pricing and competition from cloud alternatives.

| Hypothetical mature software company | Value |
|:--|--:|
| Organic revenue growth | 0–2% |
| Maintenance share of revenue | 65% |
| Dividend ÷ FCF after stock comp | 60% |
| Net debt ÷ EBITDA after last deal | 3.0x |

That profile can work, but it leaves little room if renewals weaken while leverage is high.

## Mistake 5: leverage after a large acquisition

Large software deals are often debt-funded. Leverage can stay above target for several years, and dividend growth usually waits. See [how acquisitions can threaten or support dividend capacity](/investing/dividends/acquisitions-threaten-or-support-dividend-capacity/).

## Mistake 6: annualizing a special dividend

A trailing yield calculation that includes a special dividend can make a token payer look like a high yielder for a year. Constellation's 2019 declarations totalled US$24 a share, six times the regular rate. Strip specials out; see [how to treat special dividends in analysis](/investing/dividends/special-dividends-how-to-treat-in-analysis/).

## Mistake 7: licence revenue is not recurring

Licence sales are lumpy. Maintenance and subscription revenue is the part that renews. Build the dividend case on recurring revenue only.

## Mistake 8: forgetting the reporting currency

Several TSX software companies report in US dollars and declare dividends in US dollars. A Canadian investor's income changes with the exchange rate even if the dividend doesn't.

## A software warning-sign list

| Sign | What to check next |
|:--|:--|
| Organic growth near zero or negative | Retention, pricing, competition |
| Share count rising despite buybacks | Stock comp level |
| Leverage above target after a deal | Paydown timetable |
| Trailing yield jumps | Special dividend in the window |
| Maintenance revenue declining | Customer migration to the cloud |
