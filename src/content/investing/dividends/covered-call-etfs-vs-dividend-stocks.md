Covered call ETFs advertise high monthly distributions, often well above the dividend yield of the stocks they hold. For investors who want income, that looks like a shortcut. The extra yield is real cash, but it comes from selling away part of the portfolio's upside. Whether that trade suits you depends on what you want income for and how you feel about missing rallies.

This post compares covered call ETFs with owning dividend stocks directly. It is educational; figures are hypothetical, and it isn't a recommendation of any fund.

## Direct answer

A covered call ETF owns stocks and **sells call options** on some or all of them, collecting **option premiums** that boost distributions. In exchange, it **gives up gains above the strike price** on the covered portion, while **keeping nearly all the downside**. Compared with owning dividend stocks directly, you get **higher current cash flow** but **less long-term growth potential, higher fees, less control and distributions that may include return of capital**. Owning the stocks suits investors focused on total return and dividend growth; covered call ETFs suit those who value steady cash flow now and accept capped upside.

The **coverage ratio** (the share of holdings with calls written against them) is a key setting. Funds that write calls on only part of the portfolio keep more upside; funds that cover more collect more premium. The fund's website or factsheet usually states its approach.

The premium helps in flat and gently falling months. It barely dents a sharp fall, and it caps the big up months that drive much of long-run stock returns. Over a full cycle, a covered strategy usually trails the underlying stocks in rising markets and recovers more slowly after a sell-off.

## Side by side

| | Covered call ETF | Owning dividend stocks directly |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Current cash flow | Higher (dividends + option premium) | Dividend yield only |
| Upside | Capped on covered portion | Full |
| Downside | Nearly full, cushioned by premium | Full |
| Income growth | Depends on markets and option pricing; can fall | Grows if companies raise dividends |
| Fees | MER typically above plain index ETFs | Trading costs only |
| Control | Fund decides holdings and option strategy | You choose each stock |
| Tax character of payouts | Mix of dividends, capital gains, ROC; varies yearly | Mostly eligible dividends (Canadian stocks) |
| Effort | Low | Higher: research and monitoring |

## Distributions aren't the same as dividends

A covered call ETF's distribution can include **return of capital**, especially when it pays a fixed monthly amount that exceeds what the fund earns. Some of that is a tax classification; some can be the fund paying out more than it earns, which shows up as a falling net asset value per unit. Check the fund's annual tax breakdown and NAV history. [Return of capital distributions](/investing/dividends/return-of-capital-distributions-what-to-track/) explains how to tell the difference.

## Fees and drag

| Hypothetical on C$100,000 | Annual cost |
|:--|--:|
| MER 0.20% (plain equity ETF) | $200 |
| MER 0.70% (covered call ETF) | $700 |
| Owning 15 stocks, commission-free broker | $0 to small trading costs |

Fees come out of returns every year. See [MER, TER and tracking difference](/investing/indexes-etfs/mer-ter-tracking-difference/) for how to read them.

## Who might choose which

| Situation | Leans toward |
|:--|:--|
| Long horizon, reinvesting income, wants growth | Owning dividend growers or broad ETFs |
| Spending income now, values steady monthly cash | Covered call ETF may fit, accepting capped upside |
| Wants control and to learn stock analysis | Owning stocks directly |
| Wants a hands-off income sleeve alongside stock picks | A small covered call allocation |

Some investors split it: a core of dividend stocks they research themselves and a smaller covered call sleeve for cash flow. See [Canadian equity ETFs vs individual stocks](/investing/indexes-etfs/canadian-equity-etfs-vs-individual-stocks/).

## Common mistakes

- Comparing a covered call ETF's distribution yield with a stock's dividend yield as if they're the same thing.
- Ignoring NAV erosion behind a steady distribution.
- Expecting covered calls to protect against big declines.
- Overlooking that higher payouts in a taxable account may come with a different tax mix.
