A company's results can depend on businesses it does not control: a single supplier of a key component, a port that handles most of its imports, a country that supplies a raw material, or a supplier finance program that quietly funds its working capital. These **supply chain dependencies** rarely appear in headline numbers, but filings often describe them if you know where to look.

This post shows where supply chain risk shows up in Canadian filings and how to turn scattered disclosures into a simple map. It pairs with [how to analyze customer concentration risk](/investing/analyze/analyze-customer-concentration-risk/), which covers the other end of the chain. It is educational and does not rate any company; figures are hypothetical.

## Direct answer

Build a supply chain map from five sources: (1) the **AIF description of the business** (raw materials, suppliers, economic dependence, foreign operations); (2) **risk factors** naming single-source or sole-supplier inputs; (3) the **MD&A** discussion of input costs, logistics and tariffs; (4) **financial statement notes** on inventory, purchase commitments and supplier finance arrangements; and (5) **segment and geographic** disclosures. Mark each link as diversified, dual-sourced or single-source, and note what would happen if it broke.

## Where supply chain risk appears

| Filing | What to look for | Example wording |
|:--|:--|:--|
| AIF: description of the business | Raw materials, key suppliers, economic dependence | "We rely on a limited number of suppliers for..." |
| AIF: risk factors | Single-source inputs, geopolitical and trade risk | "sole supplier", "single source", "tariffs" |
| MD&A | Input cost trends, freight, inventory build, shortages | "supply constraints", "expedited freight" |
| Inventory note | Write-downs, inventory levels vs sales | See [inventory turns and receivables quality](/investing/analyze/analyze-inventory-turns-receivables-quality/) |
| Commitments note | Long-term purchase obligations | Take-or-pay supply agreements |
| Supplier finance note | Reverse factoring or supplier finance programs | Amounts payable under the arrangement |
| Segment and geographic notes | Where assets and production sit | See [geographic revenue mix](/investing/analyze/analyze-geographic-revenue-mix-canadian-multinationals/) |

Since 2024, IFRS has required companies to disclose **supplier finance arrangements** (amendments to IAS 7 and IFRS 7), including the terms and the amounts suppliers have already been paid by finance providers. These programs can make operating cash flow look stronger than the underlying business; see [how to read cash flow from operations vs net income](/investing/analyze/read-cfo-vs-net-income/).

## Dependency scoring table

| Link | Sourcing type | Substitute available? | Time to switch | Filing reference |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Key component | Single-source | No | 12+ months | Risk factors |
| Main raw material | Diversified | Yes | Weeks | AIF |
| Primary port or rail route | Dual | Partly | Months | MD&A |
| Contract manufacturer | Single | Yes, at cost | 6 to 12 months | AIF |

Fill in a table like this (hypothetical example above) for each company you own. The aim is not a precise score but a short list of links where one disruption could move results.

## Checklist

| Step | Done when |
|:--|:--|
| Map sketched | Inputs, suppliers, logistics, company, customers |
| Single-source links named | From risk factors and AIF |
| Commitments noted | Purchase obligations and supplier finance amounts |
| Inventory checked | Turns and write-downs over several years |
| Geographic exposure noted | Countries of key suppliers and production |
| Follow-up questions written | For the next earnings call or investor day |
