Many large Canadian companies trade on both the TSX and a US exchange such as the NYSE or Nasdaq. It's one company with one set of financial statements, listed twice. That raises practical questions for DIY investors. Which filings are the primary source, which currency are the results reported in, and does it matter which line you buy?

Educational only, not financial advice. This post focuses on dual-listed *Canadian* issuers. For owning US-domiciled companies, see [SEDAR+ vs EDGAR](/investing/analyze/sedar-plus-vs-edgar-us-stocks/). For valuation comparisons, see [comparing a TSX stock to its US peer](/investing/valuation/compare-tsx-stock-to-us-peer/).

## Direct answer

Research the company once, starting with its SEDAR+ filings (AIF, MD&A, financial statements). Then check EDGAR for the US version (often Form 40-F for eligible Canadian issuers, plus 6-K furnishings) so you know nothing is disclosed only there. Note the **reporting currency**. Many dual-listed Canadian companies report in US dollars. Pick the listing that fits the currency you already hold and your account type, and count FX conversion costs, which often outweigh commissions. Cross-border arbitrage generally keeps the two prices close after adjusting for the exchange rate, so the quote line itself rarely creates a bargain.

## What's the same and what differs

| Item | TSX line | US line |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Underlying company and shares | Same | Same (often interlisted common shares) |
| Quote currency | CAD | USD |
| Core filings | SEDAR+ | EDGAR (40-F/6-K, or 10-K/10-Q if the issuer files as a domestic filer) |
| Financial statements | Usually identical package | Usually identical package |
| Trading hours | TSX session | US session (largely overlapping) |
| Liquidity | Varies by name | Varies; sometimes deeper on the US line |
| Dividend currency | Per declaration; check the news release | Per declaration; broker may convert |

## Reporting currency checklist

| Question | Where to look | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|:--|
| What is the presentation currency? | Financial statements, note 1–2 | Per-share figures may be in USD while you track a CAD price |
| What is the functional currency? | Accounting policies note (IAS 21) | Drives FX gains/losses through the income statement |
| Is the dividend declared in CAD or USD? | Dividend news release | Your received amount floats with FX |
| Does guidance use a stated FX assumption? | MD&A outlook | A CAD move can change results with no change in operations |

If the company reports in USD and you value it in CAD, convert per-share figures with the same rate on both sides. More on this in [handling currency in CAD vs USD results](/investing/analyze/handle-currency-cad-vs-usd-results/).

## Account and cost considerations

- **FX friction:** Buying the US line with Canadian dollars triggers a conversion. Some brokers offer USD sub-accounts. See [commission-free trading: what you still pay in FX and spreads](/investing/brokers-tools/commission-free-trading-fx-spreads/).
- **Dividends:** A Canadian company's dividend is Canadian-source, whichever line you buy. Confirm how your broker pays it (CAD or USD). This differs from [US withholding on US-domiciled dividend payers](/investing/dividends/us-dividend-withholding-tax-tfsa-rrsp-taxable/).
- **ACB tracking:** If you buy one line and sell the other, or journal between them, record each transaction in CAD at that day's rate. See [ACB tracking for DIY portfolios](/investing/accounts/adjusted-cost-base-tracking-diy-portfolios/).
- **Liquidity:** Check the average volume and bid-ask spread on each line before placing a larger order.
