A **rights offering** lets every existing shareholder buy new shares, usually at a discount, in proportion to what they already own. On paper that is the fairest way to raise equity, because nobody gets diluted unless they choose to be. In practice many holders get diluted by accident: they miss the deadline, their broker never asks them, or they live in a jurisdiction the offer excludes.

This guide covers the mechanics and math. It is not a view on any offering. Every number is hypothetical. For the bigger dilution picture, see [dilution risk in Canadian issuers](/investing/analyze/spot-dilution-risk-canadian-issuers/), [What is dilution?](/investing/glossary/what-is-dilution/) and [cap tables](/investing/analyze/read-cap-tables-fully-diluted-share-counts/).

## Direct answer

Read the **rights offering circular** (or prospectus) for five numbers: the **ratio** (rights per share and rights per new share), the **subscription price**, the **expiry date**, any **standby commitment**, and the **use of proceeds**. Work out the theoretical ex-rights price and the value of each right. Then pick one of three actions before your broker's deadline, which is often a few days before the official expiry: take up, sell the rights, or (the costly default) do nothing.

## The math (hypothetical)

Theoretical ex-rights price (TERP) = (old shares × market price + new shares × subscription price) ÷ total shares.

| Item | Value |
|:--|--:|
| Shares outstanding | 100M |
| Terms | 1 new share for every 2 held |
| New shares | 50M |
| Market price before ex-date | $10.00 |
| Subscription price | $7.00 |
| TERP = (100 × 10 + 50 × 7) ÷ 150 | $9.00 |
| Value per right (1 right per share; 2 rights per new share) | ($9.00 − $7.00) ÷ 2 = $1.00 |

A holder of 1,000 shares gets 1,000 rights. Taking them all up costs 500 × $7.00 = $3,500 and leaves the holder with 1,500 shares worth about $13,500 at TERP. Before, the position was worth $10,000 plus $3,500 in cash, so the holder is no worse off. Selling the rights at about $1.00 each brings in $1,000, which also roughly keeps total value but cuts ownership. Doing nothing turns $1,000 of value into zero.

## What else to read in the circular

| Term | What it means | Why it matters |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Basic subscription privilege | Your pro rata right to buy | Keeps your stake |
| Additional subscription privilege | Request extra shares that others do not take up | Lets you increase your stake at the same price |
| Standby commitment | A party agrees to buy any shares not taken up | Guarantees the raise; check what the standby holder is paid and whether it gains control |
| Ineligible jurisdictions | Holders outside listed provinces or countries cannot exercise | Their rights may be sold for them by an agent |
| Rights trading | Rights often list on the TSX or TSXV until shortly before expiry | Thin trading can make selling hard |
| Use of proceeds | What the money is for | Same test as any [prospectus offering](/investing/analyze/read-prospectus-secondary-offerings/) |

Under the CSA's 2015 amendments to the rights offering exemption in NI 45-106, reporting issuers use a short notice and a rights offering circular, and that exemption is limited to offerings that would increase outstanding shares by no more than 100% over 12 months. Larger raises need a prospectus.

## Checklist

| Step | Done when |
|:--|:--|
| Ratio and price logged | Rights per share, rights per new share, price |
| TERP and right value computed | Hypothetical math matches the circular's terms |
| Broker deadline confirmed | Earlier than the official expiry |
| Standby terms read | Fees and potential control change noted |
| Decision recorded | Take up, sell or deliberate no action, with reasons |
| Cap table updated | New share count added to your notes |
