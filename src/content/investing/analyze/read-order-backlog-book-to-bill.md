Order **backlog** and **book-to-bill** show up in industrials, aerospace, tech hardware, and some services. When disclosed well, they are leading indicators. When disclosed badly, they are theatre. This page is how to read them without inventing precision the filing does not support.

Educational only — not financial advice. Charts use **hypothetical** figures. Pair with [recurring vs cyclical](/investing/analyze/identify-recurring-vs-cyclical-revenue/), [segment reporting](/investing/analyze/read-segment-reporting-canadian-filings/), and [management guidance language](/investing/analyze/evaluate-management-guidance-language/).

## Direct answer

First confirm the issuer **defines** backlog and bookings (firm orders? options? framework agreements?). Align the booking window with the revenue window before computing book-to-bill (bookings ÷ revenue). Trend the ratio and backlog dollars over several quarters. Ask about cancellations, deferrals, price escalation, and margin mix on new orders — backlog is not cash.

## Definitions you must pin down

| Term | Good disclosure answers | Red flag |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Backlog | Firm orders under contract; timing bands | "Pipeline" mixed into backlog |
| Bookings / orders | Signed in the period; FX policy stated | Awards announced but not contracted |
| Book-to-bill | Same-period bookings ÷ revenue; method stable | Ratio without definitions |
| Remaining performance obligations (RPO) | IFRS 15 RPO vs "backlog" reconciled | Two numbers, no bridge |

Canadian reporters may discuss backlog in MD&A even when IFRS 15 RPO is the formal note metric — know which table you are reading.
