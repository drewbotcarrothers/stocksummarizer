"Which is better, A or B?" is one of the most common questions DIY investors ask about TSX stocks — RY or TD, CNR or CPKC, ENB or TRP. The honest answer starts with making the comparison **fair**. Most bad pair comparisons fail before any valuation happens.

Educational only — not financial advice. The worked example uses **hypothetical** companies.

## Direct answer

Pick peers that share economics (not just a sector label), align fiscal periods and currencies, use the same data source and date, normalize one-time items, then compare four buckets: **growth, quality, balance sheet, valuation**. Write a conclusion that says *what you'd have to believe* to prefer each one.

### Step 1 — Are they really peers?

| Test | Pass example | Fail example |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Same revenue drivers | Two Canadian Class I railroads | A railroad vs a trucking broker |
| Similar customers/regulation | Two Big Six banks | A Big Six bank vs a mono-line lender |
| Similar cyclicality | Two senior gold producers | A gold royalty company vs a gold developer |
| Similar reporting basis | Both IFRS, CAD | IFRS/CAD vs US GAAP/USD without adjustment |

Our [sector deep dives](/sectors/) group TSX names into peer sets you can start from — for example [transportation](/sectors/transportation/) or [banks](/sectors/banks/).

### Step 2 — Align periods, currency and accounting

- Use **trailing twelve months (TTM)** for both, or the same fiscal year; Canadian banks' fiscal year ends October 31.
- Convert to one currency at a stated rate if one reports in USD.
- Check IFRS 16 lease treatment and whether either uses US GAAP.

### Step 3 — Normalize

Remove clearly one-time gains/losses (asset sales, legal settlements) from both, use **diluted** share counts, and use the same definition of "adjusted" for both — or none. See [reading non-GAAP metrics critically](/investing/analyze/read-non-gaap-metrics-critically/).

## Step 4 — Score the four buckets (illustrative example)

Two **hypothetical** TSX industrial companies, same data date, TTM:

| Metric | Company A (hypothetical) | Company B (hypothetical) | Edge |
|:--|--:|--:|:--:|
| *Growth* | | | |
| Revenue growth (organic) | 6% | 9% | B |
| EPS growth, 3-yr avg | 7% | 11% | B |
| *Quality* | | | |
| Operating margin | 24% | 18% | A |
| FCF ÷ net income | 1.05 | 0.70 | A |
| Return on invested capital | 12% | 9% | A |
| *Balance sheet* | | | |
| Net debt ÷ EBITDA | 1.8× | 3.4× | A |
| *Valuation* | | | |
| P/E (TTM) | 18× | 16× | B |
| EV/EBITDA | 11× | 10× | B |
| P/FCF | 17× | 23× | A |
| Dividend yield | 2.5% | 3.0% | B |

*Illustrative numbers only — not real companies. Note how B looks cheaper on P/E and EV/EBITDA but more expensive on P/FCF because less of its earnings become cash.*

### Reading the table

- B is growing faster and looks cheaper on earnings multiples.
- A converts more earnings to cash, earns higher returns and carries less debt.
- The P/FCF flip is the tell: B's "cheapness" may reflect lower earnings quality and higher leverage. That's a question for its [MD&A liquidity section](/investing/analyze/how-to-read-mdna/), not a verdict.

## Step 5 — Write the conclusion as beliefs

| To prefer A, you must believe… | To prefer B, you must believe… |
|:--|:--|
| Quality and balance-sheet strength deserve a premium | B's growth persists long enough to grow into its debt |
| B's cash conversion won't improve | B's cash conversion improves as capex normalizes |
| Downside protection matters more right now | The market is over-penalizing leverage |

That is a far more useful output than "B is cheaper." Drop it into your [bull / bear / watch brief](/investing/analyze/stock-brief-template-bull-bear-watch/).

## Common mistakes

1. Comparing a bank's P/E with a pipeline's P/E (different business models).
2. Mixing one company's adjusted EPS with the other's IFRS EPS.
3. Using different data dates or sources for each company.
4. Ignoring share-count dilution.
5. Comparing a TSX name to a US peer without currency and accounting adjustments.
